Taiwan, China and South Korea roll out market support over the weekend as Asia braces for the AI sell-off, while US stock futures open Monday with Nasdaq 100 futures up about 2%, Reuters reports

After a week in which the Nasdaq fell 10%, its steepest weekly drop since March 2020, governments in Asia spent the weekend trying to steady their markets before Monday trading. According to Reuters, Taiwan's financial regulator on Saturday ordered a further tightening of short-selling rules, Chinese stock exchanges announced support steps such as easier margin financing requirements, and South Korea's finance ministry said on Sunday it stands ready to deploy market stabilization funds immediately if prices keep falling. Taiwanese shares lost 17% last week, their largest weekly fall since 1990, and the Korean benchmark sank to 10-month lows.

When US stock futures opened for the new week, they recovered some ground: Reuters says Nasdaq 100 futures rose about 2% and S&P 500 futures around 1%. Asia was still expected to open weaker. Japanese markets were shut on Monday for a holiday, but futures pointed to selling, Hang Seng futures signaled a fall and Australian shares were 0.5% lower. Reuters links last week's slide to the exit of Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, among other factors; the Philadelphia semiconductor index dropped 10% on Friday alone.

Why it mattersWhen three of Asia's most chip-heavy markets change trading rules and line up rescue money over a single weekend, the AI sell-off has become a financial stability issue, not just a valuation debate. Short-selling curbs and looser margin rules can slow forced selling, but they also make price signals less reliable for a while. The bounce in US futures shows buyers are still there, yet it does not tell anyone where the bottom is for AI hardware and the suppliers that depend on it.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says Washington still has no answers from Nvidia on whether its AI chips reached China despite export curbs, and that the board is moving fast to find out, CBS News reports

Speaking on CBS News' Face the Nation on Sunday, 22 November, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the administration still needs Nvidia to explain whether it complied with US export restrictions on advanced AI chips. Last week he said on Fox News that he wanted those answers within 10 days; on Sunday he said they had not yet come in. He told CBS that officials have been talking to Nvidia's interim management, its lead directors and its lawyers. The US is investigating how China may have gained access to the company's most advanced AI chips, and Nvidia has been in crisis since chief executive Jensen Huang resigned after revelations about alleged affairs, including one with a woman CBS describes as a suspected Chinese spy. Chief financial officer Colette Kress has taken charge since his departure.

Bessent said a culture of noncompliance at Nvidia is only a possibility for now, and said the board is working quickly to establish whether one existed. He rejected the idea that the company's informal, handshake-style way of doing business would leave no record: "For deals that size, there's no such thing as an untraceable handshake deal." In his view, payments and paperwork can be used to rebuild what happened even without long formal contracts. He separated that compliance question from Huang's alleged relationship, which he played down. President Trump has called for Huang to return as chief executive; Bessent, who has urged Nvidia to bring in an outsider, said it is still unclear how far up the business knowledge of the chip flows went. CBS adds that Huang has agreed to testify before the Senate Banking Committee and will be interviewed by House panels in early December.

Why it mattersThe question the administration is pressing is not about one executive's private life but about whether a company at the center of the AI supply chain had the controls to stop restricted chips from reaching China. Bessent's point that large deals leave money trails signals that investigators will follow payments and intermediaries, not just contracts. Whatever the board finds is likely to shape how tightly Washington polices AI chip sales through distributors and cloud resellers, and that affects every buyer of high-end AI hardware.

South Korea's KOSPI opens 2.77% lower on Monday despite the weekend support steps, with Samsung Electronics and SK hynix both in the red, Yonhap reports

Seoul's main index fell again at Monday's open as worries about AI spread from Wall Street. Yonhap, as published by The Korea Times, reports that the KOSPI opened 2.77% lower and by 9:15 a.m. was down 129.7 points, or 2.66%, at 4,740.26. That follows a 5.21% plunge on Friday that left the index at 4,869.96, under the 5,000 mark and at its lowest close in about 10 months.

Chip and export heavyweights led the early losses: Samsung Electronics was down 3.39%, SK hynix 2.34%, Hyundai Motor 3.73% and Samsung Biologics 4.21%. Yonhap says sentiment was hurt by concern over tighter US semiconductor export restrictions and by reports that AMD, like Nvidia, could face risks tied to its China business. The won was slightly firmer, at 1,338.8 per US dollar at 9:15 a.m., 7.2 won stronger than the previous close.

Why it mattersSeoul's opening trade is one of the first reads each week on how investors see AI hardware, because chipmakers such as Samsung Electronics and SK hynix weigh heavily in it. A smaller early fall than Friday's may reflect the government's weekend pledge, but continued selling in Samsung Electronics and SK hynix shows that worries about US export rules are now being priced into the suppliers, not only into Nvidia.

Selling spreads across Asia on Monday, with Seoul's KOSPI down as much as 4.5% in morning trade and Hong Kong's Hang Seng off 1.8%, Al Jazeera reports

Friday's tech-led sell-off carried into Monday trading across much of Asia. According to Al Jazeera, the main indexes in South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and mainland China all fell. The KOSPI slid as much as 4.5% during the morning, a deeper loss than at the open, while the Hang Seng Index dipped 1.8% and Shanghai's CSI 300 lost more than 1.3%. Taiwan's Taiex dropped more than 1% at the open and then moved back and forth between losses and gains. Japanese markets were closed for Labour Thanksgiving Day.

The slide follows a Friday on which, Al Jazeera notes, the S&P 500 closed 3.7% lower for its seventh loss in a row and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.3%, erasing everything it had gained in 2026. The KOSPI, Nikkei 225 and Taiex went into the weekend between 5.1% and 6.2% lower. US futures pointed to a rebound on Monday, with S&P 500 futures up 2% and Nasdaq futures up 2.4% at the time of the report. Al Jazeera also cites research firm Gartner's estimate that global AI-related investment, much of it financed with debt, will reach $2.7 trillion in 2026 and $3.6 trillion in 2027, and says the Democratic Party's midterm victory is expected to slow data center construction in US states.

Why it mattersThe selling is no longer limited to the chip-heavy markets of Seoul and Taipei: Hong Kong and mainland China are falling too, despite the support steps announced over the weekend. The Gartner figures show how much spending sits behind the AI trade. If a large share of that investment relies on borrowed money, a sustained fall in share prices can make funding dearer for data center builders and their suppliers, which eventually reaches the companies buying AI services.

India's Sensex and Nifty open higher for a third straight session on Monday, led by IT stocks, even as the AI sell-off weighs on the rest of Asia, Outlook Money reports

Indian shares bucked the regional trend at Monday's open. Outlook Money reports that the Sensex started the day 502.26 points, or 0.60%, higher at 84,421.97, and the Nifty 50 rose 128.55 points, or 0.50%, to 25,929.15, extending gains into a third session. Information technology shares did best, with the Nifty IT index up 1.22% at the open, and financial services and public sector bank indexes gained between 0.50% and 1%. Every sector index started in positive territory apart from metals, which fell 1.06%. Midcap and smallcap benchmarks also edged up.

The contrast with neighboring markets was sharp. The same report says South Korea's main index fell close to 5%, China's CSI 300 and Hong Kong's Hang Seng were each down more than 0.50%, and Japan was shut for a holiday. Outlook Money ties Friday's US losses, including a 5.28% drop in the Nasdaq Composite and a fall of more than 14% in Nvidia, to worries about further Federal Reserve rate rises and closer regulatory scrutiny under a Democrat-led Congress. US futures recovered on Monday, with Nasdaq 100 futures up about 2.8%. The outlet frames the AI sell-off as steering investor interest toward India, though the report gives no fund-flow data to show that money is actually moving.

Why it mattersIndia's market has little exposure to the AI chipmakers and data center builders now under pressure, so it can look like a refuge when those stocks fall. The strong start for Indian IT is worth watching: if investors see the services companies as winners from cheaper AI tools rather than losers, that changes how boards and buyers should think about outsourcing contracts. One open session is not a trend, though, and without flow data the "safe haven" story remains an interpretation, not a measured shift.

Editor's note
The AI sell-off has moved from trading desks to finance ministries: governments are now managing the fallout.

Over the weekend, Taiwan tightened short-selling rules, Chinese exchanges eased margin financing and South Korea said rescue funds are ready, all after a week in which the Nasdaq lost 10% and Taiwanese shares 17%. Monday's first trades show the limits of that support: US futures bounced, but Seoul still opened 2.77% lower, with Samsung Electronics and SK hynix down, and by mid-morning, Al Jazeera reports, the KOSPI was down as much as 4.5%, with Hong Kong and Shanghai also lower. India was the exception, with the Sensex and Nifty opening higher for a third session, led by IT shares. Behind the market story sits the policy one. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says Washington still lacks answers from Nvidia on whether its chips reached China, and concern over US export rules is now weighing on chip suppliers, not just Nvidia. For executives, the message is to treat recent price swings as a planning base case: stress-test AI and chip exposure, confirm supplier funding and allocation for 2027, and make sure the paper trail behind large hardware purchases would stand up to scrutiny.
