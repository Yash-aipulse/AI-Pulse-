Anthropic ends its first day of trading at $97.17, 17% above its $83 IPO price, after giving back part of an early surge as the Nasdaq falls 2.25%

Anthropic's shares finished their first session on the Nasdaq at $97.17 on Thursday, a gain of 17% on the $83 price set for the offering, according to a Stocktwits report republished by Yahoo Finance. The stock had opened 34% above the offer price, a level the report says implied a market value near $2 trillion, and touched an intraday high near $115 before it eased back. At the IPO price, the company carried a fully diluted valuation of roughly $1.5 trillion. Anthropic sold 980 million Class A shares to raise $81.3 billion, and a $26 billion private placement running alongside the listing, with Nvidia, Microsoft, Google and others taking part, lifted total proceeds to about $107 billion.

The debut came on a weak day for the rest of technology. The report says the Nasdaq Composite fell 2.25%, with Apple, Microsoft, Nvidia and Google weighing on the index. Economist Mohamed El-Erian argued in a post on X that the sell-off was "about making room for it": investors sold other holdings to pay for their Anthropic allocations, much as a very large new borrower in the bond market raises funding costs for everyone else. By our calculation, the closing price leaves first-day buyers who got shares at the offer price $14.17 a share ahead.

Why it mattersA 17% first-day gain is solid but well below the opening pop, which suggests investors are willing to fund a frontier AI lab at public-market scale while still haggling over the price. If El-Erian is right that money for Anthropic came out of other large technology stocks, very large AI listings can weigh on the shares of the same companies that supply and partner with them, which matters for anyone holding or benchmarking against AI-heavy portfolios.

Asian shares head for their worst week since 2020 as the AI sell-off spreads, with Japan's Nikkei down 4.1% and South Korea's KOSPI down 4.7% on Friday, Reuters reports

Stock markets across Asia fell sharply on Friday, putting the region on course for its weakest week since 2020, according to Reuters. MSCI's broadest Asia-Pacific index dropped 2.5% and was set for a six-day losing run, leaving it 7% lower for the week, its poorest showing since March 2020. Japan's Nikkei lost 4.1% and was heading for a weekly fall of 6.8%, while South Korea's KOSPI slid 4.7% and was on track to end the week 7.5% down. The moves followed declines of more than 2% in both the S&P 500 and the Nasdaq Composite in the previous US session, and US stock futures were lower again in Asian hours, with Nasdaq futures down 0.7%.

Reuters says Anthropic's surge of more than 30% on its market debut did nothing to lift the wider market. Kyle Rodda, a senior financial market analyst at Capital.com, said the listing "proved not enough to assuage the overall concern" about the outlook for AI stocks, adding that a fading AI trade and the likelihood of Federal Reserve rate increases leave sentiment fragile. Reuters links the sell-off to several pressures: Jensen Huang's departure from Nvidia, growing opposition to data centers after the Democrats' sweep of the US midterm elections, worries about debt loads, and warnings that AI could threaten human survival. Fed funds futures show a quarter-point rate increase next month as all but certain, according to CME's FedWatch tool, and the 10-year US Treasury yield stood at 5.026%.

Why it mattersThe AI trade is no longer a US-only story: Japan and South Korea carry heavy weightings in chips, memory and AI-linked conglomerates, so a week like this hits supplier valuations, pension portfolios and the cost of capital for AI projects across the region. With rates also expected to rise, the sell-off makes financing for large AI and data center investments harder to price and more expensive to secure.

Trump says he and Xi Jinping both want AI rules for safety and will work together on them, as China's embassy calls for keeping AI under human control, Reuters reports

After meeting Chinese President Xi Jinping at Kylin Villa in Shenzhen on Thursday, US President Donald Trump told Fox News that both leaders want artificial intelligence to be regulated for safety and that they will cooperate on "reasonable AI rules," Reuters reports. In a statement the same day, China's embassy in Washington said the two countries should step up communication and coordination, guard against the security risks of AI and make sure it always stays under human control.

Trump also said he did not discuss Nvidia, or former Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang's relationship with a woman accused of spying for Beijing, in any depth with Xi during the visit. The New York Times reported that relationship in October; Reuters says it has not independently confirmed that the woman is a spy. Reuters notes that Washington and Beijing are both racing to build better AI models, which each sees as central to military and technological leadership, and that the US has spent years trying to keep Nvidia's most advanced chips away from China's military.

Why it mattersA stated willingness by the two largest AI powers to work on shared safety rules is a shift in tone, even though neither side has published any detail on what those rules would cover or when. For companies operating in both markets, it raises the prospect of some common ground on AI safety while chip export controls and military competition stay firmly in place.

A House committee says the Commerce Department approved licenses for 879,000 Nvidia and AMD AI chips to Chinese companies in six months, including 150,000 for Tencent, Reuters reports

The Trump administration signed off on sales of 879,000 advanced Nvidia and AMD AI chips to Chinese companies between December 2025 and May 2026, the House Foreign Affairs Committee said on Thursday, according to Reuters. The committee's figures show Commerce Department approvals of 150,000 chips for Tencent, 75,000 for Alibaba, 43,000 for Baidu and 4,000 for BYD, and it noted that all four appear on the Pentagon's list of alleged Chinese military-backed companies. Over the same period, companies applied to send more than 3.4 million chips to China. The chips in question are Nvidia's H200 and AMD's comparable MI325X, and Reuters says it is not yet clear how many of the approvals turned into actual deliveries.

Committee chair Brian Mast, a Florida Republican, released the license details using his powers under the Export Control Reform Act. Chris McGuire, a former US official, told Reuters that more than 30% of the AI chips sent to China went to companies the US has designated as Chinese military companies, which he said "calls into question whether Commerce conducted any serious national security review." Nvidia said the licenses came with strict conditions and limited volumes, and that it has since stopped shipping H200s to China. AMD said it complies with US export control laws and keeps rigorous compliance processes. Commerce and the Chinese companies named did not immediately comment. Reuters notes that a Commerce official testified in July that shipments had begun but that there had been very few.

Why it mattersThis is the most detailed public view yet, company by company, of how many advanced US AI chips Washington was prepared to let China's largest technology groups buy, and it lands in the same week as summit talk of US–China cooperation on AI rules. Expect pressure from Congress for tighter licensing, more disclosure and possibly direct oversight of chip exports, which would affect chipmakers' China revenue, the supply plans of Chinese AI developers and the compliance burden on anyone in the AI hardware chain.

California's attorney general subpoenas Nvidia to find out whether its advanced chips or confidential information reached China and whether investors were misled, the BBC reports

California has served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena, according to the BBC. Attorney General Rob Bonta says his office is examining whether actions by Nvidia or its former chief executive, Jensen Huang, led to China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information, and says such conduct could break California law if the company misled investors and the public. "Companies cannot mislead their investors," Bonta said. The inquiry follows the scandal that ended with Huang's departure, which centered on his affair with Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a major Nvidia customer, who is alleged to be a Chinese spy.

The BBC notes that some members of Congress have asked the Commerce Department to look into whether Megaspeed diverted Nvidia chips to China; Megaspeed has said such reports contain claims not backed by verifiable evidence. Nvidia's shares have fallen 22% since The New York Times reported the affair in early November. Board chair Harvey Jones has said Nvidia has no intention of bringing Huang back, after Huang said he would be honored to return. The BBC said it had asked Nvidia for comment. The Hill adds that, according to The Wall Street Journal, the SEC is reportedly running a similar probe that includes whether Nvidia misled investors.

Why it mattersA state securities inquiry adds a new front to the scrutiny of Nvidia's China exposure, alongside congressional pressure and a reported federal probe. Because the question is whether investors were misled, the case could shape how all AI chip companies describe China-related risks in filings, and how much evidence buyers and investors expect about where advanced chips end up.

AMD shares fall 8% by midday after short seller Hunterbrook says newer AMD firmware turned up at IEIT Systems, a blacklisted Chinese server maker, and AMD says the claims misrepresent its practices

Shares of Advanced Micro Devices were down 8% at $489.49 in midday trading on Thursday, according to TheFly, after Hunterbrook Capital disclosed a short position in the chipmaker and its sister newsroom, Hunterbrook Media, published a report on AMD's ties to China. As TipRanks summarizes the report, IEIT Systems, a Chinese server maker the US blacklisted in March 2025, has since posted at least six server updates containing newer AMD firmware, the latest on 19 October. Hunterbrook also found nearly identical firmware offered by three other server brands, Aivres, Kaytus and Maginfra. Corporate filings link Aivres and Kaytus to IEIT, and AMD's website lists Aivres as a partner for servers built on its MI300X and MI325X AI accelerators. The report does not show how IEIT obtained the firmware or whether a US export license covered it.

AMD told Bloomberg that the claims misrepresent its business practices and relationships, and that "AMD complies with applicable US export control laws and regulations," TheFly reports. TipRanks says AMD had earlier asked Hunterbrook for more time to answer its questions. The report also highlights how much of AMD's business sits in or near China: China and Hong Kong made up $7.75 billion, or 22.4%, of its 2025 sales, and it points to more than $33 billion of AMD lease and loan guarantees plus pledges of up to 320 million shares tied to AI customers. The timing matters. A Commerce Department rule taking effect this week requires licenses to ship advanced AI chips to non-US buyers in Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, and a pause on a separate rule covering firms at least 50% owned by blacklisted groups ends on 10 January 2027. US limits on AMD's MI308 chip led to roughly $800 million in inventory charges in April 2025.

Why it mattersAMD is the main alternative to Nvidia for AI accelerators, and many buyers have added it to spread supplier risk. If export-control questions now reach AMD's channel as well, that diversification carries its own compliance exposure. The allegations are unproven and come from a firm that profits if the stock falls, but with new Commerce licensing rules arriving and a wider affiliates rule due back in January, buyers, resellers and investors will be asked to show where AI hardware and firmware end up.
