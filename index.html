Nvidia's board is reportedly preparing to remove CEO Jensen Huang and the stock falls about 8% after hours, TechCrunch reports, citing The Wall Street Journal

Nvidia's board is getting ready to push out co-founder and chief executive Jensen Huang, according to a Wall Street Journal report summarized by TechCrunch on 11 November 2026. People familiar with the matter told the Journal that directors could meet as soon as that Wednesday to settle his future. Huang has led the company for 33 years. TechCrunch says Nvidia shares dropped about 8% in after-hours trading after the report and are down about 11% over the past five days.

The report comes three days after Huang admitted, on Sunday, to an extramarital affair with a woman US intelligence reportedly believes is a Chinese spy. On Wednesday evening, The New York Times reported a second affair, with an executive who reports to him. TechCrunch points out that Huang has about 60 direct reports, has said he delegates very little, and that Nvidia has no obvious successor or succession plan. The article did not include a statement from Nvidia.

Why it mattersNvidia sits at the center of most organizations' AI hardware plans, and a sudden change at the top with no clear successor adds governance risk to the export control and national security questions already surrounding the company. Nothing has been decided yet, but buyers, investors and boards should plan for a period of uncertainty at a key AI chip supplier rather than assume business as usual.

British AI chip startup Fractile raises a $720 million Series C, with backing from the UK's Sovereign AI Fund, TechCrunch reports

Fractile, a British company that designs chips for running AI models, has raised $720 million in a Series C round, TechCrunch reported on 11 November 2026. Thrive Capital, Founders Fund, Lightspeed Venture Partners and Redpoint led the round, and the UK government's Sovereign AI Fund also invested. The UK government put Fractile's valuation at more than £5 billion and presented the investment as a way to give Britain a bigger share of the hardware its future economy will rely on. Chief executive Walter Goodwin wrote on LinkedIn that the money will let the company "ship [its] first chips and systems next year."

TechCrunch places Fractile among the best-funded startups building chips made specifically for AI, alongside Axelera, Etched and OLIX. These designs target inference, the cost of running models once they are in use, which grows as AI agents carry out long chains of tasks on large models. Fractile describes itself as a frontier AI company and builds the full stack, including a machine learning compiler meant to speed up large language models on its own hardware. After a change of direction, its chips are built around DRAM memory stacked on each chip, which the company hopes will avoid the usual trade-off between memory and capacity.

Why it mattersInference is a large and growing recurring cost of enterprise AI, and buyers have few real alternatives to Nvidia today. A well-funded challenger with direct government money behind it adds a credible option for the late 2020s and shows that the UK is willing to take equity stakes to secure domestic AI hardware. Nothing ships until next year, so this is a supplier to watch rather than one to buy from yet.

Senator Michael Bennet presses four US officials over Megaspeed, a Singapore firm reported to have bought at least $4.6 billion of Nvidia chips, and asks for answers by 24 November

US Senator Michael Bennet of Colorado, a member of the Senate Intelligence Committee, has written to Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick, Attorney General Todd Blanche, Secretary of State Marco Rubio and Director of National Intelligence Jay Clayton about Megaspeed International, a Singapore-based company, his office said on 11 November 2026. Drawing on press reports, the letter describes Megaspeed as a possible channel for restricted US technology, including Nvidia AI chips, to reach China. According to the release, Megaspeed's units in Malaysia and Indonesia have reportedly bought at least $4.6 billion of Nvidia chips since 2023, including Blackwell processors that are not approved for export to China, and Nvidia still listed Megaspeed as a cloud partner as of 29 October 2026.

The letter cites New York Times reporting that US intelligence concluded Megaspeed executive Alice Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset, and refers to her reported close relationship with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang. It also says Megaspeed allegedly obtained chips through Aivres, a US-registered subsidiary of the sanctioned Chinese company Inspur, and may have given Chinese clients remote cloud access to restricted hardware. Bennet wants written replies by 24 November 2026, covering the Justice Department's investigation, any Commerce steps to stop Megaspeed buying Nvidia chips, such as adding it to the Entity List, and efforts to close loopholes that let Chinese companies reach chips and cloud computing through Southeast Asia or through US subsidiaries of China-based firms. The release presents these points as reported allegations.

Why it mattersThe letter adds congressional pressure on two routes that matter to anyone who sells or rents AI hardware: resellers and cloud providers in Southeast Asia, and remote access to restricted chips. If the agencies respond with Entity List additions or new licensing demands, distributors, server makers and GPU rental firms in the region could face fresh screening duties at short notice, and enterprise buyers could see some capacity providers disappear from the market.

Chinese AI companies try to lock in Nvidia computing capacity at data centers abroad, fearing the Huang scandal will bring tighter US remote-access rules, Reuters reports

Chinese AI companies are looking to rent computing power built on Nvidia chips at data centers outside China, three people familiar with the matter told Reuters in an exclusive report on 12 November 2026. Their worry is that the scandal around Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang could lead Washington to tighten the rules on remote access to advanced chips. US export controls keep Nvidia's most advanced AI chips out of China, but Chinese firms can still rent that computing power overseas. According to Reuters, one major Chinese cloud provider has debated internally whether to lock in capacity abroad, some companies have approached data center operators about reservations, and smaller AI labs are weighing rented-compute providers in Southeast Asia.

Reuters notes that the Remote Access Security Act, which would give the Commerce Department authority over remote, cloud-based access to restricted technology, passed the House by 369 votes to 22 in January and is waiting for the Senate. The report says Alice Huang, the former Megaspeed executive at the center of the scandal, had business and investment ties with Tencent and Alibaba, according to two people, and that Singapore filings show an Alibaba subsidiary financed Speedmatrix, a Malaysian company linked to Megaspeed. Megaspeed denies illegally diverting chips to China. Reuters could not verify reports that US authorities concluded Alice Huang was a Chinese intelligence asset, and China has denied them. Nvidia, Megaspeed and the Commerce Department did not immediately respond, and Tencent and Alibaba did not immediately comment.

Why it mattersRenting chips in a third country has been the main legal workaround for Chinese AI developers, and this report suggests they expect that door to narrow soon. A rush to reserve capacity could tighten GPU availability and lift prices at data centers in Southeast Asia, while any new remote-access rule would put the burden of checking who is using the hardware on cloud operators and their customers.

Meta's infrastructure chief Santosh Janardhan will hand the job to former AWS executive Dave Brown in early 2027, The Next Web reports

Santosh Janardhan, who runs infrastructure at Meta and is co-head of engineering, will step back from leading the infrastructure team in early 2027, according to The Next Web, which cites an internal Meta memo reported by Bloomberg. His successor is Dave Brown, who joined Meta this year after nearly two decades at Amazon Web Services, where he oversaw computing and machine learning services. Janardhan will stay through the first quarter of 2027 to help with the handover and will then become chief technology officer of Meta Compute. The memo did not say why he is moving.

Janardhan joined Facebook in 2009 and helped build its data centers, networks and custom chips, and in January he became a co-lead of Meta Compute with Dina Powell McCormick and Daniel Gross. Mark Zuckerberg wrote in the memo that Janardhan had "built a strong team that can carry this work forward." The Next Web puts the change in the context of Meta's spending: the company expects capital spending of up to $145 billion this year, mostly on AI, has about $825 billion in future commitments, mainly for data centers and cloud computing, and has explored renting out spare compute, including talks to lease $10 billion of compute to Anthropic. This is the second senior Meta departure planned for 2027, after policy chief Joel Kaplan.

Why it mattersMeta is one of the largest buyers of AI chips, power and data center capacity, so a change at the top of its infrastructure team in the middle of this build-out matters to every supplier in that chain. Choosing a long-time AWS compute leader while Meta explores renting out spare capacity is a sign that Meta could act more like a cloud provider over time, which would make it a possible seller of compute as well as a buyer. Suppliers and enterprise buyers should watch whether priorities shift once Brown takes over.

United Airlines bars third-party personal AI agents from booking flights for travelers, citing security risks and federal rules, CBS News reports

United Airlines said on 11 November 2026 that it will no longer allow AI agents to make flight reservations on behalf of travelers, CBS News reports. The airline is restricting unauthorized third-party personal AI agents and assistants from its own digital platforms and from bookings made through travel agencies and aggregators, and it is updating its booking and ticketing policies to match. United said that handing account logins to such assistants can expose sensitive information, and that actions taken by these tools can lead to purchases, account changes or use of MileagePlus miles that the customer did not intend.

United also said AI agents could break federal rules, such as the requirement that travelers confirm in person that their luggage holds no banned items; CBS News notes the airline did not name the specific rules. Henry Harteveldt, an aviation analyst at Atmosphere Research Group, told CBS News the move suggests United wants to keep control of the booking process rather than accept another powerful intermediary, and that flight searches cost airlines money as bot traffic rises. He said United fares will still appear on Kayak, Hopper and Google Flights. The decision contrasts with Southwest, which has made its flight inventory visible to ChatGPT users.

Why it mattersConsumer AI agents only work if the companies on the other side let them in, and United's move shows large brands may block them on security, liability and channel-control grounds. Any business planning for agent-driven sales, or any employer whose staff use AI assistants for travel, now has to plan for a mixed landscape in which some suppliers welcome agents and others shut them out.

Munich robot maker Agile Robots raises $500 million in a SoftBank-led round at an $8.5 billion valuation, with Nvidia and Hitachi joining, The Next Web reports

Agile Robots, a Munich company that builds humanoid robots and robotic arms, has raised $500 million in a round led by SoftBank that values it at $8.5 billion, The Next Web reported on 12 November 2026, citing the company's statement and Bloomberg. Nvidia, Hitachi and T.Capital also took part. TNW puts the company's total funding at about $2 billion and notes that the final amount is smaller than the roughly $800 million SoftBank was reported to be discussing about five months earlier. The company says it will spend the money on growth in the US and other markets and on more manufacturing capacity.

Agile Robots recently opened a humanoid robot factory near Munich and plans to build about 2,000 units in 2026, with output that could double or triple in 2027, according to TNW. Its newest systems are being tested with manufacturing customers including Volkswagen's Audi, and the company says it has installed more than 20,000 robotic systems worldwide. Founded in 2018 by researchers from the German Aerospace Center, it now describes itself as Europe's most valuable privately held robotics company. Chief executive Zhaopeng Chen told The New York Times the funding puts the company in a stronger position to consider a future IPO, while acknowledging that battery life, weight distribution and the quality of training data remain unsolved problems.

Why it mattersPhysical AI, the push to put AI models into machines that work on factory floors, is now attracting the same strategic backers as AI chips, with SoftBank, Nvidia and the industrial group Hitachi in one round. A European company with its own factory and carmaker pilots shows humanoid robots moving from demonstrations toward small-scale production, but about 2,000 units a year is still modest, and the company's own list of open technical problems means buyers should treat this as a pilot-stage technology.

South Korea sets out a roadmap for 10 homegrown on-device AI chips by 2030 under an 800.2 billion won program, Newspim reports

South Korea's industry ministry presented a plan on 12 November 2026 to develop 10 types of advanced AI chips by 2030 that run directly inside products such as home appliances, cars, robots and drones, according to Korean news agency Newspim. The roadmap was shown to about 150 people from companies, universities and research institutes at a general meeting of the government's AI chip alliance in Seoul. It sets the schedule for a K-on-device AI semiconductor program worth 800.2 billion won that began in June.

Newspim lists the planned chips as one for autonomous vehicles, three for smart appliances, one each for collaborative robots, humanoids, agricultural robots and unmanned drones, and two sets of chip design building blocks for advanced processes and harsh environments. One of the appliance chips is now due more than a year ahead of the original plan. A public-private committee, co-chaired by a vice minister and Kyung Hee University president Kim Jin-sang, will approve yearly plans and settle disputes between companies. Samsung Foundry plans 21 prototype production runs in 2027 for the program and will give these domestic chips priority, including on its 2nm-class process, while chip design licensors will offer more flexible payment terms to small Korean chip designers.

Why it mattersKorea is betting that AI will increasingly run on devices rather than only in distant data centers, and it is using its foundry and appliance makers to build a home supply chain for those chips. For buyers of robots, vehicles and connected equipment, this points to more non-US AI chip options later in the decade, and to government-backed suppliers that may compete hard on price.
