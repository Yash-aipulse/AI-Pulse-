OpenAI will keep investing in AI data centers even after voters backed restrictions in all but one of 41 local ballot measures, CFO Sarah Friar tells CNBC

OpenAI's chief financial officer, Sarah Friar, said on Friday, 6 November 2026, that the company will go on investing in AI infrastructure despite the voter pushback against data centers in Tuesday's US midterm elections. Speaking to CNBC's Sara Eisen at the Citi TMT Leadership Summit in Pebble Beach, California, she said the results did not dramatically change anything for OpenAI and that its spending is driven by customer demand. According to Investing.com's report of the interview, voters across 10 states backed restrictions or rejected measures meant to encourage development in all but one of 41 local ballot measures, based on Ballotpedia and additional results gathered by CNBC.

Friar said that if the election brings more attention to data centers, OpenAI will keep working to win the trust of the communities where it builds; the company published a policy blueprint and a set of commitments on Thursday. She also said OpenAI, which filed its prospectus confidentially with the US Securities and Exchange Commission in June, is still looking at a 2027 stock market debut, and that a funding round is under way. "The demand for AI is real," she said. Her comments came a day after the Nasdaq's steepest drop in 19 months, led by chip and neocloud stocks, and as the index partly recovered on Friday. Investing.com also notes that rival Anthropic aims to list on the Nasdaq in mid-November and that, according to its prospectus, it has purchase commitments and leases with total committed spend of about $895.4 billion.

Why it mattersThe midterm results have turned local resistance to data centers into a market-moving political issue, and the largest AI buyers of compute are now being asked whether they will slow down. OpenAI's answer is no, which means demand for power, land, chips and construction capacity is likely to stay high even as the rules for where and how data centers can be built get tighter. For companies that depend on that capacity, the main risk is shifting from whether the money is there to whether projects can win local approval on time.

Business spending on AI tokens grew just 1.5% across August and September after a 42% jump in July, Ramp data reported by TechCrunch shows

Company spending on AI models has slowed sharply, according to data from Ramp, the corporate card and spend management company, reported by TechCrunch on 6 November 2026. Ramp says token spend rose 1.5% across August and September, compared with 42% growth in July, even though the number of tokens used kept rising. Spending at the heaviest users, the top 1% of firms, slipped slightly to just under the roughly $10,000 per employee per month they reached at their summer peak. A typical (median) firm spends $12 to $15 per employee per month, and the top 10% spend $600 to $1,000. Spend picked up again in October, rising 8.9% between 23 September and 23 October.

Part of the slowdown reflects lower prices. TechCrunch notes that OpenAI halved API prices for its GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna models in September, and that Anthropic cut prices for Fable 5.1 and Opus 5.5, which it said in a filing reduced its revenue growth by about $600 million. Ara Kharazian, Ramp's lead economist, told TechCrunch that nearly all AI spending comes from venture-backed, fast-growing technology companies, and that the price cuts have made AI cheaper to use. "The era of hypergrowth may be over," he said. Notion co-founder Akshay Kothari posted internal data that, according to TechCrunch, matches Ramp's findings. The data lands as Anthropic prepares its stock market listing.

Why it mattersInvestors have priced AI model companies on the assumption that business spending will keep compounding at very high rates. Ramp's data suggests that usage is still growing but spending is not keeping pace, because prices are falling and a small group of tech-heavy firms accounts for most of the money. For enterprise buyers this is good news on cost, but it also means AI vendors may push harder on volume commitments and new products to protect revenue.

Microsoft is winding down an arrangement to rent AI computing capacity from Oracle, Bloomberg News reports, and Oracle shares fall nearly 3%

Microsoft is ending an arrangement under which it rented AI computing power from Oracle, according to a Bloomberg News report on Friday, 6 November 2026, that cited people familiar with the matter and was carried by Reuters. Bloomberg reported that Microsoft chose not to renew the contract and has been switching off the rented Oracle capacity over recent months, as part of a wider review of billions of dollars of cloud partnerships. According to the report, the capacity had become less useful because it ran on an older generation of Nvidia chips and no longer fit what Microsoft needs.

Reuters says Oracle shares fell nearly 3% in afternoon trading, while Microsoft's stock was little changed; both had already been under pressure this week after the Democratic midterm sweep raised the prospect of closer scrutiny of new data centers. Neither company immediately responded to Reuters' requests for comment. Reuters also points to a related signal: AI cloud company IREN said on Thursday that it had delayed some infrastructure deliveries after Microsoft asked for changes to network design, pushing its target for bringing more than $4 billion of annualized revenue capacity online from year-end to January, with a possible further slip to late February.

Why it mattersThe largest buyers of AI computing are starting to treat rented capacity as something to trim and upgrade, not simply to accumulate. If Microsoft is letting go of capacity built on older Nvidia chips, the value of AI data center capacity depends heavily on which chip generation it runs, and providers that signed deals around earlier hardware may face weaker renewals. For enterprise buyers, it is a reminder that AI capacity can age quickly and that contract length should match the useful life of the hardware.

Google buys Crusoe's Wyoming data center site a day after announcing a $17 billion investment in its first data center in the state, Reuters reports

Google has acquired the data center site in southeast Wyoming that was being developed by AI infrastructure company Crusoe, according to Reuters, which said Crusoe announced the deal on Friday, 6 November 2026. Financial terms were not disclosed. The purchase follows Google's announcement on Thursday of a $17 billion investment to build its first data center in Wyoming on the project. Crusoe and Tallgrass Energy originally developed the campus, and Crusoe says it has completed the early development work, including choosing the site, securing government approvals and setting the power strategy.

Google said it will build the facility together with Tallgrass Energy, Black Hills Energy, American Electric Power and Related Digital. Crusoe chief executive Chase Lochmiller said the company has more than 6 gigawatts of contracted capacity and over 40 gigawatts in its development pipeline. Crusoe is also developing a separate Google data center campus in Armstrong that was announced earlier this year. Reuters notes that Google is pressing ahead in Wyoming despite growing political opposition to data center projects in parts of the US.

Why it mattersThe deal shows one way hyperscalers are responding to tougher local politics: buying sites that already have approvals and a power plan in place rather than starting from scratch. For developers like Crusoe, the value increasingly lies in the early work of securing land, permits and energy, which can then be sold to the largest buyers. For utilities and energy suppliers, it confirms that large AI campuses are still moving forward in states that welcome them, with power partners named from the start.

CoreWeave's credit default swap spreads pass 1,000 basis points to a record as the AI selloff sets off stress signals across markets, Reuters reports

The sharp fall in AI stocks this week has started to show up in credit and volatility markets, according to a Reuters analysis published on Friday, 6 November 2026. Reuters reports that the cost of insuring the debt of neocloud company CoreWeave against default, measured by its five-year credit default swap spreads, rose past 1,000 basis points to a record, and describes that spread as a gauge of investor anxiety about AI. Spreads on SpaceX and Intel debt also widened this week. The VIX volatility index climbed above 28 on Thursday, its highest since late March, and is up more than 40% this week.

Other signals are mixed. The Nasdaq 100 rose nearly 2% on Friday after a drop of more than 6% on Thursday. The premium on US high-yield bonds is at its highest since the April 2025 Liberation Day tariff turmoil, and for the weakest borrowers it is near its highest since 2020. Cash funds took in $140 billion in the week to Wednesday, the seventh-largest inflow on record, according to BofA's Flow Show, and the US 10-year yield is 50 basis points below its peak above 5% last month. Reuters says bond markets are not showing stress on the scale of COVID-19 or Liberation Day. "It's still early days," said First Eagle Investments portfolio manager Idanna Appio.

Why it mattersWhen stress moves from share prices into credit markets, it affects whether AI infrastructure companies can borrow to keep building. Much of the AI data center build-out is funded with debt, so a jump in the cost of protecting against a CoreWeave default is an early sign that lenders are demanding more for that risk. Finance teams should treat this as a warning about funding conditions for AI suppliers, not yet as evidence of a wider financial crisis.

Editor's note
AI infrastructure is entering a phase where buyers, lenders and voters are all getting more selective.

Today's five verified stories describe the same shift from different angles. OpenAI's CFO, Sarah Friar, told CNBC that the company will keep investing in data centers after voters backed restrictions in all but one of 41 local ballot measures, while Ramp data reported by TechCrunch shows business spending on AI tokens grew only 1.5% across August and September as prices fell. Bloomberg News, as carried by Reuters, reports that Microsoft is winding down capacity it rented from Oracle on older Nvidia chips, and Google has bought Crusoe's Wyoming site, which already had approvals and a power plan, a day after announcing a $17 billion investment there. In credit markets, Reuters reports that CoreWeave's default swap spreads passed 1,000 basis points to a record. The common thread is that money is still flowing into AI infrastructure, but toward newer hardware, sites that are already approved and borrowers that lenders trust. For executives, the practical steps are to map AI capacity contracts by chip generation and end date, ask providers how their sites and financing could affect delivery, benchmark per-token prices, and check exposure to AI suppliers whose borrowing costs are rising.
