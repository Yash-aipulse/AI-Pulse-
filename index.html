Oracle builds OpenAI's ChatGPT and Codex into its business software, so employees can run approved Oracle workflows from ChatGPT and build apps without writing code

At its AI World event in Las Vegas on Tuesday, Oracle announced a group of products built with OpenAI. The main one, Oracle AI Workspace, places OpenAI's frontier models alongside Oracle's applications, data and cloud services in a single interface. Through plugins in ChatGPT, staff can look up information, carry out approved business workflows and take actions inside Oracle systems. Oracle says the workspace links to Oracle Fusion Applications, Oracle NetSuite, Oracle AI Database and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI), as well as other enterprise tools and data, and that governance, approvals and controls stay in place.

Three further offerings were announced alongside it. Oracle AI Security Mesh is a managed service that uses OpenAI cyber models to find and fix weaknesses in applications and infrastructure on OCI, on other clouds and on premises, with customers deciding where testing is authorized and how far it goes. Oracle App Composer lets employees without coding skills build business applications with Codex, while Oracle runs deployment and operations on OCI under enterprise identity, permission, network and audit controls. For smaller companies, a free-to-start Oracle AI SMB Bundle packages ChatGPT Business, AI Workspace, Codex, App Composer, OCI and NetSuite QuickStart, and includes $12,000 of Oracle cloud credits a year, subject to terms and restrictions. Oracle gave no release dates and says timing and pricing may change. OpenAI president Greg Brockman said the wider partnership "helps bring that opportunity to more companies."

Why it mattersOracle runs finance, HR, supply chain and procurement systems for many large companies. Letting ChatGPT act as a front door to those systems moves AI from a side tool into the core back office, where actions have financial and audit consequences. The promise is less time spent navigating screens; the test will be whether approvals, permissions and audit trails hold up when an assistant, rather than a person, starts a transaction. The no-code App Composer also means business teams could build their own applications on company data, which IT will need to govern.

Oracle widens the AI chips on its cloud, adding Google's TPUs and inference chips from Cerebras and SambaNova as it grows Nvidia Vera Rubin capacity to multi-gigawatt scale

Oracle will offer Google's tensor processing units (TPUs) on its cloud for the first time, Reuters reported on Tuesday, and also plans to offer chips from Cerebras Systems and Intel-backed SambaNova Systems, both of which design chips for inference, the stage where AI systems answer user requests. At the same time Oracle is expanding its Nvidia deals: the two companies say Oracle's cloud will grow to multi-gigawatt scale using Nvidia's newest Vera Rubin servers. They did not disclose the value of the deal. Reuters adds that some Nvidia-based systems will use Oracle's in-house Acceleron networking chips instead of certain Nvidia networking chips.

Reuters notes that Nvidia dominates chips for training AI models, while the market for inference is more competitive and varied. "Different AI workloads have different compute needs," said Mahesh Thiagarajan, president of Oracle Cloud Infrastructure, according to Reuters. Oracle did not say how it will pay for the chips. On its most recent earnings call it said customers are increasingly paying for chips, and it kept its capital spending target of about $90 billion to $95 billion for fiscal 2027. For scale, Reuters recalls that Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang has said Vera Rubin systems can generate up to $40 billion of revenue per gigawatt for Nvidia. Oracle plans to hold an investor day this week.

Why it mattersA large cloud provider offering Google's own AI chips, plus two specialist inference chipmakers, next to Nvidia's flagship systems shows that buyers want a choice of hardware matched to each job, especially for the fast-growing work of running models rather than training them. For enterprises, more chip options in one cloud can mean better prices and availability for inference. For investors, the open question is financing: Oracle is committing to multi-gigawatt capacity without saying how it will fund the purchases.

Google DeepMind previews Genie 4, a world model that generates interactive scenes with sound and lets users change them while the simulation runs

Google DeepMind on Tuesday released Genie 4, a model that generates interactive, simulated worlds in real time, as a research preview for trusted testers, TechCrunch reports. Over the coming weeks it will reach Google AI Ultra subscribers through Project Genie, and early access partners of the Gemini API and Google AI Studio. New in this version is sound generated in real time, including music, background noise and dialogue that responds to the user.

Users can also make free-form changes while a simulation is running, such as switching the weather or the camera angle, or adding people and objects to the scene. According to TechCrunch, Genie 4 can keep a scene consistent for up to two minutes, twice as long as its predecessor, Genie 3, which launched as a research preview a little more than a year ago. The report gives no figures for pricing or resolution.

Why it mattersWorld models like Genie are an early step toward software that can simulate an environment instead of just describing it. For businesses, the near-term uses are in prototyping games, ads and media concepts, and in creating varied practice environments for robots and AI agents. Two minutes of consistent scene is still short and the model is only in preview, so this is a technology to watch and test, not one to build production plans on yet.

President Trump says the administration will ban the term "AI" in official records and proposes "SI," for super intelligence, though no formal order has been described

President Donald Trump said in a post on Truth Social on Tuesday that the administration plans to ban the term AI in official records and other materials, according to Reuters. He called it "a very bad and inaccurate name" and proposed SI, short for super intelligence, in its place. Reuters says he also asked when the Wall Street Journal would start using his preferred phrase.

The Reuters report does not describe any executive order, regulation or agency guidance putting the ban into effect; it is based on the social media post alone. The report also mentions a separate executive order of 29 September 2026 about an AI-powered government website, but does not link that order to the proposed change in wording.

Why it mattersOn its own, a change of name does not alter any law or contract. But if agencies follow through, the wording of solicitations, grant notices, guidance and reporting templates could change, and companies that sell AI products to the US government would need to track the new terms to find and answer opportunities. Until a formal instruction appears, the practical effect is uncertainty rather than new obligations.
