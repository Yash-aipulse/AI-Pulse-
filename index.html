Google launched Gemini 3.5 Flash at its I/O 2026 developer conference – priced at $1.50 per million input tokens, running four times faster than competing frontier models, and outperforming the previous generation on coding, agentic, and multimodal benchmarks

At its annual I/O developer conference on 19–20 May 2026 in Mountain View, California, Google launched Gemini 3.5 Flash, the first model in its new 3.5 series. Gemini is Google's family of large language models – software systems that process text, images, and other data to generate responses and complete tasks. The new model surpasses Gemini 3.1 Pro, the previous leading model in the series, across coding, agentic task completion, and multimodal understanding benchmarks, while retaining the speed characteristics of the Flash tier: four times faster than other frontier models on output tokens per second, according to Google's own measurements.

Pricing has been confirmed at $1.50 per million input tokens, $9.00 per million output tokens, and $0.15 per million cached input tokens – roughly one-third to one-half cheaper than the previous tier. A token is roughly four characters of text; a million tokens is approximately 750,000 words. Gemini 3.5 Flash is rolling out immediately in the Gemini app, Google Search, Antigravity 2.0 (Google's consolidated developer platform), and the Gemini API. Gemini 3.5 Pro – targeting deeper reasoning tasks – was announced as in testing, with general availability expected next month. On benchmarks, confirmed scores include 76.2% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 (coding performance), 83.6% on MCP Atlas (tool-use reliability), and 84.2% on CharXiv Reasoning (multimodal understanding). Separately, Gemini CLI – Google's command-line interface for developers – is being retired, with users required to migrate to Antigravity CLI by 18 June 2026. One pricing detail worth flagging: independent analysts have noted that $1.50 per million input tokens represents ~5x the input cost of Gemini 2.5 Flash, the model it effectively supersedes, though Google frames the comparison on per-task cost rather than per-token cost given the speed advantage.

Why it mattersGemini 3.5 Flash entering the market at benchmark parity with or above the previous generation's top tier, while carrying Flash-tier pricing and speed, materially shifts the cost calculus for enterprise AI deployments. With Google CEO Sundar Pichai citing that heavy users could save more than $1 billion annually by switching to Google's models at a fraction of the cost of competing frontier systems, this is a direct pricing attack on OpenAI and Anthropic's enterprise accounts at a moment when both are preparing for public stock market listings.

Google restructured its consumer AI subscription tiers at I/O 2026 – adding a new $100-per-month entry Ultra plan, cutting the top tier from $250 to $200 per month, and replacing flat daily usage caps with a compute-based model

At Google I/O 2026, Google announced a significant restructuring of its consumer AI subscription ladder. A new "AI Ultra" entry tier priced at $100 per month was introduced, offering five times the usage limits of the $20-per-month AI Pro plan, along with 20 TB of cloud storage, YouTube Premium, and priority access to Antigravity – Google's consolidated developer and agent platform. Separately, Google reduced its highest-end AI Ultra plan from $250 to $200 per month, maintaining the same feature set at a 20% price cut; that tier retains a 20x usage limit over AI Pro. Google is also replacing flat daily prompt caps with a compute-based usage model that refreshes every five hours until a weekly ceiling is reached, with paid top-up credits planned for heavier workloads.

The pricing reset lands against a backdrop of significant usage growth. Sundar Pichai disclosed at the keynote that the Gemini app now has 900 million monthly active users – up from 400 million at I/O 2025 – while Google AI Overviews has reached 2.5 billion monthly active users. Google is now processing more than 3.2 quadrillion tokens per month, versus 480 trillion tokens per month at I/O 2025 – a ~6.7x increase in twelve months. The new $100 tier creates direct price competition with OpenAI's ChatGPT Pro at $200 per month and Anthropic's equivalent offerings, targeting developers and professional power users who need agentic capability without the full premium ceiling.

Why it mattersGoogle's multi-tier expansion effectively brackets the professional AI subscription market – $20 for casual use, $100 for developers and heavy professionals, $200 for maximum-throughput enterprise power users – at a moment when OpenAI and Anthropic are pricing above the new Google floor. The switch from flat-rate unlimited plans to compute-credit pricing also changes how enterprise procurement teams must model AI tool costs going forward.

Google launched Gemini Spark – a persistent personal AI agent running 24 hours a day on dedicated cloud servers, available to Ultra subscribers from the week of 19 May 2026 – with continuous access to users' Gmail, Calendar, and documents

At Google I/O 2026, Google unveiled Gemini Spark, a persistent personal AI agent it describes as a "24/7 partner that does real work on your behalf." Unlike conventional AI assistants that respond to individual queries, Spark runs continuously on dedicated Google Cloud virtual machines – cloud-based computers maintained by Google – and can be accessed from any device via the Gemini app, email, text message, and Chrome. It is powered by Gemini 3.5 Flash with the full Antigravity 2.0 agent harness, enabling what Google calls "long horizon tasks" – multi-step workflows that involve writing code, parsing documents, and synthesising data across time. In its launch form, Spark integrates with Gmail, Docs, and other Google Workspace apps, with planned expansion to third-party tools via the Model Context Protocol (MCP) – a standard for connecting AI models to external software – over the summer.

Google disclosed specific initial use cases: automatically parsing monthly credit card statements to flag hidden subscription fees; synthesising raw meeting notes from across emails and chats into polished Google Docs; and extracting deadline information from school communications to deliver consolidated daily digests. High-stakes actions such as sending emails or spending money require explicit user confirmation. Gemini Spark will be available to Google AI Ultra subscribers in the US starting the week of 19 May 2026, before expanding more broadly. A companion feature, "Daily Brief," pulls from Gmail, Calendar, and Tasks to present a prioritised morning digest of the day's obligations and suggested next steps. Gemini Spark's functionality depends on persistent access to Gmail, Calendar, Docs, and Chrome browsing context. Google has not yet disclosed retention policies for the cloud VM sessions, nor specific data residency terms – a gap that enterprise legal and compliance teams will need to evaluate before broad deployment.

Why it mattersGemini Spark is the first persistent, always-on AI agent from a major platform to be rolled out to consumer subscribers at scale. Its arrival places immediate pressure on Microsoft Copilot, OpenAI Operator, and Anthropic's Cowork product to accelerate their own persistent agent launches. The Workspace-first integration strategy also reinforces Google's competitive advantage against non-Google productivity suites in organisations where employees are already working inside Gmail and Docs.

The White House is preparing an imminent AI executive order that would create a voluntary framework requiring frontier AI labs to share their most powerful models with the federal government at least 90 days before public release

Axios reported on 20 May 2026, citing two sources familiar with the plans, that a Trump administration AI executive order is imminent. In its current draft form, the order creates a voluntary framework under which AI labs would share their frontier models – their most capable and powerful AI systems – with the federal government at least 90 days before public release, and would also provide access to certain critical infrastructure providers. The order's primary stated purpose is to bolster cybersecurity around advanced AI models. Both national security and civilian agencies appear to have enforcement roles under the draft. Some details were previously reported by Bloomberg.

The draft represents a notable shift for the Trump administration, which repealed the Biden-era AI executive order on Inauguration Day and had characterised heavy AI regulation as a barrier to American competitiveness. Axios reported that the shift was accelerated by concerns about Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview model – the first model verified by the UK AI Security Institute (AISI) to autonomously complete a 32-step corporate-network penetration simulation, achieving success in 3 of 10 runs. Mythos also achieved a 73% expert-level task success rate on AISI's broader benchmarks. Sources at top AI companies confirmed to Axios that they are cooperating with the White House. The Department of Commerce had already announced separate agreements with Google DeepMind, Microsoft, and xAI to test powerful models ahead of the expected order. The draft's "voluntary" characterisation is significant and its scope contains key unresolved questions: which agencies would participate in the 90-day review, what capability or compute threshold triggers the framework, and whether open-weight model releases – such as Meta's Llama series, which are freely downloadable – fall under its scope.

Why it mattersEven a voluntary 90-day pre-release framework, if adopted, would be the first formal mechanism by which the US government gains structured early access to frontier AI models before public deployment. For AI labs preparing initial public offerings – OpenAI and Anthropic are both in that category – this creates a new category of regulatory disclosure risk and could affect product launch timelines. For open-source AI, the unresolved scope question around open-weight models could determine whether the framework creates a structural competitive advantage for closed API providers.

Preparation now avoids a compressed drafting exercise under public scrutiny later.

For enterprise CISOs and critical infrastructure operators in banking, energy, and healthcare

The draft order explicitly includes critical infrastructure providers as recipients of early model access. CISOs at regulated entities should engage their government affairs teams within the next 30 days to determine whether their organisation qualifies for early access under the framework, and begin assessing what internal security review capability they would need to meaningfully evaluate a frontier model 90 days before its public release. Any requirement to delay open-weight releases 90 days would fundamentally restructure Meta's competitive differentiation from closed API providers – the entire value of the open-weight strategy depends on releasing faster and without restriction.

Alphabet confirmed $180–190 billion in AI capital expenditure for 2026 at the I/O keynote – six times its 2022 infrastructure spend – as part of a combined $680–720 billion committed by the four largest US cloud platforms in a single year

At the Google I/O 2026 keynote, Alphabet CEO Sundar Pichai publicly confirmed that Alphabet – the parent company of Google – expects to spend $180–190 billion in capital expenditure (capex) in 2026, describing this as roughly six times its $31 billion annual capex in 2022. Capital expenditure refers to spending on physical infrastructure such as data centres, computing hardware, and power systems. The figure had been previously guided during Alphabet's Q1 2026 earnings call, where CFO Anat Ashkenazi raised guidance from $175–185 billion to $180–190 billion following a single quarter in which Google Cloud revenue grew 63% year-over-year to $20 billion. Pichai confirmed at I/O that the majority of 2026 capex is directed at custom silicon – specifically eighth-generation Tensor Processing Units (TPUs), Google's proprietary AI chips: the TPU 8t for large-scale AI training and the TPU 8i for inference workloads.

To contextualise the scale: Alphabet's $180–190 billion is one component of a broader hyperscaler infrastructure commitment. Amazon projects $200 billion in 2026 capex, Microsoft is tracking toward $190 billion, and Meta has guided $115–135 billion. Combined, the four largest US cloud platforms have committed to between $680–720 billion in 2026 infrastructure spend – nearly double 2025 levels, per analysis from Fortune citing company earnings reports. Pichai also disclosed that Google Cloud ended 2025 at an annual run rate of over $70 billion. The aggregate commitment is occurring against documented supply constraints: Pichai acknowledged in the Q4 2025 earnings call that Alphabet expects to "go through the year in a supply constrained way," citing power, land, and chip manufacturing bottlenecks. At I/O, he specifically noted custom silicon as the primary near-term scaling lever, signalling that TPU investment is intended to reduce dependency on external GPU supply chains.

Why it mattersAlphabet's public confirmation of $180–190 billion in 2026 capex – at the developer conference where its AI products were simultaneously launched – signals that the infrastructure buildout and product roadmap are now explicitly linked. For the broader market, the combined $680–720 billion in hyperscaler AI infrastructure spending in a single year represents the largest coordinated private capital deployment in technology infrastructure history, with structural consequences for power grids, semiconductor supply chains, and data centre real estate that will extend well past 2026.

Editor's note
Five stories, one structural pattern: AI has moved from a product feature into critical infrastructure, and the regulatory, economic, and security consequences of that transition are now arriving simultaneously.

Google's I/O announcements are coherent only when read together. Gemini 3.5 Flash is a pricing attack on OpenAI and Anthropic's enterprise accounts. The subscription restructuring brackets the professional AI market at price points that force direct comparison. Gemini Spark is a consumer-facing declaration that persistent agents – AI systems running continuously in the background with access to your email and calendar – are no longer a research concept but a live product. And the $180–190 billion capex confirmation is Google publicly linking its infrastructure bet to its product roadmap in the same keynote. These are not individual product announcements; they are components of a coordinated platform strategy.

The White House executive order sits alongside this differently. The Trump administration, which dismantled the Biden-era AI order on day one, has been moved toward a pre-release review framework by a single demonstration: an Anthropic model autonomously completing a 32-step corporate-network takeover simulation. That one benchmark result – 3 successful runs out of 10 – appears to have done more to accelerate US AI governance than years of policy advocacy. The organisations most exposed by today's issue combined are mid-size enterprises still procuring AI as a subscription SaaS product: they face converging pressure from repriced tiers, mandatory governance requirements, persistent-agent data access decisions, and infrastructure cost signals that will flow through to API pricing across all providers over the next 12 to 18 months – all arriving faster than most enterprise change management cycles can absorb.
