Amazon Web Services grows 39% to $46.0 billion in the third quarter as Amazon spends $77.3 billion on property and equipment in three months and sticks to about $220 billion of capital spending for 2026

Amazon reported third-quarter results on Thursday showing sales at its cloud arm, Amazon Web Services, up 39% from a year earlier to $46.0 billion, with AWS operating income rising to $17.8 billion from $11.4 billion. Group net sales rose 12% to $201.9 billion and operating income reached $28.1 billion, a 13.9% margin that Amazon calls its highest ever. The cost of the AI build-out is clear in the cash flow statement: purchases of property and equipment were $77.3 billion in the quarter, against $35.1 billion a year earlier, and free cash flow over the past twelve months was an outflow of $42.4 billion, compared with an inflow of $14.8 billion a year before. Amazon still expects 2026 cash capital spending of about $220 billion.

Chief executive Andy Jassy said the AWS investment is working and pointed to projected AWS revenue growth of more than 40% in both 2027 and 2028. Amazon says spending on its Bedrock AI model service by Fortune 500 companies grew nearly 7 times year over year and total token volume nearly 17 times; Bedrock added models from OpenAI, Anthropic and others and opened a public preview of managed agents. Amazon also says customers of AgentCore, its toolkit for running AI agents, deployed twice as many agents in the third quarter as in the previous three quarters combined, and lists new AWS agreements with Meta, Nvidia, OpenAI, NATO and others. For the fourth quarter, Amazon guides to net sales of $240.0 billion to $247.0 billion and operating income of $28.5 billion to $33.5 billion.

Why it mattersAWS is the largest cloud provider, so its growth rate is a direct read on how fast enterprise AI workloads are moving from pilots into production. Amazon is now spending more on infrastructure than its operations bring in over a year, and management is openly betting that demand will keep growing faster in 2027 and 2028. For customers, that means more AI capacity and models on offer, but also a supplier with strong reasons to lock in long-term consumption commitments.

Alphabet shares fall 13% in the stock's worst day on record, the second-biggest one-day loss of market value for any US stock, as weak search and ad sales revive fears about AI's threat to Google

Alphabet's shares closed 13% lower on Thursday, the steepest one-day fall in the stock's history, Bloomberg reported in a story carried by The National. By Bloomberg's account, the value erased in a single session is the second-largest ever for a US-listed company, behind only Nvidia, which lost over $590 billion in January 2025. The selling followed third-quarter results released on Wednesday in which search and advertising sales disappointed, adding to investor worries that AI threatens Google's core business.

The drop pulled technology shares down with it. The Nasdaq 100 fell 1.4% and the S&P 500 lost 0.3%, ending three straight days of gains for both, while the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index also slid 1.4%. The damage was concentrated in big tech: an equal-weighted version of the S&P 500 rose 1.2%, with about 400 of the index's members ending higher. Enterprise software was also hit, with ServiceNow closing 27% lower, its largest decline on record. Michael O'Rourke of JonesTrading said Alphabet missed forecasts on almost every key measure apart from cloud revenue and was "playing the AI Hunger Games from a position of weakness".

Why it mattersInvestors are now separating the AI winners from those whose existing business AI may erode, and they are doing it quickly and at enormous scale. A record fall at one of the biggest spenders on AI infrastructure shows that heavy infrastructure spending is only rewarded when it is matched by growth in the core business. For companies that depend on Google for advertising, search traffic or cloud services, it is a signal that the vendor will be under pressure to show returns, which can shift pricing, product priorities and contract terms.

ServiceNow shares fall as much as 25% in their biggest intraday drop since the 2012 listing after a weaker bookings outlook, as analysts warn of renewed fears that AI will disrupt software sellers

Shares in ServiceNow, whose software runs IT, HR and customer service workflows at large companies, fell as much as 25% to $112.37 shortly after the New York open on Thursday, the steepest intraday decline since the company went public in 2012, TNW reported, citing Bloomberg. The fall followed third-quarter results released on Wednesday. Subscription revenue grew 21% to $3.988 billion, slightly above expectations, but the company guided to 18.5% fourth-quarter growth in current remaining performance obligations, a measure of contracted revenue due over the next year, while analysts on average had looked for 19%.

ServiceNow is leaning harder on AI to offset that slowdown. It raised its target for annual contract value from AI products to $1.7 billion by the end of 2026, from $1.5 billion three months earlier, and finance chief Gina Mastantuono said year-to-date use of its agentic AI in production is up more than 13 times. On results day the company also expanded its partnership with OpenAI and launched an AI governance alliance whose founding members include Anthropic, Amazon Web Services, Nvidia, Okta and OpenAI. Analysts offered several technical explanations for the softer guidance, but TD Cowen's Derrick Wood said it "could re-raise AI disruption fears", the worry that AI agents will erode traditional software business models.

Why it mattersInvestors are no longer rewarding software companies simply for AI announcements; they want to see AI turn into new contracted revenue. A half-point shortfall on a bookings measure was enough to trigger a record fall at one of the largest enterprise software vendors. For buyers, a vendor under this kind of pressure is likely to push AI add-ons and longer commitments, which gives customers more room to negotiate on price, flexibility and proof of results.

Intel's Data Center and AI sales jump 78% to $7.3 billion in the third quarter as it races to add Xeon supply, but a weaker PC outlook sends its shares down sharply after hours

Intel reported third-quarter revenue of $16.5 billion on Thursday, up 21% from $13.7 billion a year earlier. The growth came from servers: its Data Center and AI unit, which sells Xeon processors to cloud providers and businesses, grew revenue 78% to $7.3 billion, and finance chief David Zinsner called it the strongest data center growth in 15 years. Intel Foundry, which makes chips, grew 58% to $6.7 billion. The unit selling chips for PCs and edge devices shrank 5% to $8.1 billion. Chief executive Lip-Bu Tan said Intel's immediate priority is to free up more supply for what he described as extraordinary demand for Xeon.

The outlook disappointed. Intel expects fourth-quarter revenue of $15.0 billion to $16.0 billion and a GAAP gross margin of 37.1%, down from 43.0% in the third quarter, and Zinsner said he expects the PC slowdown to last at least through the first half of next year. The company also lists industry-wide shortages of substrates and memory as a risk. Intel shares were down 17.60% in after-hours trading at 4:25 p.m. New York time, StockTitan reported. Among AI highlights, Intel cited a multiyear deal to expand Xeon computing on Oracle Cloud Infrastructure and new details on its GPU D-series chip for AI inference.

Why it mattersAI is lifting demand for ordinary server processors, not only for GPUs, because AI agents and inference workloads still need general-purpose computing around them. Intel's data center business is now close in size to its PC business, but Intel says supply is tight and flags industry-wide component shortages as a risk. For buyers, that points to longer lead times and firmer pricing for servers, while the PC market faces a slower period just as many companies plan device refreshes.

Anthropic is the unnamed customer behind 34.5% of SpaceX's third-quarter revenue, Bloomberg reports, making the AI lab a bigger SpaceX client than the US government

SpaceX disclosed in a filing last week that about 34.5% of its quarterly revenue came from a single client it called only "Customer B". On Thursday Bloomberg, citing a person familiar with the matter, identified that client as Anthropic, TNW reported. That would make the Claude developer a larger customer than the US government, which has long been SpaceX's main source of income. Anthropic declined to comment and SpaceX did not respond to Bloomberg.

The relationship centers on computing capacity. According to Anthropic's own IPO filing, as cited by TNW, the lab could spend up to $120.5 billion on SpaceX computing under contracts running to 2029, about $3.1 billion a month from December. Anthropic can, however, cancel with 90 days' notice after early 2027, which leaves SpaceX heavily dependent on a client that can walk away. SpaceX also rents computing power to Google, which pays about $920 million a month, and TNW says SpaceX's AI unit made a profit for the first time last quarter.

Why it mattersAI labs have become some of the largest buyers in the technology economy, large enough to reshape the revenue of a company best known for rockets and satellites. The deal also shows how AI computing is being bought: through very large, long-dated commitments that still carry short exit clauses. For anyone assessing AI infrastructure suppliers, customer concentration and cancellation terms now matter as much as headline contract values.

UK competition regulator opens a formal investigation into whether Meta's business terms unfairly let it take publishers' and advertisers' website content for its own products, including AI features

Britain's Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) opened an investigation on Friday into whether Meta is imposing exploitative terms on publishers and advertisers, Reuters reported. The regulator's concern is that Meta's updated commercial terms could allow it to gather content from businesses' websites and use it to build its own products and services, including AI features, without those businesses having any say over whether or how their material is used. The CMA said the terms could amount to an unlawful imposition of unfair trading conditions. Meta had planned to bring in the revised terms on 20 November but has since put them on hold.

The case will first establish whether Meta holds a dominant position in a relevant UK market and then whether the terms abuse that position; no decision has been made. According to the CMA's case page, as reported by Securities.io, the work runs under Chapter II of the Competition Act 1998, with initial information gathering expected from October 2026 to January 2027, and the CMA can impose interim measures if it judges temporary action necessary. Meta told Reuters there were "no grounds" for an investigation, saying it had already committed not to change its terms without consulting publishers and other stakeholders. The probe follows complaints from publishers in the UK and Europe, who argue the terms could let Meta copy their content for AI products.

Why it mattersThis is one of the first formal competition cases to test whether a dominant platform can make access to its business services conditional on taking partners' content for AI. Whatever the outcome, it will shape the terms that publishers, retailers and other advertisers can expect from large platforms, and it gives companies in the UK a regulatory route to challenge contract clauses that hand over their data for AI use without a real choice.

AI agent startup Serval raises $150 million at a $3 billion valuation, pitching itself as a replacement for ServiceNow in IT and employee support

Serval, a San Francisco company whose AI agents handle employee support requests, has raised $150 million in a Series C round led by Sequoia that values it at $3 billion, TNW reported on Thursday. That is three times the $1 billion valuation it reached in December, when it raised $75 million, and takes its total funding to $277 million across three rounds in 12 months. Existing backers including Elad Gil, General Catalyst and Redpoint took part, and Conviction and Headline joined as new investors.

Serval's agents began with IT tasks and now also cover HR, finance, security and legal requests. A tool called Catalyst lets staff without engineering skills build automated workflows, while administrators control guardrails, permission checks and approvals. Chief executive Jake Stauch presents the product as a full alternative to platforms such as ServiceNow rather than an extra layer on top, and told Reuters that Lucid Motors moved its roughly 10,000-person organization from ServiceNow to Serval in 45 days. The company says annual recurring revenue has doubled every quarter since its previous round, and it plans to hire more than 500 people over the next 12 months, including over 100 automation engineers for a new professional services arm.

Why it mattersThe round was announced on the same day ServiceNow's shares suffered their biggest intraday drop since the 2012 listing, and it shows where investors think value is moving: toward newer companies that build around AI agents from the start rather than adding them to older platforms. For large companies, credible challengers give more options and negotiating leverage in a category that is usually hard to switch, but a fast-growing young vendor also brings delivery and continuity risk that needs managing.

South Korea's Kospi falls 3.85% in early Friday trade and SK hynix drops 6.82%, as Alphabet's results revive doubts about returns on heavy AI spending, Yonhap reports

The sell-off in US technology shares spread to Seoul on Friday morning. South Korea's main Kospi index opened 2.99% lower and by 9:15 a.m. local time was down 228.43 points, or 3.85%, at 5,709.23, Yonhap reported in a story carried by The Korea Times. The two memory makers at the center of the AI chip boom led the decline: SK hynix was down 6.82% and Samsung Electronics 3.66%, a day after chip stocks had already seen profit-taking despite Samsung's record quarterly earnings.

Yonhap linked the fall to overnight losses on Wall Street, where the Nasdaq Composite lost 1.38% and the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index 1.4%, and to Alphabet's shares dropping more than 12% after its third-quarter revenue of $127.58 billion missed expectations. The news agency said the results had raised questions about the returns on very large AI investments, amid what it called "renewed concerns about the sustainability of the AI boom." Company-specific news added to the pressure, with Hyundai Motor down 2.39% after cutting its sales target and Hanwha Aerospace down 7.76% after weaker results.

Why it mattersKorea's market is heavily weighted toward the memory chips that AI data centers consume, so it reacts quickly to any doubt about whether the biggest buyers will keep spending. A sharp fall the morning after Alphabet's slide shows how closely the AI supply chain is tied to investor confidence in a handful of US spenders. For companies buying memory and servers, today's shortages are unchanged, but a lasting shift in sentiment could eventually affect suppliers' expansion plans and pricing.

Editor's note
Investors are sorting AI winners from AI casualties at speed: cloud and server chip sales are booming on AI demand, but companies whose core business AI may erode are being punished on a record scale, and the AI labs themselves have become customers big enough to reshape their suppliers.

Amazon Web Services grew 39% to $46.0 billion in the third quarter while Amazon spent $77.3 billion on property and equipment, and Intel's Data Center and AI unit grew 78% to $7.3 billion as the company scrambles to add Xeon supply. On the other side of the ledger, Alphabet shares fell 13% on Thursday, their worst day on record and, by Bloomberg's account, the second-largest one-day loss of market value for any US stock, after search and advertising sales disappointed; ServiceNow closed 27% lower after a softer bookings outlook, even as AI agent challenger Serval raised $150 million at a $3 billion valuation. Intel's own shares fell sharply after hours following a weaker fourth-quarter outlook. Bloomberg also reported that Anthropic is the unnamed customer behind 34.5% of SpaceX's quarterly revenue, under computing contracts worth up to $120.5 billion that it can cancel on 90 days' notice after early 2027, Regulators are moving too: on Friday the UK's Competition and Markets Authority opened a formal investigation into whether Meta's business terms unfairly let it take publishers' and advertisers' website content for its own products, including AI features. The doubts crossed the Pacific overnight: Yonhap reported that Korea's Kospi was down 3.85% early on Friday, with SK hynix off 6.82%, as Alphabet's results raised questions about returns on AI spending. Taken together, AI is now redistributing value quickly: infrastructure suppliers are growing fast but depend on a few very large buyers, while incumbents in search and enterprise software must prove AI adds to their business rather than eroding it. For executives, the practical steps are to separate AI usage from core spend in cloud commitments, to secure server and memory supply early for 2027, to demand measured AI results and keep a credible alternative supplier in every major software renewal, to check how concentrated each critical AI supplier's customer base has become, and to review which platform terms let partners reuse your content for AI.
