AMD posts a record $13.4 billion quarter as data center sales nearly double to $8.5 billion, and expects about $17 billion in the fourth quarter

AMD reported third-quarter 2026 revenue of $13.4 billion on 3 November, up 45% from a year earlier and, the company says, above the top of its own guidance. Its Data Center segment, which sells EPYC server processors and Instinct AI accelerators, brought in a record $8.5 billion, up 97% year on year. AMD credits strong demand for EPYC chips and the launch of its Instinct MI450 Series GPUs. Gross margin was 54% on a GAAP basis and 56% on a non-GAAP basis, and GAAP net income was $2.4 billion. Not every business grew: Gaming revenue fell 40% to $773 million.

For the fourth quarter, AMD expects revenue of about $17 billion, plus or minus 3%, which it says would be roughly 66% higher than a year earlier and about 27% above the third quarter. The company says Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure are already running models and workloads on its first Helios rack-scale systems, which pair MI450 GPUs with its 6th Gen EPYC processors. AMD also disclosed agreements to acquire World Labs, for model research expertise, and Taalas, for specialized inference chip technology. Chair and CEO Lisa Su said the company expects "growth to accelerate further in the fourth quarter" as it ramps up production of Helios and its Venice products.

Why it mattersThe list of named Helios customers shows that the largest AI labs and cloud providers are now running production work on a second source of rack-scale AI systems, not just testing chips. For buyers, that means more room to negotiate on price and delivery for AI capacity. The outlook also suggests that spending on AI hardware was still climbing into the end of 2026, which matters for anyone planning 2027 budgets on the assumption that capacity will get cheaper or easier to secure.

Operations & Enterprise Venture Capital

Enterprise AI spending reaches an estimated $176.8 billion by September, up from $33 billion in all of 2025, Menlo Ventures says, with coding tools the largest category

Venture firm Menlo Ventures published its 2026 State of Enterprise AI report on 3 November, estimating that companies spent $176.8 billion on AI by September 2026, compared with $33 billion in 2025 – a 5.4x increase after 3.9x growth the year before. The totals are Menlo's own estimates from a bottom-up model that draws on a survey of nearly 400 US enterprise decision-makers, public filings and third-party reports. The share of enterprises paying for AI rose from 55% to 65%, which Menlo reads as growth coming mostly from existing buyers spending more. Applications accounted for about $77 billion, led by coding at $34 billion and AI assistants and productivity tools at $25 billion.

Among model providers, Menlo estimates that Anthropic's share of enterprise LLM API spending rose from 40% to 55% and OpenAI's from 27% to 31%, while Google's fell from 21% to 9%. Menlo discloses that it is an investor in Anthropic and in OpenRouter, whose usage data it drew on for some estimates. The report also says agents now generate 80% of tokens on OpenRouter, that companies spent $1.70 putting AI to work for every dollar spent on model training, and that respondents ended up spending 2.5 times what they had budgeted. Menlo found little sign that businesses are replacing core systems such as CRM or HR software: new budgets funded 55% of the additional AI spending, while 22% came out of existing software budgets.

Why it mattersIf the estimate is close, AI has moved from pilot budgets to one of the largest lines in enterprise technology spending within two years, and most of it is going to tools people use every day, especially for writing code. The finding that buyers overspent their plans by a wide margin points to usage-based pricing that is hard to forecast. The figures come from an investor in two of the companies it measures and are modeled rather than audited, so they are best read as a direction of travel rather than an exact market size.

Energy & Utilities Hardware & Devices

Drax, Brookfield and Radiant plan gigawatt-scale AI data center capacity at Drax Power Station, with Brookfield estimating total investment could exceed £20bn

UK power generator Drax and Radiant, Brookfield's UK-headquartered AI infrastructure platform, announced a partnership on 3 November to develop, deliver and operate gigawatt-scale data center capacity at Drax Power Station in North Yorkshire, The Energyst reports. Brookfield estimates the total investment could exceed £20bn, including an initial £2.5bn for a first phase that targets up to 100 MW of capacity, operational from the first quarter of 2028. A second phase aims to grow to 500 MW between 2028 and 2031, with further capacity possible after 2031. Backing comes from Brookfield's AI infrastructure joint venture with Qai.

The site's main asset is power. The estate covers 1,000 acres with 4 GW of grid access and 2.6 GW of active dispatchable generation, along with cooling systems. The first phase still depends on planning permission, regulatory and network approvals from the government and the system operator, and finding a suitable tenant. Brookfield calls the project an important piece of sovereign AI, and Radiant says it will give British researchers, startups and businesses access to frontier computing at home. The partners plan a voluntary community benefits fund and say a community consultation will start shortly, ahead of a formal planning application. Drax chief executive Will Gardiner described the station as one of the few UK sites "that can provide large-scale reliable power".

Why it mattersAccess to power, not land or chips, is often the slowest part of building an AI data center. Reusing a large power station that already has grid access is one way around that bottleneck, and it gives the UK a possible route to large-scale domestic AI capacity. The project is still a plan: the investment figure is Brookfield's estimate, and the first phase needs planning consent, approvals and a tenant before it can go ahead.

Policy & Regulation Operations & Enterprise

China's economic planner says it will speed up AI legislation and set safety standards covering the full AI life cycle, Bloomberg reports

China plans to accelerate laws governing AI from development through deployment, a senior official at the National Development and Reform Commission (NDRC), the country's economic planner, said at a briefing in Beijing on Tuesday, according to Bloomberg News as reported by PYMNTS. The official, Wang Ruomeng, said the government will "accelerate legislation to support core AI enablers", which it identifies as data, computing power and algorithms. The plans also cover regulating key AI application scenarios and setting safety standards and ethical guidelines across the whole AI life cycle. Bloomberg described them as among the most thorough guidelines yet against a loss of control as AI becomes more powerful, and noted that rules stretching from data to computing power would cover companies such as DeepSeek, Moonshot and Alibaba.

Officials also intend to review regularly how the rules are applied and revise outdated guidelines, and to monitor AI's effect on employment, with protections for workers who are displaced. The NDRC paired the tighter oversight with continued encouragement of AI development and called for international cooperation on safeguards, with Wang arguing that no single company, industry or country can handle the problem alone. The reports do not name a specific document, draft text or timetable, so the scope and effective dates of the new rules are not yet known.

Why it mattersChina is home to several of the developers whose models compete with US labs, and many companies outside China now use open models from Chinese developers. Rules that reach from training data to computing power could change how those models are built, documented and released, and how foreign companies deploy AI inside China. Linking AI policy to job protections also signals that workforce effects will be part of compliance, not only a public-relations issue.

Venture Capital Hardware & Devices

AI inference platform fal raises $1.3 billion led by Bessemer, doubling its valuation to $16 billion as demand for running AI models surges

Fal, a San Francisco company that hosts and runs AI video, image and audio models, has raised $1.3 billion in a Series F round led by Bessemer Venture Partners, with Nvidia, Sound Ventures and T. Rowe Price also taking part, TechCrunch reported on 3 November. Citing Bloomberg, TechCrunch said the round values fal at $16 billion, double the $8 billion valuation it reached in April. Fal announced the new round together with its earlier Series E, which Sequoia led, and said the two raised $1.65 billion in total.

The raise is tied to fast-growing demand for inference – the work of running trained models to produce outputs – particularly from customers using open-source models. Bloomberg reported that fal's annualized revenue passed $800 million earlier this year. Competitors Baseten, Fireworks AI and Modal are also growing quickly and raising large rounds, but TechCrunch, citing several people familiar with the companies' finances, reports that their gross margins remain thin because buying or leasing compute is expensive. Fal was co-founded in 2021 by Burkay Gur and Gorkem Yurtseven.

Why it mattersInvestors are now putting very large sums behind the companies that run AI models, not only those that build them, which shows where usage, and spending, is growing. For businesses that rely on these platforms for image, video or audio generation, the thin margins reported across the sector are a reminder that prices and capacity can change quickly when compute costs move. Fal's valuation doubled since April, so expectations for its growth are high.

Editor's note
The money flowing into AI is still compounding, from chips to enterprise budgets to the platforms that run models: the harder questions are now about power supply, rules and whether buyers can keep their spending under control.

Today's five stories trace the AI spending chain from end to end. AMD reported a record $13.4 billion quarter, with data center sales up 97% to $8.5 billion, and expects about $17 billion in the fourth quarter, with Anthropic, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure already running work on its Helios systems. In the middle of the chain, inference platform fal raised $1.3 billion at a $16 billion valuation, double its April level, even as TechCrunch reports that margins across the sector remain thin. The constraints are coming into view as well: in the UK, Drax, Brookfield and Radiant want to use a power station with 4 GW of grid access to host AI capacity that Brookfield says could draw more than £20bn of investment, but the first 100 MW phase still needs planning consent and a tenant, while in Beijing the NDRC said China will speed up AI legislation and set safety standards across the full AI life cycle, without yet publishing a text or timetable. For executives, the practical steps are to get a second supplier quote for AI capacity before 2027 contracts are signed, to put monthly caps and alerts on usage-priced AI tools, to keep model contracts flexible, to confirm capacity commitments with inference providers, to map AI systems and models that could fall under China's coming rules, and, for anyone counting on new UK capacity, to treat power and planning milestones as the real timeline.
