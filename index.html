Financial Services Hardware & Devices

The Nasdaq closes 2.86% lower and Intel sinks about 29% as worries about AI spending and cash burn spread from Alphabet and Amazon to chipmakers

US technology shares fell sharply on Friday, the last trading day of October. According to closing figures compiled by 24/7 Wall St., the Nasdaq Composite ended 2.86% lower and the S&P 500 lost 1.80%, or 139 points, to 7,581.40, with the damage concentrated in AI and growth technology stocks. Intel closed at $65.12, down 29.3%, the widest single-day move among the 380 stocks the site tracks most closely. Other hardware stocks also fell hard, including SanDisk (down 13.6%), Applied Optoelectronics (down 12.9%) and Coherent (down 11.8%), while some healthcare stocks, including Regeneron and AstraZeneca, rose.

Reuters tied the selling to the week's earnings. Amazon reported a sharp cash outflow in the third quarter and signaled that capital spending will rise again next year, while Intel warned it would burn cash this year because of a weak PC market, manufacturing problems and heavy spending to add production. By late morning, Reuters reported, the S&P 500 technology sector was down 3.9%, on course for its worst day since 23 June, the chip index had lost 7%, and the Nasdaq-100 was set to lose just over $1 trillion in market value on the day and more than $2 trillion over the month. Money moved into consumer staples and healthcare instead. Joe Saluzzi of Themis Trading said AI-related stocks had run ahead of earnings, adding: "right now, they're not producing."

Why it mattersAfter Alphabet's record fall on Thursday, Friday's selling shows investors are now demanding proof that AI spending pays off, and they are punishing companies whose cash flow is shrinking to fund it. Hardware suppliers that rode the build-out are being re-priced alongside the cloud providers they sell to. For buyers, a weaker share price and tighter cash can change how hard vendors push on pricing, long-term commitments and supply priorities over the coming quarters.

Venture Capital Financial Services

OpenAI cuts the target for its latest funding round by $10 billion to $20 billion, at a lower $1.25 trillion valuation, the Financial Times reports

OpenAI has lowered the amount it hopes to raise in its current funding round by $10 billion, according to the Financial Times, which cited people with knowledge of the matter. Reuters, which relayed the FT report late on Friday, said the company is now seeking $20 billion at a valuation of $1.25 trillion.

Reuters had previously reported that OpenAI was aiming for $30 billion at a $1.4 trillion valuation. On our arithmetic, the new terms shrink the planned round by one third and the valuation by $150 billion, or about 11%. The Reuters report did not give a reason for the change.

Why it mattersOpenAI is the best-known private AI developer, and its funding terms act as a reference point for the whole sector. If it is settling for less money at a lower price, private investors are showing price discipline even at the top of the market. That matters for anyone whose plans assume AI funding keeps rising: startups pricing their own rounds, suppliers counting on lab budgets, and companies building on a single model provider.

Financial Services Operations & Enterprise

S&P turns its outlook on Meta to negative, saying the cost of the AI build-out leaves the company's spending and cash flow highly uncertain

S&P Global Ratings moved its outlook on Meta Platforms to "negative" from "stable" on Friday, Reuters reported, pointing to significant uncertainty about the company's capital spending and free operating cash flow. The move comes a week after Meta forecast annual capital expenditure of $135 billion to $145 billion and reported a third-quarter free cash flow deficit of $2.12 billion, against positive free cash flow a year earlier. Meta has also said it plans to sell $97.5 billion of stock to pay for its AI infrastructure.

S&P now expects free operating cash flow deficits of $39 billion in 2026 and $71 billion in 2027, and says the figure could stay negative beyond 2028. The agency believes Meta's leverage could climb above 1.0x as early as 2027, the threshold it uses for an "AA-" issuer credit rating; leverage was 0.1x in 2025. S&P also judged Meta's ability to earn money from its AI investments to be more limited than its peers'. A Meta spokesperson said the company respectfully disagrees with that assessment. Meta's shares were little changed in extended trading on Friday.

Why it mattersUntil now, worries about AI spending have mostly shown up in share prices. A rating agency warning that one of the strongest balance sheets in technology could weaken brings those worries into the credit market, where they affect borrowing costs and how lenders, suppliers and partners judge counterparty risk. It also sets a public test for every big AI spender: show when the build-out turns cash positive.

Policy & Regulation Financial Services

The SEC's enforcement chief says the agency is watching how companies disclose AI risks, as investors worry about a possible AI bubble fueled by circular deals

The US Securities and Exchange Commission is monitoring what AI companies tell investors in their corporate disclosures, its enforcement director David Woodcock told Reuters on Friday. The comments come amid investor concern about a possible AI bubble, which Reuters notes may have been fed by circular deals, and in a week when big tech shares fell after companies disclosed they are burning cash to pay for AI expansion while interest rates rise.

Woodcock, who took over the enforcement division in May, said the agency does not want to hold back innovation, but that its job is to make sure issuers give investors a full picture of the risks; where disclosure falls short, he said, enforcement "will be present." He also warned that AI has made scams much easier and cheaper to produce at scale, putting more people at risk, and said brokers and other firms need processes to protect retail investors.

Why it mattersWhen the top enforcement official at the main US markets regulator says publicly that AI disclosures are being watched, listed companies should expect closer questions about how they describe AI revenue, spending, partnerships and risks. Deals in which suppliers, customers and investors are the same parties are now explicitly part of the conversation. For financial firms, the warning about AI-enabled scams points to expectations on customer protection, not only on corporate reporting.

Venture Capital Operations & Enterprise

AI inference company Baseten nears a funding round of about $2 billion that could value it at up to $30 billion, more than double its June valuation, Bloomberg reports

Baseten, a startup whose platform companies use to run AI models in production, is close to raising about $2 billion, Bloomberg reported on Friday, citing people familiar with the matter. According to the report, as republished by Investing.com, the money would come in two tranches, the first valuing the company at $26 billion and the second at $30 billion. Existing investor Sands Capital is expected to lead, and the round is not yet final.

Baseten was valued at $13 billion in June, when it raised $1.5 billion; by our calculation, the top of the new range is about 2.3 times that level. Founded in 2019, the company also counts Altimeter Capital, IVP and Spark Capital as investors. Its rival Fireworks AI, backed by Sequoia Capital, raised $1.5 billion in July at a $17.5 billion valuation. The report links the raise to growing demand for inference, the stage where trained models are put to work. Baseten did not respond to requests for comment, and Sands Capital declined to comment.

Why it mattersInference is where AI spending recurs every time a model is used, so it is the part of the stack that grows with real adoption. Investors paying up for inference platforms on the same day OpenAI reportedly trimmed its own round suggests money is moving toward the layer that earns from usage. For buyers, a crowded and well-funded inference market means more choice and more room to negotiate serving costs.

Legal & Professional Services Venture Capital

AI-first law firm Crosby bids for the brand and trademark of collapsing Wall Street firm Weil, Gotshal & Manges, and is trying to hire some of its roughly 1,200 lawyers

Crosby, a law firm founded around two years ago that delivers legal work with its own AI, has made an offer for some assets of Weil, Gotshal & Manges, the long-established Wall Street firm that is winding down. TechCrunch confirmed the bid, which The Information reported first. The assets in play are Weil's trademark, its brand and some internal data that was not specified. Crosby is also separately approaching some of Weil's roughly 1,200 lawyers about joining. The terms were not disclosed, and TechCrunch notes there is no guarantee Crosby will win.

Weil told The New York Times it expects to vote on dissolving within days. According to the Times, the decline followed the refusal of the expected next leader to take the role, the exit of several top earners and the shelving of a planned merger with Willkie Farr & Gallagher; TechCrunch says nobody has blamed legal AI for Weil's troubles. Crosby differs from legal AI vendors such as Harvey and Legora, which sell software to existing firms, because it practices law itself. It has raised about $85 million, including a $60 million Series B in March led by Lux and Index, after launching in 2025 with $5.8 million in seed funding.

Why it mattersAn AI-native firm trying to buy the name of one of the best-known US law firms is a clear signal that venture-backed, technology-led firms now see themselves as direct competitors to traditional partnerships, not just suppliers to them. If the bid succeeds, a legacy brand would sit on top of an AI-first delivery model. For corporate legal buyers, it widens the choice of providers and adds pressure on established firms to explain how they price and staff work.

Editor's note
The market's patience with AI spending is running short, and AI is testing old business models: investors are selling companies whose cash is being consumed by the build-out, OpenAI is reportedly asking for less money at a lower price, a rating agency is questioning Meta's cash flow, the main US markets regulator is watching what they tell shareholders, and an AI-first law firm is bidding for a famous Wall Street name.

Friday closed a punishing week for technology shares. The Nasdaq fell 2.86% and Intel lost about 29% after Amazon's cash outflow and Intel's warning that it will burn cash this year added to the doubts raised by Alphabet's record fall on Thursday, and Reuters reported during the session that the Nasdaq-100 was on track to lose more than $2 trillion in market value over October. After the close, S&P Global Ratings turned its outlook on Meta negative, forecasting free operating cash flow deficits of $39 billion in 2026 and $71 billion in 2027 as the company spends on AI, which moves the debate from share prices into credit. That evening, the Financial Times reported that OpenAI has cut its funding target by $10 billion to $20 billion at a $1.25 trillion valuation, below the $1.4 trillion Reuters had earlier reported, while Bloomberg reported that inference company Baseten is nearing a round of about $2 billion at up to $30 billion, a sign that investors are getting choosier about price while still backing the infrastructure that runs AI day to day. On the same day, the SEC's enforcement director told Reuters that the agency is monitoring AI companies' disclosures amid concern about a possible bubble fed by circular deals, and warned that AI is making scams cheaper to produce at scale. Late on Friday, TechCrunch confirmed that Crosby, an AI-first law firm, has bid for the brand and trademark of Weil, Gotshal & Manges as that firm prepares to vote on dissolving, a sign that AI-native providers now want to compete with traditional firms directly. Taken together, the questions for AI are shifting from how much companies spend to what that spending returns and how honestly it is described. For executives, the practical steps are to tie each AI investment to a measurable cash outcome, to stress-test plans that assume AI funding keeps rising, to lock in 2027 hardware supply and pricing with vendors now under pressure, to make sure every public AI claim is backed by evidence, and, in financial services, to test defenses against AI-generated scams aimed at customers, and, in legal departments, to start comparing AI-first firms with traditional panel firms on real work.
