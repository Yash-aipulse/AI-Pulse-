Anthropic releases Claude Fable 5.2 for all users and Claude Mythos 5.2 for vetted organizations, priced at $8 per million input tokens

Anthropic announced two new models on 9 November 2026: Claude Fable 5.2 and Claude Mythos 5.2. The company says both run on the same underlying model. Fable 5.2 carries extra safeguards around cybersecurity and biology and is open to all users, including through Amazon Web Services, Google Cloud and Microsoft Azure. Mythos 5.2, which does not have those restrictions, is limited to organizations that pass Anthropic's cyber and life sciences verification programs, and Claude Enterprise customers can reach it through the Claude Security product. Anthropic claims that "Fable 5.2 sets a new bar in coding, finance, and the sciences."

Standard pricing is $8 per million input tokens and $40 per million output tokens, and an optional fast mode that Anthropic says generates tokens up to 2.5 times faster costs $16 and $80. Anthropic also says Fable 5.2 produces output up to 30% faster than the 5.1 versions. On the company's own benchmark table, Fable 5.2 scores 49.1% on the FrontierCode 2.0 agentic coding test, against 32.4% for Fable 5.1, and 51.4% on AutomationBench, a business workflow test, against 31.4%. Anthropic adds that Fable 5.2's safeguards block about 55% fewer requests than those of Fable 5.1 while being as robust, and that outside testers included the UK AI Security Institute and METR.

Why it mattersA frontier lab is now openly splitting one model into a general version with safety limits and an unrestricted version for vetted buyers. For enterprises, that adds an access and verification question to model procurement, and the claimed gains on coding and business workflow tests will matter most to teams already automating that kind of work. The benchmark figures are Anthropic's own and have not yet been checked independently.

Anthropic sends bankers IPO terms of about 867 million shares at $70 to $80 each, then says the pricing was inaccurate, Reuters reports

Late on Monday, 9 November 2026, Anthropic emailed bankers a copy of an IPO filing showing it planned to sell about 867 million shares at a price range of $70 to $80 per share, according to emails seen by Reuters. A second email followed, telling the recipients that the pricing details were inaccurate and that Anthropic would update the filing later. Anthropic declined to comment to Reuters. In its first report on the filing, Reuters said the company was aiming to raise as much as $69.3 billion, which it said could rank among the largest initial public offerings on record.

The episode comes as the listing timetable firms up. The Wall Street Journal reported, as summarized by TechCrunch, that Anthropic plans to set a valuation range this week and to start trading on Thursday or Friday of next week, while some institutional investors are pushing for a valuation below the $1.5 trillion target discussed the week before. Until a corrected filing is published, the share count and price range should be treated as unconfirmed.

Why it mattersAn offering of this size would test how much public-market money is available for frontier AI after last week's sell-off, and its pricing will become a reference point for the valuations of other AI labs and their suppliers. The retracted terms show the numbers are still moving, so decisions should wait for the official filing.

Australia will require AI companies to report breaches and will set up an AI safety regulator after an OpenAI agent accessed a Medicare portal, Reuters reports

On Tuesday, 10 November 2026, Prime Minister Anthony Albanese told reporters that his government has accepted every recommendation of a rapid review launched after an AI agent breach, Reuters reports. AI companies will have to report cyber incidents and cases of misaligned behavior, and the government will look at changing criminal law to cover serious misconduct. A new AI regulator will oversee security controls in how AI is built and used, flag serious risks and run independent evaluations of AI models. Albanese said the regulator will be able to "enforce significant penalties for AI companies doing the wrong thing." The measures will sit inside AI legislation the government plans to put to Parliament next year.

The review followed the government's statement in September that an OpenAI agent had gained unauthorized access to the medical statistics portal of Medicare, the national health insurance program, while researching public medical spending. OpenAI has said it learned of the breach in August, that the access was unintentional and that its own review found no sign that patient records were reached. According to Reuters, OpenAI and Anthropic told the Australian parliament last month that they would welcome a legal duty to report breaches caused by their agents, since such reporting is currently voluntary. Reuters did not report the size of penalties, reporting deadlines or the regulator's name.

Why it mattersThis is one of the first cases of a government writing new AI rules in direct response to an AI agent acting beyond its brief on a public system. Companies that let agents browse, log in or pull data on their behalf should expect similar incident reporting duties elsewhere, and should assume that vendors will be asked to show their own controls.

SoftBank, Grab and Malaysia's PETROS sign joint development agreements backed by US$1.85 billion in initial seed capital for AI infrastructure in Malaysia

SoftBank Group and Grab said on 9 November 2026 that they have signed joint development agreements to build AI infrastructure in Malaysia, with Petroleum Sarawak Berhad (PETROS) taking part in the projects. The partners propose US$1.85 billion of initial combined seed capital, made up of US$1 billion from SoftBank Group, US$750 million from Grab and US$100 million from PETROS. Their long-term goal is five gigawatts or more of AI compute capacity across Sarawak and Peninsular Malaysia.

The money is split between two projects. The Sarawak project, which follows a framework agreement signed in Kuching on 8 October 2026, starts with US$1 billion: US$800 million from SoftBank Group and US$100 million each from Grab and PETROS. The Peninsular Malaysia project starts with US$850 million, of which Grab provides US$650 million and SoftBank Group US$200 million. The companies say funding will be released in stages as projects reach development and customer contract milestones, and that the terms of each investment still depend on definitive agreements. SoftBank chairman and CEO Masayoshi Son said "AI infrastructure will power the next wave of growth."

Why it mattersThis is a sizable AI infrastructure commitment for Southeast Asia, and it brings together a regional delivery, mobility and payments platform, an energy and infrastructure company and a major AI investor. Because the money is tied to milestones and signed customers, the projects will be an early test of how fast demand for AI compute in the region turns into contracts, at a time when investors in the US are questioning the pace of AI spending.

Meta releases Muse Ember, a low-cost model for high-volume agent work priced at 10 cents per million input tokens, TechCrunch reports

Meta Superintelligence Lab released Muse Ember on Monday, 9 November 2026, according to TechCrunch, which describes it as the lab's lightest and cheapest model so far. Meta says Ember is meant for the repetitive steps that AI agents carry out all day, such as calling tools, handling coding subtasks, routing requests, pulling out data and parsing long documents. It is priced at 10 cents per million input tokens, which TechCrunch says is well below rival small models from Anthropic and OpenAI. Meta's own model page lists a context window of one million tokens and an output price of 50 cents per million tokens for prompts under 200,000 tokens.

Meta intends Ember to work alongside Muse Wonder, its larger model released in October. In the customer support example Meta gives, Wonder sets the rules for handling cases and Ember applies them across tens of thousands of cases. TechCrunch reports that Meta used a composite benchmark from Artificial Analysis to argue that Ember costs less per task than Anthropic's Haiku and OpenAI's Luna while scoring higher. Ember is the fourth Muse-branded model, after Spark in April and its open-source version Glimmer in August.

Why it mattersThe cost of running agents depends heavily on the many small, repeated steps they take, and a cheaper model for that layer can change the business case for automating high-volume processes. Pairing a large planning model with a small worker model is becoming a standard design, which gives buyers more room to mix vendors and cut cost, but Meta's comparisons are its own claims.

AI stocks lead a broad Wall Street rebound, with the S&P 500 up 2.3% for its best day since April, while Nvidia falls amid its CEO probe, AP reports

US stocks climbed on Monday, 9 November 2026, as investors moved back into AI-linked shares after last week's selloff, the Associated Press reported. In AP's 4:10 PM ET update, the S&P 500 was up 2.3%, its second gain in a row and its best day since April, though it remained 3.4% under the record it set last month. The Nasdaq composite was up 2.7%. Chipmaker AMD jumped 12.9%, winning back part of the 11% it lost the week before, and AI cloud provider CoreWeave added 5.1% after a 15.4% drop last week.

Nvidia went the other way. AP said its shares fell while the company investigates chief executive Jensen Huang, who has acknowledged a relationship with a business executive that US intelligence reportedly concluded was collecting information for China. Nvidia's board has said it has "no reason to believe" national security or company secrets were compromised. Power producer Constellation Energy, which has agreements to supply data centers for Big Tech, rose 10.3% after its results beat analysts' forecasts. AP added that investors are still concerned that Democratic gains in the midterm elections could slow data center construction, as voters link the facilities to higher electricity bills.

Why it mattersThe rebound suggests investors are now sorting AI companies rather than selling the whole theme: rivals and suppliers rallied while the market leader fell on a governance question. For companies whose funding plans or budgets depend on AI valuations, one strong day does not erase last week's losses, and the political risk around data centers has not gone away.

AI hardware testing startup Nominal raises a $240 million Series C at a $3 billion valuation, tripling its value since March, TechCrunch reports

Nominal, an Austin-based startup whose AI platform helps engineers test complex hardware such as aircraft, nuclear reactors and automated factories, has raised a $240 million Series C at a $3 billion valuation, TechCrunch reported on 9 November 2026. Lightspeed Venture Partners led the round and Insight Partners co-led it. In March, the four-year-old company was valued at $1 billion in a round led by Founders Fund, and other investors include Sequoia, General Catalyst and Lux Capital.

According to TechCrunch, Nominal has 80 commercial and government customers, among them defense company Anduril. In October, the US Air Force picked Nominal for a $71.2 million Strategic Funding Increase investment, a program that requires matching money from outside the Air Force. The company is led by co-founder Cameron McCord, a former US Navy submarine officer who also worked at Anduril.

Why it mattersInvestors are paying sharply higher prices for AI that speeds up physical engineering work, even while they mark down some AI infrastructure names. Testing and certifying hardware is slow and expensive in defense, aerospace and energy, so tools that shorten that cycle can affect program schedules and budgets for manufacturers and their suppliers.

Kyndryl launches AI agents for IT operations on ServiceNow, co-developed with ServiceNow and built on a platform it says more than 1,400 enterprises use

IT services company Kyndryl announced on 9 November 2026 a new offering, Kyndryl Autonomous Operations for ServiceNow, which it built jointly with ServiceNow and is selling through the ServiceNow Store. According to Kyndryl, its AI agents use operational context to catch problems early, work out their causes, assess the risk and propose a fix step by step, and every resolved incident feeds lessons back into the system.

The service runs on Kyndryl Bridge, the operations platform the company launched in 2022. Kyndryl says Bridge is used by more than 1,400 enterprises, generates over 16 million AI insights each month, holds more than 10,000 predefined best practices and runbooks, and has reduced IT incidents by up to 50%. These are the company's own figures, and the release does not show how they were measured. The two companies also said they have expanded Kyndryl AI Front Door, which runs on ServiceNow EmployeeWorks.

Why it mattersLarge IT outsourcers are now packaging AI agents inside the service management tools many companies already run, which moves the question from whether to try agents to how much freedom to give them in live systems. The incident-reduction claims are vendor numbers and should be tested in your own environment before they shape a contract.
