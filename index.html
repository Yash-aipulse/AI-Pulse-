Google Cloud revenue more than doubles to $30.7 billion as Alphabet sells its TPU AI chip systems to outside customers and spends about $51.4 billion on property and equipment in a quarter

Alphabet reported third-quarter results after the US market closed on Wednesday, with total revenue of $127.6 billion, up 25% from a year earlier, according to its earnings release. Google Cloud was the standout: revenue rose 103% to $30.7 billion, and the unit's operating income climbed to $11,121 million from $3,594 million a year earlier. Alphabet says sales of its TPU systems, the AI chips it designs in-house, were a meaningful contributor to that growth, and that Cloud's product revenue comes mainly from selling those systems. CEO Sundar Pichai credited demand for Google's AI infrastructure and AI products.

Spending kept pace. Alphabet's purchases of property and equipment reached $51,355 million in the quarter, against $23,953 million a year earlier, and $131,953 million so far this year. Pichai said the Gemini app has reached one billion users and that Google's own model APIs now process nearly 32 billion tokens a minute, more than four times the level of a year ago. He also said the company is "now on a half-trillion dollar revenue run rate." Net income available to shareholders fell to $26.2 billion from $35.0 billion, which the release mainly attributes to an $8.7 billion loss on equity securities.

Why it mattersGoogle is no longer just renting out computing time; it is selling its own AI chip systems to other companies, and that is now a large part of why its cloud business doubled. For buyers, this widens the choice beyond Nvidia-based capacity and gives more leverage in negotiations. For investors and finance teams, the quarter shows both sides of the AI build-out: strong revenue and profit growth in cloud, alongside a quarterly equipment bill that has more than doubled.

Microsoft's Azure grows 48% and its contracted future commercial revenue rises 73% to $677 billion, while quarterly spending on property and equipment roughly doubles

Microsoft reported results for the July to September quarter, the first of its 2027 fiscal year, on Wednesday afternoon, according to its earnings release. Revenue rose 19% to $92.3 billion and revenue from Azure, its cloud platform, grew 48%. Microsoft Cloud revenue reached $63.9 billion, up 30%. Operating income rose 21% to $45.9 billion, and net income rose 32% to $36.6 billion, or $4.92 per share; gains linked to its OpenAI investment added $207 million to net income. Microsoft now reports under new segments, and the larger one, Agents and Infra, brought in $76,795 million of revenue, up 25%.

Two figures show how much future AI demand Microsoft is lining up and paying for. Commercial remaining performance obligation, the value of contracted revenue not yet recognized, grew 73% to $677 billion. Additions to property and equipment came to $39,367 million, compared with $19,394 million a year earlier, roughly double (calculated). CEO Satya Nadella said Microsoft is offering customers "greater capability, choice, control, and cost efficiency" across its technology stack.

Why it mattersAzure's growth rate and the $677 billion of signed-but-unbilled commercial business indicate that large customers are committing to multi-year AI and cloud spending, not just experimenting. That supports Microsoft's continued heavy investment, but it also means capacity is being reserved well in advance. Enterprises that have not secured their own AI capacity and pricing may find less room to negotiate as backlogs grow.

Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Research & Science

Google launches Gemini 4 Carbon, which it calls its most powerful model, giving cyber defenders first access and pricing it at $2 per million input tokens

On Wednesday, two weeks after releasing Gemini 4 Argon, Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon, according to Investing.com. Google presents it as its strongest model so far, with a particular edge in cybersecurity and in tasks that run over long periods. Google says Carbon ranks first on version 5.0 of the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, with a score of 59. On Google's cybersecurity tests, it scored 88.2% on SEC-bench Pro for finding vulnerabilities, 54.8% on ExploitGym for exploiting them and 71.0% on CWE-bench for patching them. Google also says its Chrome team found Carbon more than 10 times better than another lab's large frontier model at producing high-quality patches.

The rollout is staged. Selected cyber defenders get access from this week, followed by more partners through Google's Fairwind Program, and only then developers, businesses and consumers. Google says it is taking part in the US government's voluntary process for testing models before release, and reports a 0.0% attack success rate on Gray Swan's indirect prompt injection benchmark. Pricing is $2 per million input tokens and $10 per million output tokens, the same as Argon's introductory price, with cached input tokens 95% cheaper than standard input. All benchmark figures are Google's own and have not been independently checked.

Why it mattersGoogle is the latest frontier lab to treat cyber capability as something to release to defenders before anyone else. That gives security teams a window to use the model on their own code and systems before the same capability is widely available. For buyers, the pricing matters as much as the benchmarks: a top-ranked model at Argon's introductory price, with steep discounts for cached input, puts pressure on what rival labs can charge for comparable work.

The US Justice Department is asking how Nvidia's $12.93 billion purchase of Hugging Face would affect competition in AI, Reuters reports

The US Department of Justice has been asking questions about how Nvidia's planned $12.93 billion acquisition of Hugging Face, the open-source platform where developers share AI models and datasets, could affect competition, Reuters reported in an exclusive, citing two people familiar with the matter. According to Reuters, officials want to know whether the deal would hand Nvidia control of an important channel for distributing AI, and whether Nvidia could use the platform to hurt rivals. Nvidia expects the purchase to close in the first half of 2027.

Reuters says the questions are part of a longer-running antitrust investigation into Nvidia's conduct, covering its acquisitions, investments and sales practices, and whether it pressures customers. Officials have also looked at Nvidia's December 2025 purchase of SchedMD, its 2025 licensing and hiring deal with Groq, including whether Nvidia favored customers that bought its networking equipment and Groq processors, and its acquisition of Run:ai. The probe began under President Joe Biden and has continued under President Donald Trump, and may not lead to any action. The Justice Department declined to comment on specific matters, Nvidia and Groq declined to comment or did not respond, and Hugging Face did not respond.

Why it mattersHugging Face is where many companies find, test and download open models. If the world's largest AI chip supplier owns that hub, regulators want to know whether it could tilt the field toward its own hardware. Any conditions or delay attached to the deal would affect companies that rely on the platform, and a wider case on Nvidia's sales practices would matter to everyone buying AI infrastructure.

SAP replaces some of the costliest AI models in its products with cheaper ones, a shift it says will help deliver more than €2 billion in AI efficiency gains

SAP has taken some of the most expensive AI models out of its products, including its internal consulting assistant, and put lower-cost alternatives in their place where performance held up, Chief Financial Officer Dominik Asam told Reuters. The German software maker says the change will help it reach more than €2 billion in AI-driven efficiency gains. It uses more than 70 AI models from providers including OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, and Asam said it increasingly chooses the cheapest model that can do a given task to the required standard.

SAP is not trying to spend less on AI overall; Asam expects that spending to rise as adoption grows. In written answers to Reuters, the company said its aim is "healthy margins as scale grows, not an absolute reduction in AI expenditure." Because SAP bills customers for completed tasks rather than for the computing used, lower running costs feed straight into profit. Asam also said government agencies and companies in regulated industries are setting specific requirements and excluding some models based on where they come from, and that European customers increasingly want local options.

Why it mattersOne of the largest business software vendors is now openly treating AI models as interchangeable parts to be bought on price. That is a useful signal for any company building AI into its own processes: the most capable model is often not the right one for routine work, and the savings from matching model to task can be large. It also shows that a model's country of origin is becoming a buying criterion for regulated customers.

Anthropic tells California Governor Gavin Newsom that officials should be able to ask a court to block the release of AI models that pose a serious risk

In a letter dated 15 October to California Governor Gavin Newsom, Anthropic proposed that government officials be able to seek court orders stopping the release of AI models found to pose a "serious risk" to the public, Reuters reported on Wednesday. An Anthropic spokesperson confirmed the letter is genuine. Newsom set up a panel of AI experts in September to draw up safety proposals, and Anthropic sent its recommendations after that. Politico first reported the letter's contents, according to Reuters.

Anthropic argues against rules that would make developers build kill switches able to shut down an entire model. It prefers narrower steps, such as disabling a single AI agent that is causing problems or cutting off one customer's access, which it says would work better. Reuters notes that in September a bipartisan group of US senators debated a bill that would let the government block unsafe models, with companies able to challenge decisions in federal court, but it has not advanced; in July, House lawmakers proposed an "AI Kill Switch Act." The letter comes as Anthropic prepares for its US stock market listing.

Why it mattersA leading AI developer is backing a legal route for blocking a model before launch, while arguing against blanket shutdown powers. If California adopts something similar, model releases could face review and possible court challenge, which would affect launch timelines for vendors and the planning of companies that depend on them. Anthropic's preference for targeted measures, such as disabling one agent or one customer, also points to the kinds of controls enterprise users should expect in their contracts.
