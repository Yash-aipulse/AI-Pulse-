Howard Lutnick pushed for a narrower US rule on Chinese use of AI chips in overseas data centers, a rule that touches businesses linked to Cantor Fitzgerald, The New York Times reports

Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick has argued inside the administration for a less stringent version of planned controls on Chinese companies using advanced US chips in data centers outside China, The New York Times reported on Sunday, 15 November 2026, in a story republished by The Philadelphia Inquirer. The report focuses on A201, a data center in southern peninsular Malaysia run by Singapore-based Bitdeer. It is due to open next year with hundreds of millions of dollars of Nvidia AI servers and would provide computing power to a large Chinese tech company, reportedly Tencent. That setup is legal, but it works around US limits on selling advanced chips directly to Chinese firms. Bitdeer has said the Malaysian facility should bring in $1.7 billion in revenue over five years, and it is one of five data centers the company plans to open in the country.

According to the Times, Cantor Fitzgerald, the firm Lutnick ran for decades, is a Bitdeer client: it was a designated sales agent this year for a Bitdeer equity offering of up to $1 billion and helped arrange a stock offering of up to $250 million in 2024. The paper says Cantor also has dealings with companies in Thailand, Indonesia, Singapore, Australia and Texas, and holds a stake in Bitdeer through its investment in Tether. Lutnick recused himself when he joined the government in 2025, though a limited waiver lets him make some decisions affecting Cantor, and his remaining stake had passed to his family as of October 2025. The draft Commerce rule would cover seven countries – Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand, Indonesia, Brazil, South Africa and Norway – apply only to future chip shipments, require companies to notify the government rather than obtain a license when Chinese customers train AI models in their facilities, and apply only above higher computing thresholds; other officials want it to apply worldwide. The Times says it found no evidence that Cantor's investments shaped Lutnick's views. A Commerce spokesperson said Lutnick makes decisions based on US interests and does not discuss department business with his sons; Cantor declined to comment and Bitdeer did not respond.

Why it mattersThe scope of this rule will decide whether Chinese companies can keep renting top-end Nvidia hardware in Southeast Asia, and the report adds a conflict-of-interest question to a decision already under pressure after Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia. A seven-country rule that spares facilities already running would leave much of today's capacity untouched, while a worldwide rule would reach much further. Either way, AI computing capacity in the region now carries regulatory and reputational risk that buyers, lenders and investors need to price in.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says Nvidia should hire an outside CEO and explain alleged chip shipments to China within 10 days, Seeking Alpha reports

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent told Fox News that Nvidia should recruit its next chief executive from outside the company following Jensen Huang's reported removal, Seeking Alpha reported on Sunday, 15 November 2026. Colette Kress has been named interim CEO. Bessent also said Nvidia should explain alleged shipments of its chips to China within 10 days, with the company due to release its quarterly earnings on Tuesday.

According to Seeking Alpha, Bessent argued that existing US export rules are enough as long as they are enforced, even as lawmakers advance proposals to tighten them. Seeking Alpha adds that the allegations raise questions about Nvidia's compliance oversight and could affect its access to customers and its revenue outlook. The report does not include a response from Nvidia. The remarks put a senior administration official on record about both who should run Nvidia and how it should account for where its products ended up.

Why it mattersPublic pressure from the Treasury Secretary on a listed company's succession is unusual, and it lands on Nvidia's board just before an earnings release where investors will want answers on leadership and export compliance. Bessent's view that current rules only need enforcement also suggests the administration may lean toward stricter policing of existing controls rather than new legislation, which matters for every company in the AI hardware supply chain.

Jensen Huang is weighing a return as Nvidia CEO and allies are organizing to lobby for it, the Financial Times reports

Jensen Huang, who stepped down as Nvidia's chief executive on Thursday, has talked with people close to him about coming back to the job, the Financial Times reported on Sunday, 15 November 2026, citing people familiar with the matter. The report was carried by wire services and republished by The Egyptian Gazette. Nvidia did not immediately respond to a request for comment outside regular business hours.

According to the FT, one Huang ally said they were putting together a "war room" of Silicon Valley executives to press for his return, would try to win support from the White House, and said the effort had Huang's backing. The same ally argued that Huang is widely liked across the technology industry outside the frontier AI labs. The report comes days after the FT said Nvidia's board had approached Arm CEO Rene Haas as a possible successor. Huang's exit followed revelations of extramarital affairs, including one involving a suspected Chinese spy, which raised questions about exports of Nvidia's advanced AI chips to China.

Why it mattersNvidia's leadership is now contested from several directions at once: the board's own external search, a senior administration official calling for an outside hire, and an organized campaign to bring Huang back. Until the board settles the question, customers, suppliers and investors face uncertainty over who will set Nvidia's strategy, pricing and export-compliance posture at a time when it supplies much of the world's AI computing capacity.

KB Securities says Samsung Electronics stands to gain most as AI chip demand spreads beyond Nvidia after Jensen Huang's exit, bloomingbit reports

Korean brokerage KB Securities expects Samsung Electronics to be the biggest beneficiary as competition in the AI chip market shifts following Jensen Huang's departure as Nvidia's chief executive, the Korean financial news site bloomingbit reported, citing a report from KB research head Kim Dong-won. In KB's view, the leadership change adds uncertainty to Nvidia's business in the near term, but over the longer term it will speed up the diversification of AI chip supply, with Google's TPUs, Amazon Web Services' Trainium, other custom chips (ASICs) and AMD GPUs emerging as alternatives to Nvidia.

The argument rests on memory. KB forecasts that next year Nvidia will account for 35% of Samsung's high-bandwidth memory (HBM) shipments, with the other 65% going to customers outside Nvidia, including Google, Amazon and AMD. For global HBM demand over the same period, it projects shares of 38% for Nvidia, 37% for Google, 13% for AMD and 12% for others. The brokerage also expects rising demand for customized HBM, server DRAM and high-capacity memory as custom chips spread. These figures are one brokerage's forecasts, not guidance from Samsung or its customers.

Why it mattersThe forecast puts Google almost level with Nvidia as a buyer of high-bandwidth memory next year, which would change who memory makers prioritize when supply is allocated. For buyers of AI computing, it is another sign that the market is moving from a single dominant chip supplier toward several, so capacity plans and supplier negotiations built only around Nvidia deserve a second look.

India, South Korea, Qatar and the UAE sign a US-led declaration to protect AI supply chains, with Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission expected to follow, ANI reports, citing the Financial Times

India has joined the United States and several partners in signing the San Francisco Declaration on the Secure Foundations of Enduring Prosperity, an agreement aimed at protecting critical technologies and the supply chains behind AI as competition with China intensifies, the news agency ANI reported from Washington, citing the Financial Times. According to US Under-Secretary of State for Economic Affairs Jacob Helberg, South Korea, Qatar and the United Arab Emirates have also signed, and Germany, Sweden, the Netherlands and the European Commission were expected to sign the following week. The report says the US, Japan, Australia, Israel and the UK are among the signatories, and that the countries will work toward a shared list of critical technologies and AI supply chains that need stronger protection.

The declaration sits within Pax Silica, the State Department initiative Helberg launched in December 2025 that covers semiconductors, critical minerals, energy and advanced manufacturing; the report puts its total signatories at 24. Helberg's argument, as reported, is that chip design know-how, chipmaking equipment and shipping can each sit in a different country, so no single government can secure the chain alone, and that partners have agreed to treat the security of these technologies as a shared responsibility. The report also notes that Quad foreign ministers earlier this month announced more than $85 million in assistance for trusted AI infrastructure in the Indo-Pacific, and that the signing comes as President Donald Trump prepares to meet President Xi Jinping at the APEC summit in Shenzhen this week.

Why it mattersA joint list of protected AI technologies and supply chains is the kind of document that later turns into investment screening, export rules and procurement preferences across signatory countries. For India, signing puts its chip, data center and critical minerals ambitions inside a US-aligned framework just as Washington and Beijing head into a summit where AI chips are expected to be on the table.

Alibaba launches its Qwen4-Max flagship model and says average cost per task on its own benchmark falls to $1.67 from $4.43, Startup Fortune reports

Alibaba has released Qwen4-Max as the new flagship of its Qwen family of AI models, following a preview that began on 11 November, Startup Fortune reports. On Alibaba's own Work360 benchmark, which the company says spans 721 professional fields, the model costs an average of $1.67 per task against $4.43 for its predecessor Qwen3.8-Max, and uses up to roughly half as many tokens. Qwen4-Max scored 52.69 on that test. The model is promoted with a 1 million-token context window, although the report notes another outlet's account that the base window is 262,000 tokens, extendable to 1 million.

On price, Startup Fortune, citing the newsletter AlphaSignal, puts API rates on Alibaba's QwenCloud at $1.50 per million input tokens and $4.50 per million output tokens, each about 25% below Qwen3.8-Max's $2 and $6. Alibaba has not disclosed the model's parameter count or architecture details, and the report says the Work360 results have not been independently reproduced. The figures are therefore the company's claims on a benchmark it designed, not a neutral comparison with US models.

Why it mattersThe pitch is less about topping leaderboards than about doing work tasks for far less money, which is the measure enterprise buyers increasingly care about when they run AI agents at scale. A Chinese flagship priced well below its predecessor adds pressure on what US providers can charge, though companies weighing it will also need to factor in data location and regulatory questions around Chinese AI services.
