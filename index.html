Fitch Ratings says tighter US chip controls will slow Asia-Pacific data center investment and hit neocloud operators hardest, Reuters reports

Fitch Ratings expects data center spending across Asia-Pacific to cool and become choosier, Reuters reported on Thursday, because the US chip rules that took effect on 19 November put pressure on operators that depend on Chinese customers and on advanced American chips. The rules require licenses to ship covered chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia and Thailand, and to overseas data centers that supply significant computing power to companies based in China. According to Reuters, Fitch believes capital will move toward projects with assured chip supply and a broad customer base, and that the gap between owners of physical data center facilities and heavily indebted computing providers will widen.

Fitch singles out neoclouds – companies that rent out AI computing – as the most exposed, since their business depends on keeping expensive GPUs busy. Operators focused on China may have to cut prices, sell less advanced computing, or stay on their current Nvidia chips without upgrading, which Fitch says could weigh on sales, profits and competitiveness. Projects close to completion could slip if operators cannot obtain chips or confirm that customers are eligible, pushing back the point at which they earn revenue, and Fitch expects lenders to run more checks and hold back funding or new loans where chip supply or customer eligibility is uncertain. The warning came a day after S&P placed Nvidia's rating on watch for a possible downgrade over governance concerns, while Moody's said Jensen Huang's reinstatement as chief executive reduced near-term execution uncertainty but that governance and regulatory risks remain.

Why it mattersA credit rating agency is now saying the export rules will change who gets financed, not just who gets chips. Lenders and investors in Asian data center projects are likely to build chip access and customer eligibility into their terms, so projects that rely on Chinese demand should expect slower or tougher funding.

Japan's largest power generator, JERA, is in talks with Anthropic, Meta and other US tech companies to build data centers next to its Tokyo-area power plants, Reuters reports, citing Nikkei

JERA, the biggest electricity producer in Japan, is negotiating with Anthropic, Meta and other large American technology companies about placing data centers close to its power stations around Tokyo, Reuters reported on Friday, citing the Nikkei newspaper. According to Nikkei, Yukio Kani, chief executive and chair of JERA Global, said in a recent interview that the group is also holding discussions with Amazon, Microsoft and Google.

The talks follow a partnership JERA announced in October with Dell Technologies and RHAELM, a UK-based AI infrastructure developer, aimed at speeding up the build-out of AI infrastructure in Japan. Under a memorandum of understanding, the partners plan to create a standard model for building AI infrastructure at national scale, starting with a $15 billion hyperscale data center in Chiba, near Tokyo. Reuters said JERA and the five US technology companies did not immediately respond to its requests for comment, so the talks have not been confirmed by the companies themselves.

Why it mattersElectricity, not chips alone, now decides where AI capacity can be built. A utility offering sites beside its own plants gives AI developers a shortcut to power, and a deal with any of these companies would make the Tokyo area a more serious option for large AI workloads in Asia.

South Korea's finance minister meets the central bank and market regulators as authorities warn that AI uncertainty is making stocks more volatile, Yonhap reports

South Korean officials said on Friday that doubts about the AI sector, together with shifts in global trade, are adding to swings in the country's stock market, according to a Yonhap report published by Korea JoongAng Daily. Finance Minister Lee Hyoung-il held a meeting earlier in the day with the heads of the Bank of Korea, the Financial Services Commission and other agencies to review market conditions. Policymakers said the risks arising from the close link between the semiconductor sector and the stock market need careful monitoring, as volatility tied to AI is also rising in markets worldwide.

The meeting comes a week after the authorities announced steps to steady the market, including lifting the limit on share buybacks by listed companies. Officials are also working to enlarge a stock-market stabilization fund, currently set at some 11 trillion won ($8.2 billion), because the total value of the market has grown sharply. The fund was set up by financial institutions and was last activated after the 2008 global financial crisis. The report does not say how large the enlarged fund would be or when it might be used.

Why it mattersWhen a finance minister brings the central bank and the market regulator together over AI-linked swings, the AI trade has become a financial-stability issue, not just an investment theme. By naming the chip sector's tie to the stock market as a risk, Korean officials signal that further backstop measures are on the table.

AI could displace around 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over five years in an illustrative scenario from JP Morgan Wealth Management, ANI reports

A JP Morgan Wealth Management report sets out a scenario in which AI displaces around 24 million knowledge-worker jobs worldwide over the next five years, ANI reported on Friday. That equals about 1.3 percent of the global knowledge workforce each year. The scenario assumes that half of potential enterprise AI spending goes toward replacing jobs that would otherwise have been filled, with AI costing about half as much as the workers it replaces. If two-thirds of those roles were in the US, the report says around 16 million American workers could be affected over five years, or roughly 3 million a year – about 2 percent of payrolls annually, more than four times the pace of manufacturing job losses in the 2000s and close to the Great Recession rate, but spread over five years rather than two.

JP Morgan says the more likely result is disruption within jobs rather than the outright loss of roles, noting that "Much of the AI adjustment could come through roles left unfilled rather than layoffs." According to ANI, the report also says only 6 percent of US work hours are currently assisted by AI, even though two-thirds of tasks may in theory be exposed, and it sees a bigger risk if displacement becomes concentrated in particular occupations or regions.

Why it mattersThe report is a scenario, not a forecast, but it gives boards a number to test workforce plans against. Its main message for employers is that the effect is likely to show up first in hiring and backfill decisions, and the small share of work hours using AI today suggests most of the adjustment is still to come.

Asian markets extend their recovery from the AI chip sell-off in early Friday trade, with Tokyo's Topix up 1.01% and Nasdaq futures up 1%, Kotak Neo reports

Asian shares started Friday higher as investors kept returning to chip and technology names after Wednesday's heavy losses, Kotak Neo reported. As of 6:57 AM IST, South Korea's Kospi was up 0.67 percent, Japan's Nikkei 225 up 0.3 percent and the broader Topix up 1.01 percent, while the MSCI Asia Pacific index had gained 0.6 percent. The move was not universal: Hang Seng futures were down 0.4 percent and Taiwan's TAIEX opened 0.15 percent lower. With Wall Street shut on Thursday for Thanksgiving, S&P 500 futures were up 0.8 percent and Nasdaq futures up 1 percent at 7:23 AM IST.

The gains follow a bruising stretch for AI-linked stocks. According to the report, the Kospi lost more than 6 percent on Wednesday before Japanese chip-related shares led a rebound on Thursday, when Kioxia Holdings jumped around 12.5 percent and SoftBank Group rose more than 7 percent. US exchanges hold a shortened Black Friday session from 8 PM to 11:30 PM IST, which will give the first read on how American investors view the week's AI chip swings.

Why it mattersTwo days of gains do not undo a week of AI-driven losses, but they suggest the forced selling has paused for now. For finance teams, the thin Black Friday session in New York is a weak signal, so the more reliable test of the recovery comes when full trading resumes next week.
