AI chip and data center stocks lead a sharp US selloff, with the S&P 500 down 3.2% and the Nasdaq down 5% by late morning on Thursday, AP reports

Companies tied to the construction of AI data centers drove a steep fall on Wall Street on Thursday, 5 November, according to the Associated Press. In its report from around 11 a.m. Eastern time, the S&P 500 was down 3.2% and heading for its worst day since April 2025, the Nasdaq composite had lost 5%, and the Dow Jones Industrial Average was down 1,146 points, or 2.1%. These are intraday figures from the middle of the trading session, so closing levels may differ.

Nvidia, the stock with the biggest influence on the S&P 500, was down 8.3% after a 3.2% fall the previous day. AMD dropped 15.9%, AI cloud provider CoreWeave 14.9% and power equipment maker GE Vernova 9.1%. AP linked the selling to two worries: that more expensive borrowing could slow data center building, and that Democrats' midterm victories make it more likely that governments pause new data center construction, which would mean fewer AI chip orders. Uncertainty over the war with Iran and the outlook for energy markets added to the pressure.

Why it mattersThe stocks that fell hardest are the suppliers that most AI programs depend on: chips, GPU cloud capacity and power equipment. A one-day drop of this size shows investors are now putting a price on political and financing risk to the data center build-out, not only on demand. Lower valuations can make it harder and more expensive for these suppliers, especially younger GPU cloud providers, to raise money for new capacity, which can feed through to availability and contract terms for their customers.

Vistra falls 13.5% and KeyBanc downgrades it after Democrat Gina Hinojosa, who backs a pause on new data centers, wins the Texas governor's race

Shares in Texas power producer Vistra dropped 13.5% on Wednesday, 4 November, after Democrat Gina Hinojosa beat Republican Governor Greg Abbott by about seven percentage points, Investing.com reported. She is the first Democrat elected governor of Texas since 1990. During the campaign, Hinojosa called for a halt to new data center construction until lawmakers set further requirements, a bigger say for local communities, protection for local water and electricity supplies, and for developers rather than households and taxpayers to pay for the infrastructure their projects need.

KeyBanc analyst Sophie Karp cut her rating on Vistra from Overweight to Sector Weight. She argued that the result adds significant policy uncertainty for companies that sell power to large industrial customers and data centers, and named Vistra as the most exposed company KeyBanc covers. Karp also sees Constellation Energy as vulnerable, though less so because its power plants are spread across more regions. Investing.com noted that Democrats also made gains in Georgia, where they won control of the Public Service Commission that regulates utilities, and in Ohio. Vistra is due to report third-quarter results on 6 November.

Why it mattersTexas has been one of the main destinations for new AI data centers, and power producers there have been valued partly on the promise of long contracts with those projects. A governor-elect who wants a pause on new builds and wants developers to carry grid costs changes the risk for projects that are not yet built. Her proposals are campaign positions, not law, but the share price reaction shows that investors now treat state politics as a direct input into the value of AI power deals.

AI and data centers were a major factor for 56% of US midterm voters, an NBC exit poll found, as candidates critical of data centers won key races, TIME reports

Public unease about AI and data centers became a deciding issue in Tuesday's US midterm elections, TIME reported on 4 November. It cited an NBC exit poll in which 56% of voters said AI and data centers were a "crucial or major factor" in their vote for Congress. Democrats spent more than $100 million on broadcast ads portraying Republicans as too close to Big Tech and data centers, and won many of those contests, including Sherrod Brown's five-point victory over Jon Husted in the Ohio Senate race. Local bans or pauses on data centers passed in places such as Edgewater, Florida, and Bloomington, Illinois, and mayors in Piqua, Ohio, and Yukon, Oklahoma, were recalled over their support for projects.

Spending linked to the tech industry did not reliably buy results. TIME says Elon Musk was the largest donor of the cycle at $446 million, yet Ken Paxton, who had about $168 million in support from Musk, lost the Texas Senate race to James Talarico. Groups that favor AI safety outspent pro-acceleration groups in several key races. TIME adds that sweeping federal AI legislation is still unlikely soon, because Democrats would need substantial Republican support to overcome a presidential veto; Brendan Steinhauser of the Alliance for Secure AI told TIME he expects more congressional scrutiny and a wave of state and federal bills. OpenAI is reportedly planning a policy and publicity push that includes a new name for its data centers.

Why it mattersFor the AI industry, community consent is becoming as scarce as power and chips. When a majority of voters say AI and data centers shaped their vote, local officials have strong reasons to slow approvals, ask for tax and utility concessions, or put projects to a vote. With Washington unlikely to pass broad rules soon, the real policy action is likely to happen in state capitals, county boards and utility commissions.

Policy & Regulation Energy & Utilities

OpenAI pledges that its future AI campuses will pay their full power and grid costs, and publishes a blueprint urging regulators to make large users pay the grid costs they create

OpenAI released two documents on 5 November 2026, a National Community Compact and a Compute Campus Policy Blueprint, Unite.AI reports. The compact covers future campuses that OpenAI owns and operates. OpenAI says it will pay for the electricity those sites use and for the infrastructure needed to serve them, offer financial assurances before major spending so that other utility customers are protected if a project is delayed, scaled back or canceled, and give utilities realistic forecasts of its power needs. It also commits to disclosing site-level water and electricity use, publishing plans for construction traffic, noise and lighting, giving each campus a water plan that favors closed-loop or low-water cooling, and not asking public officials to sign nondisclosure agreements.

The compact points to local spending already agreed. In Effingham County, Georgia, OpenAI has an $80 million commitment to community priorities, including a $4.8 million grant for free school meals and $2 million for a local park, and in Pike County, Ohio, it made a joint $6 million commitment with SB Energy after families named childcare as a priority. The blueprint, aimed at the wider industry, asks state utility commissions to adopt large-load tariffs so that big electricity users pay the incremental grid costs they cause, and asks that discretionary tax incentives come with public terms, measurable commitments, reporting and clawbacks. In Georgia, OpenAI is contracting for 3.2 gigawatts delivered in phases between 2028 and 2032, and Georgia Power can cut deliveries to the campus by up to 1,000 megawatts at times of high demand. The documents build on OpenAI's Stargate Community commitments of 20 January 2026 and its Grid Protectors program launched on 2 November.

Why it mattersComing as data centers turned into an election issue, this is the largest AI developer trying to set the terms of the debate: it wants its own promises to become the floor for the industry and to be written into utility tariffs and incentive rules. If commissions adopt large-load tariffs and clawbacks along these lines, every hyperscaler and developer will face similar costs and disclosure duties, which raises the bar for smaller builders and makes site economics depend more on local negotiation than on headline tax breaks.

About 600,000 Nvidia H200 AI chips are waiting in Hong Kong for shipment to mainland China as Beijing withholds approval for large-scale imports, Reuters reports

Roughly 600,000 of Nvidia's H200 processors are being held in Hong Kong until they can enter mainland China, Reuters reported in an exclusive on 5 November, citing two people familiar with the matter. According to the news agency, Beijing has not signed off on large-volume imports even though Chinese technology companies want the chips, and the stockpile had not been reported before. The H200 is not Nvidia's newest design, but Reuters notes that it still offers capabilities Chinese-made alternatives have yet to match. Washington had barred H200 sales to China until the Trump administration, following US-China trade negotiations, allowed Nvidia to sell to approved Chinese customers last year.

Some chips are still getting through. Three people told Reuters that China has let some H200s in over recent months, and one said several major Chinese internet companies have each obtained about 10,000 of them in that period; small quantities have also moved through intermediaries and indirect routes. The shortage shows up in prices: one person said an eight-card H200 server was being offered in mainland China for more than 6 million yuan, about $900,000, by the end of October, roughly three times its price a year earlier, while servers built on Nvidia's Blackwell chips were offered for more than 20 million yuan each. China's industry ministry and its National Development and Reform Commission, as well as Nvidia, Alibaba, ByteDance, DeepSeek and Tencent, did not respond to Reuters' requests for comment.

Why it mattersFor Nvidia's China business, the gate is now in Beijing as well as Washington: chips cleared for export are sitting at the border because China has not approved them for import. That points to continued pressure on Chinese firms to rely on domestic processors, keeps Nvidia's China revenue uncertain, and is pushing up the cost of AI computing inside China. The figures come from unnamed sources and none of the companies or agencies commented, so they should be treated as reported rather than confirmed.

Qualcomm's quarterly revenue falls 8% to $10.3 billion as handset chip sales drop 26%, while it reiterates a $5 billion data center revenue target for fiscal 2027

Qualcomm reported on 4 November that GAAP revenue for its fiscal fourth quarter, which ended on 27 September 2026, was $10.32 billion, 8% less than a year earlier, with non-GAAP earnings of $2.17 per share. The main drag was phones: revenue from handset chips fell 26% to $5.14 billion. Other businesses grew, with automotive revenue up 89% to a record $1.99 billion and IoT revenue up 4% to $1.89 billion. Revenue for the full fiscal year came to $43.1 billion.

For the current quarter, Qualcomm guided to revenue of $10.4 billion to $11.2 billion, a range that now includes its data center business, and it repeated its target of $5 billion in data center revenue for fiscal 2027. The company projects non-handset revenues to grow by more than 60% year over year, and it listed a multi-generational product collaboration with Amazon for AI data centers among its highlights, alongside renewed long-term licensing deals with Apple, Huawei and Transsion. Chief executive Cristiano Amon called fiscal 2027 "the start of our non-handset revenue inflection."

Why it mattersQualcomm is trying to move from a phone chip supplier to a broader computing company, and AI data centers are the biggest new bet. The quarter shows why: handset revenue is shrinking fast, while cars, devices and soon servers carry the growth story. A $5 billion data center target would make Qualcomm a real alternative supplier for AI inference, which matters to buyers looking for more choice beyond the current leaders, but the target still has to be delivered.

ABB confirms China has barred SoftBank from buying its robotics business in China, part of a $5.375 billion deal, after the FT reported concerns over AI training data

ABB said on Thursday, 5 November, that China's foreign investment security review authority has prohibited SoftBank from acquiring the China part of ABB Robotics, Rocking Robots reported. SoftBank, which handles the regulatory approval process, passed the decision on to ABB. SoftBank agreed in October 2025 to buy the whole robotics division for $5.375 billion, a deal that needed approval in the European Union, China and the United States. The two companies say they are still talking and are "working constructively on the transaction," but ABB has not said how the ruling changes the deal's timing or structure.

The Financial Times first reported the block, citing several people familiar with the matter, Reuters reported. According to that report, China's National Development and Reform Commission was concerned that robotics data sent abroad could be used to train AI models, including those of OpenAI, in which SoftBank is a major investor. Reuters noted that China accounts for about 30% of ABB's robots business. The division has about 7,000 employees and had 2024 revenue of $2.3 billion, about 7% of ABB group revenue. The deal is central to SoftBank chief Masayoshi Son's plan to combine AI with physical robots.

Why it mattersThis is one of the clearest cases yet of a government treating industrial machine data as a strategic AI asset and blocking a deal over it. Any company whose robots, sensors or connected equipment collect data in China now faces the question of whether that data can leave the country, and acquirers of businesses with large Chinese operations should expect AI data questions to feature in approval reviews.

Financial Services Policy & Regulation

US bank regulator the OCC issues a framework guiding examiners on how to review banks' use of AI and AI agents, while adding no new requirements for banks

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency, which supervises US national banks, released a supervisory framework on 4 November to help its examiners evaluate how banks use AI, including agentic AI, in their normal business, PYMNTS reported. The document, titled the "OCC Supervisory Framework on Bank Use of Super Intelligence," was issued with news release NR 2026-97 and bulletin 2026-54. PYMNTS notes that Super Intelligence is the term the executive branch now uses in place of artificial intelligence, following an executive order dated 29 September.

The OCC makes clear that the framework is not enforceable, does not set supervisory expectations and contains no prescriptive requirements, and that banks are not meant to use it to judge their own AI use or risk management. Examiners can use it to identify AI-specific considerations that add to existing reviews of model risk management, governance, technology, compliance and operational risk. They should not treat AI as a separate risk category, and banks do not need to define a separate risk appetite for it. The agency calls its approach technology-neutral: it expects banks to operate safely and soundly whatever technology they use.

Why it mattersThe framework tells banks what examiners will actually look at: AI and agents will be judged inside existing model risk, governance and operational risk reviews rather than under a new rulebook. That lowers regulatory uncertainty, but it also means weak documentation of how agents are tested, monitored and controlled will show up as ordinary exam findings. Banks that have treated agent pilots as experiments outside normal controls will need to bring them in.
