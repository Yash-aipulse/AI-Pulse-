Nvidia has told partners including Dell and Supermicro to stop shipping servers with its AI chips to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia as new US export rules take hold, the Financial Times reports

Nvidia has instructed its manufacturing partners to halt deliveries of servers built with its chips to four Southeast Asian markets – Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia – according to a Financial Times report on Thursday, as summarized by The Next Web. The FT cites three people familiar with the matter and says partners such as Dell and Supermicro have told buyers in those countries that Nvidia will not allow their orders to ship. The paper says it is not clear whether the pause is temporary while Nvidia and its partners work out how to comply with the new US controls.

Those controls, which took effect on 19 November, require a US license to ship advanced AI chips to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil, The Next Web reports. The region matters to Nvidia: revenue booked through its Singapore subsidiary reached $104bn in 2025, up 72% on the previous year. One cloud provider executive told the FT, "It's causing sheer chaos in the region." Singapore has said it wants a trusted route for its companies to keep buying advanced chips, while Malaysia's trade minister has told data center operators to follow the US rule. Nvidia, Dell and Supermicro did not immediately respond to requests for comment.

Why it mattersThis is the first sign that the new US licensing regime is stopping physical deliveries, not just adding paperwork. Any company building or renting AI capacity in Southeast Asia should assume Nvidia-based server orders there can be frozen at short notice, and that delivery dates quoted before 19 November may no longer hold.

US computing curbs and tougher guards against distillation are slowing China's next AI models, with Z.AI's roughly 3 trillion-parameter GLM-5.5 behind schedule, Reuters reports

Z.AI, the Chinese developer also known as Zhipu AI, had planned to launch its next flagship model, GLM-5.5, this month but has not yet done so, two people familiar with the matter told Reuters. The model has roughly 3 trillion parameters. One of the people said safeguards that US developers use to stop their models being distilled – copied by training on their answers – had partly held the work up, and that new US computing restrictions could push the release into the first quarter of 2027 or later. Z.AI did ship GLM-5.4 on Monday, built on an existing 750 billion-parameter model, and says the new design matches models with 2 trillion to 3 trillion parameters. Z.AI did not respond to Reuters' request for comment.

Moonshot AI is under similar strain, according to Reuters. One person said the company, which rents its computing power and favors overseas providers, expects its usual six-month gap between major releases could stretch to 12 months or longer if foreign access is cut off entirely, and estimated that Kimi K4 could slip until around the middle of 2027; Moonshot has also stepped up renting capacity in Europe. The pressure comes from a Commerce Department rule in force since 19 November that requires licenses for Chinese companies to use large amounts of advanced US chip computing in overseas data centers, at a threshold Reuters puts at roughly the equivalent of 750 Nvidia H100 chips, with 90 days for existing providers to obtain licenses. Anthropic said in September it had seen nearly 200 million Claude exchanges tied to suspected distillation campaigns that it attributed to Chinese developers including Alibaba, Moonshot and Z.AI.

Why it mattersUS policy is now aimed at rented computing and model outputs, not only at chip sales, and Reuters' reporting suggests it is starting to slow Chinese labs. Companies that use Chinese models for low-cost deployments should plan for slower upgrades, and cloud and data center operators outside the US carry new licensing obligations whenever Chinese customers use their capacity.

Jensen Huang's return to Nvidia intensifies the fight in Washington over China's access to advanced chips, with three bills pending and Democrats taking both chambers on 3 January, Reuters reports

Jensen Huang's reinstatement as Nvidia's chief executive on Monday has sharpened a debate in Washington over how far China should be able to obtain advanced American chips, Reuters reports. Investors cheered the move, but some US officials were uneasy and lawmakers from both parties called for tighter controls. According to Reuters, Nvidia brought Huang back under pressure from President Trump after he had resigned over revelations of extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a former executive at Singapore-based Megaspeed, a company accused of ties to Chinese intelligence. On Monday the Commerce Department put Megaspeed and several other firms on a trade blacklist that stops US companies from selling them chips and other technology, and Huang is due to be interviewed in Congress next week.

Reuters points out that policy had already started moving. Trump approved sales of Nvidia's H200 AI chips to China last December, but in recent weeks US authorities have tightened licensing rules for some chip shipments and for overseas data centers that serve Chinese companies. Three bills now before Congress – the AI Overwatch Act, the Chip Security Act and the Match Act – would narrow China's access to semiconductors further, and Democrats will hold large majorities in both the House and the Senate after 3 January. Senator Tom Cotton, the Republican who chairs the Senate Intelligence Committee, wrote that the Nvidia and Megaspeed affair "is just the tip of the iceberg", and Princeton professor Aaron Friedberg told Reuters he expects the episode to result in tighter rules rather than a change of course.

Why it mattersWhatever happens to Huang personally, the direction of US policy is toward stricter control of who gets advanced AI hardware and where it is hosted. Companies that buy, resell or run Nvidia systems with any link to China, or to markets seen as diversion routes, should treat export compliance as a board-level risk, and a new Congress with Democratic majorities could turn pending bills into law during 2027.

Wall Street claws back a steep early slide to close only modestly lower, but Nvidia still falls nearly 4% and Anthropic ends just under its $83 IPO price, ABC News reports

US stocks recovered most of a sharp morning drop on Wednesday, ABC News reports. Shortly after the open the Dow was down more than 1,000 points and the Nasdaq more than 4%, but by the close the Dow had lost 166 points, or 0.3%, the S&P 500 0.2% and the Nasdaq 0.6%. ABC describes the swings as part of a turbulent stretch for markets, which it links to Jensen Huang's earlier resignation from Nvidia and to Anthropic's initial public offering.

AI hardware names did not share in the recovery. According to ABC, Nvidia lost nearly 4% on Wednesday, adding to its losses from the day before, while Oracle and Sandisk each fell 5% and Micron dropped 4%. Anthropic moved the other way after a weak start, finishing up 1% at about $82.79 a share, which ABC notes is still slightly below the $83 price at which it went public. The rebound came two days after Huang's reinstatement as Nvidia's chief executive had lifted markets.

Why it mattersThe headline indexes are calming faster than AI hardware stocks, which suggests investors are separating the wider market from the AI trade rather than abandoning equities. For companies whose plans depend on AI suppliers' share prices – vendor financing, stock-funded deals, or the valuation of AI holdings – the volatility in chip and memory names is the number to watch, not the index close.

The FCC authorizes SpaceX to deploy up to 1 million Starmind satellites built to run AI computing in orbit, Fox Business reports

The US Federal Communications Commission has given SpaceX permission to put up to 1 million satellites into orbit as part of Starmind, a planned network of solar-powered computing modules meant to work as AI data centers in space, Fox Business reports, with contributions from Reuters. FCC Chairman Brendan Carr announced the decision on X on Wednesday, alongside a graphic promoting data centers in space. In a statement, Carr said "America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence," and an FCC release he shared said the system is consistent with the agency's orbital debris rules. The FCC also approved SpaceX's requests for additional communications to back up its optical network.

The approval is a ceiling, not a launch schedule. Fox Business notes that only 17,000 satellites are in orbit today and that it is unclear whether SpaceX will ever fly the full number; operators often seek approval for more than they deploy. For comparison, SpaceX asked for approval for 42,000 Starlink satellites, and that network now has just over 11,000 in space. Amazon's satellite unit Leo and other critics have argued that SpaceX is unlikely to launch all the Starmind satellites and could take centuries to do so, a criticism Carr rejected in March. According to the report, the plan depends heavily on Starship bringing launch costs down. Elon Musk thanked Carr on X and welcomed the decision.

Why it mattersPower, land and cooling are now the main brakes on AI data center growth, and this decision clears a constellation designed to move part of that computing off the ground. Even if only a small share is ever launched, the decision creates a new option in long-range compute planning and a new arena for competition between SpaceX, Amazon and others over spectrum and orbital space.

A bipartisan House bill would make the largest AI developers report how far their models are building the next generation on their own, under a new Commerce Department office, The National News Desk reports

Representatives Josh Gottheimer, a New Jersey Democrat, and Nathaniel Moran, a Texas Republican, introduced a bill this week to give Washington visibility into how leading AI labs use automation to develop their models, The National News Desk reports. Covered companies would have to report how much of their development work runs autonomously, how often people review it, and how much AI speeds up the creation of new models. The rules would apply to firms training models that meet certain computing benchmarks and spend $100 million on that training over a year. Those developers would also publish summaries of their safety and oversight work and tell the Commerce Department about major breakthroughs.

The bill does not ban automated AI research, as some lawmakers have proposed. Instead it would set up a new Commerce Department office to track progress and report to Congress, without the power to stop development. Gottheimer wrote on social media that "Both Congress and the public need to know." The report notes that Meta, Anthropic and OpenAI have disclosed cases of models acting beyond their instructions, and that Democrats taking control of both chambers plan a wide range of AI bills next year, although neither party has settled on a comprehensive approach and the White House prefers lighter regulation.

Why it mattersThis is a disclosure regime, not a ban, which makes it more likely to attract support across parties. If it advances, the biggest model developers would have to measure and report how much of their own R&D is done by AI, and those reports would give enterprise buyers, insurers and regulators a new view into how quickly the models they depend on are changing.

Amazon and Meta are reportedly in talks to let Meta's Muse AI shopping agent buy directly from Amazon's store, two months after Amazon blocked it, TechCrunch reports, citing Fortune

Amazon may be rethinking its block on Muse, the AI shopping agent built by Meta. TechCrunch, citing a Wednesday report from Fortune that relies on people familiar with the discussions, says the two companies have held multiple rounds of talks about a deal that would let Muse connect directly to Amazon's store. That would be a sharp turn from two months ago, when Muse users found they could not complete purchases on Amazon and ran into an error at checkout.

Two questions are reportedly on the table: how search results would be presented and how advertising would be handled. According to the reports, Amazon's concern is the effect that outside shopping agents could have on its advertising business and on its direct relationship with shoppers; in October, Amazon chief executive Andy Jassy argued that few customers were buying through such agents. Fortune reported that any agreement would need approval from the chief executives of both companies. Neither Meta nor Amazon had answered TechCrunch's requests for comment.

Why it mattersIf the largest US online store opens its checkout to a rival's AI agent, agent-led shopping stops being an experiment and becomes a sales channel. The terms being negotiated – who controls how results are ranked and who keeps the advertising revenue – are likely to become a template for how retailers, brands and platforms split value when software, not people, does the browsing.

India's IT Secretary S Krishnan says the government's AI regulation papers are in their final stages, at the launch of Chariot-1, a home-grown model covering English and 10 Indian languages, ANI reports

S Krishnan, secretary of India's Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY), said on Wednesday that the government's planned AI regulation papers are nearly finished and will be published soon, ANI reports. "It's in the final stages. We'll bring it out shortly," he said, without giving a date or details of what the papers will contain.

Krishnan was speaking at the launch of the Chariot-1 foundation models, which ANI says were built from scratch in India by Chariot Artificial Intelligence Pvt Ltd, one of the first four teams picked under the IndiaAI Mission to develop sovereign foundation models. The models work in English and 10 Indian languages. Co-founder Parth Sarthi said they bring text generation, text-to-speech and speech recognition together in a single architecture, and that the IndiaAI Mission helped the team get access to GPUs and infrastructure. ANI reports that the model achieved over 97% verbalisation accuracy on the Async Text-to-Speech Pronunciation Benchmark, and Krishnan said the launch vindicated the mission's faith in young Indian talent.

Why it mattersIndia is close to setting out how it intends to govern AI, while funding domestic models in parallel. Companies that build or deploy AI in India should expect formal guidance soon, and should note that the government is backing multilingual, voice-capable models that could become credible alternatives to foreign providers for customer-facing services.
