Microsoft offers voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 employees, more than a quarter of its global workforce, as it works to contain costs and fund AI spending

Microsoft said on Monday that it is opening a time-limited, voluntary exit program to roughly 60,000 staff, according to Reuters, which notes that Business Insider first reported the plan. Reuters says the aim is to keep costs in check and help pay for the company's AI investments. According to Microsoft, the offer covers employees at level 70 and below in the United States, Canada, India and the United Kingdom. A person familiar with the matter told Reuters that the eligible groups include AI experiences, Copilot, finance, human resources, Xbox, and Windows + Devices, while marketing, the commercial business and security are reportedly left out. In a memo to staff, chief people officer Amy Coleman described the program as "a new choice to make that transition now with support."

The offer is much wider than the company's earlier moves this year. Reuters reports that Microsoft offered buyouts earlier in 2026 to about 7% of its US workforce, or roughly 9,000 people, and that in July it announced 4,800 job cuts, about 2.1% of its global staff. The company had 223,000 full-time employees worldwide as of 30 June 2026, according to its annual filing, and Reuters says it is spending heavily on AI data centers and chips, with capital expenditure forecast at $175 billion for calendar 2026. Microsoft says the new program builds on its earlier voluntary retirement plan. It reports first-quarter results after the market closes on Wednesday.

Why it mattersThis is one of the clearest cases yet of a large technology company reshaping its workforce to pay for AI infrastructure. Buyouts let people leave by choice rather than through layoffs, but at this scale they can still thin out teams and slow work in the units involved, which reportedly include Copilot, Windows and Xbox. For customers and partners, the signal is that Microsoft is putting capital into data centers and chips first, and that the people they deal with in some product and support teams may change over the coming months.

Bank of America, Blackstone, Citi and Morgan Stanley line up $63 billion for a vehicle that will buy Broadcom AI chips and lease them to Anthropic, with deliveries from 2027

Four financial firms said on Monday that they have agreed a $63 billion transaction to pay for AI computing capacity built on Broadcom technology for Anthropic, according to a joint release issued by Broadcom on PR Newswire. Under the plan, a newly created special purpose vehicle will purchase Broadcom's XPU AI accelerators and networking equipment and rent them to Anthropic. The release places the deal inside a compute capacity agreement Broadcom had already announced, and says deliveries are expected to start in calendar 2027.

The money comes in layers. Bank of America, Citi and Morgan Stanley are arranging the first lien financing, Blackstone is the only provider of the committed part of the second lien, and Broadcom is giving the vehicle residual value support equal to the first lien amount, which means the chipmaker is backing the future value of the hardware. The release does not disclose interest rates, the length of the lease or how much computing power is involved. Broadcom chief executive Hock Tan said demand for AI compute "continues to grow at an extraordinary pace." Bloomberg notes that it reported earlier this month that the group was gathering roughly $60 billion of AI chip financing, and that Nvidia announced a partnership in August with six large financial firms, Blackstone among them, to mobilize more than $500 billion for AI.

Why it mattersThis is one of the largest attempts so far to turn AI chips into a financeable, leasable asset. Instead of buying hardware outright, an AI lab rents it from a vehicle funded by banks and private credit, with the chipmaker standing behind the hardware's value. That spreads the cost of the build-out across lenders and suppliers, which can speed up capacity, but it also ties banks, Blackstone and Broadcom more closely to how much the chips are worth in a few years and to Anthropic's ability to keep paying.

Nvidia commits $3 billion to Meta's share offering and widens their work on the Muse AI assistant, with Meta planning to deploy more Nvidia infrastructure, including Vera Rubin

Nvidia said early on Tuesday that it will put $3 billion into Meta's equity offering, as part of a broader agreement to expand the two companies' work on Muse, Meta's AI assistant, and the Muse family of models, according to a release published by Nvidia. The companies say they will tune their joint workflows to get more performance out of Muse in both training and inference and to lower total cost of ownership. Meta intends to roll out more Nvidia hardware for its next generation of AI agents, including Vera Rubin and Vera CPUs, along with Spectrum-X Ethernet networking and BlueField-4. The two are also considering further work on Nvidia's OpenShell reference architecture, which includes OpenShell for deploying agents safely and Sentry for monitoring how they behave. Meta chief executive Mark Zuckerberg said, "Muse is how we bring personal superintelligence to everyone."

The release does not give a share price or other terms, and its cautionary language notes that the investment has not yet closed. The commitment lands a day after Meta began raising money for its AI build-out: Reuters reported on Tuesday that Meta plans to raise up to a record $95 billion, and Financial News notes that only $35 billion of that was launched on Monday, with the remaining $60 billion at-the-market program not expected to start before the first quarter of 2027. Citing Reuters, Financial News also reported that Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom had expressed interest in buying up to $5 billion of the Class A common stock on offer. Nvidia's release does not say how its $3 billion relates to that earlier indication.

Why it mattersA chip supplier is now putting its own money into a customer's fundraising, at the same time as that customer promises to buy more of its hardware. This kind of circular arrangement helps Meta fund data centers without leaning only on debt, and gives Nvidia a firmer hold on one of its largest buyers. It also means that the fortunes of AI suppliers and AI spenders are becoming more tightly linked, which matters for anyone assessing how durable current AI infrastructure demand really is.

OpenAI releases GPT-6.1 Terra, a faster model for computer work priced at $1 per million input tokens, half the API price of GPT-6.1 Sol

OpenAI on Monday released GPT-6.1 Terra, which it presents as a quick, efficient model for visual reasoning, operating software on a computer and clearer writing. It succeeds GPT-5.6 Terra as the company's lower-cost option. According to OpenAI, the model is available now to Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users in ChatGPT Work and in Codex, and to developers through the API as gpt-6.1-terra. Standard API pricing is $1 per million input tokens, $0.05 per million cached input tokens and $5 per million output tokens, which OpenAI says is 50% lower than both GPT-5.6 Terra and its larger GPT-6.1 Sol model.

On OpenAI's own benchmarks, Terra comes close to the company's bigger models on many tasks for much less money. OpenAI says it completes tasks more than three times faster than GPT-6 Astra and GPT-6.1 Sol, and that at maximum reasoning effort it solves 50.3% of tasks on the Terminal-Bench 4.0 test. On an internal test built to provoke mistakes, the company reports that the error rate at low reasoning effort dropped to 16.7% from 53.6% for GPT-5.6 Terra, and reached 4.3% at maximum effort. OpenAI also says Terra meets the "Critical" threshold for cybersecurity under its Preparedness Framework and carries over the safeguards developed for GPT-6 Astra. For the most demanding coding work, the company still recommends Sol and Astra. All of these results are OpenAI's own figures.

Why it mattersFor companies running AI agents that click through software, fill in forms or handle IT tasks, cost and speed per completed task matter more than top benchmark scores. A model that OpenAI says is half the API price of Sol and finishes tasks several times faster changes the business case for automating routine back-office work. The "Critical" cybersecurity rating is the other side of the same capability: a model good at operating systems can also be misused against them, so access and oversight need as much attention as savings.

Cybersecurity & Infrastructure Operations & Enterprise

IBM gives AI agents their own verified identities and credentials, and adds tools to find, sandbox and switch off agents running across Amazon, Microsoft and Google clouds

IBM used its TechXchange conference in Atlanta on Tuesday to launch a set of controls for companies that are running large numbers of AI agents, according to a release on PR Newswire. The core pieces, both generally available, are HashiCorp Vault 2.2, which gives each agent its own secured account, renews its credentials automatically and adds post-quantum encryption, and Agent Identity, which records who owns each agent, where it is in its lifecycle and what it is allowed to do, so that access can be kept to a minimum and withdrawn in real time. IBM says the identity tools also work with Nvidia's OpenShell secure runtime software.

IBM is also extending watsonx Orchestrate. Its evaluation features, which test agents for quality, accuracy and compliance before launch and track cost by model and token use, are generally available, while features that discover agents built in Amazon Bedrock, Microsoft Foundry and Google Gemini, and that run agents in isolated sandboxes with a kill switch, are in tech preview. The release cites Gartner forecasts that the average Fortune 500 company will be using more than 150,000 AI agents by 2028, and that only 13% of organizations think they have the right agent governance in place. IBM software executive Dinesh Nirmal said the leaders in enterprise AI are "changing how their business runs, not simply adding more AI to it."

Why it mattersAs companies let AI agents log in to systems, move data and take actions, the hard question becomes who, or what, is acting and with what permission. IBM is treating agents like employees or service accounts that need an owner, credentials and the ability to be shut off, and is offering to track agents built on rival clouds. For security and risk teams, this is a sign that agent identity is becoming a standard control area, much as user identity did a decade ago.

OpenAI says its models have disproved a widely used assumption in computer science and proved major open problems in math and physics, and asks outside experts to check the work

OpenAI said on Monday that its models have produced three results that would settle long-standing open questions if they hold up, and that it has handed them to outside researchers rather than presenting them as confirmed. According to OpenAI, the claims are a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, an assumption about how hard certain logic problems are that the company says has been used in hundreds of papers; a proof of the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which it says would also resolve the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures; and a rigorous proof that certain four-dimensional quantum field theories exist, including pure Yang–Mills theory, a subproblem of a Clay Millennium Prize problem. OpenAI does not say which models were involved. It reports that the computer science disproof was found on 16 October and checked in the Lean proof language a day later.

Each field is being handled differently. In computer science, Russell Impagliazzo and Ramamohan Paturi of UC San Diego and Ryan Williams of MIT are examining the claim, and the proof and its Lean version have been published on GitHub; The Next Web reports that the three say their role is not an endorsement. In physics, a group led by Nobel laureate David Gross has chosen to hold back the AI material and write up the work itself, while the mathematics output has been released for researchers to study. OpenAI says collaborators sign no non-disclosure agreements and are not paid, that it agreed to the computer scientists' requests to release all raw data and fund a conference, and that it is giving $5 million to the Simons Foundation for events on AI-produced results. The company describes the manuscripts as "best understood as a form of raw research data."

Why it mattersIf even one of these claims survives expert review, it would mark a shift from AI that helps researchers to AI that produces frontier results on its own. That is not yet established: the results are machine-checked in places but not independently confirmed, and the experts involved have been careful not to vouch for them. For research-heavy businesses, the more immediate lesson is in the process OpenAI has set up, with outside reviewers, public data and no NDAs, which offers a template for how AI-generated findings might be checked and credited before anyone relies on them.

Asian chip stocks jump after Anthropic's IPO filing, with Korea's Kospi up 4.1% in morning trade and SK hynix up 7%

Semiconductor and AI-linked shares across Asia rose sharply on Tuesday morning, a day after Anthropic filed for its Nasdaq listing. In Seoul, the Kospi index was 4.1% higher at 5,689.23 at about 10:15 a.m. local time, according to Aju Press. Memory makers led the move: SK hynix gained 7% and Samsung Electronics 5.1%, while SK Square rose 10.8%. Aju Press reports that foreign investors were net buyers of 409.1 billion won (US$309 million) of Kospi shares by that point, and that suppliers of chip substrates, circuit boards and power equipment also climbed. Elice, an AI cloud infrastructure company that listed on the Kosdaq last week, rose 21.2%.

The gains spread beyond Korea. Investing.com reports that Advantest rose nearly 10% in Tokyo, Tokyo Electron gained 3% and SoftBank Group more than 6%, while Chinese chipmakers SMIC and Hua Hong moved up more modestly in Hong Kong. Aju Press puts Japan's Nikkei 225 up 2.1%. Investing.com links the rally to renewed confidence in AI demand after Anthropic's filing, which showed fast revenue growth alongside heavy losses, and says Wall Street had already pushed Nvidia up 2.4% and CoreWeave nearly 6% on Monday. It adds that investors are waiting for results from large US technology companies this week for evidence that AI spending is paying off.

Why it mattersAnthropic's filing gave investors a detailed look at the finances of a leading AI lab, and markets read it as support for continued spending on chips, memory and data center equipment. That matters most for Korea and Japan, where a few suppliers such as SK hynix, Samsung and Advantest carry much of the market's AI exposure. One day's rally does not settle the question of AI returns, and this week's US earnings could move these stocks just as quickly in the other direction, but it shows how closely Asian supply chains are now tied to news from a handful of US AI companies.

Doosan commits 399.2 billion won to a new South Korean line making circuit board material for the optical modules inside AI data centers

South Korea's Doosan signed an investment agreement on Tuesday with North Jeolla Province and the city of Gimje for a dedicated production line for copper-clad laminate (CCL) used in optical modules, according to The Herald Business. CCL is the base material for printed circuit boards, and the grade used in optical modules has to keep signal loss very low so data can move at very high speeds between servers. Doosan already supplies this material for modules running at 800 gigabits per second and faster. The new plant will cover about 33,000 square meters next to the company's existing flexible CCL factory in Gimje.

The 399.2 billion won facility is part of a broader plan of about 970 billion won ($733 million) that Doosan announced in September, the paper reports. Construction is due to finish by 2028, with new lines starting up one after another from the second half of that year, and the company expects to hire more than 200 people. Doosan's domestic lines are already running above full capacity. Kim In-wook, head of Doosan's Electronics Business Group, said demand for this material is surging as AI data centers expand, and that the company will deliver the volumes its customers need on time.

Why it mattersThe AI build-out is not only about GPUs and memory. Every large AI cluster needs fast optical links between racks, and those links depend on specialized board materials from a small number of suppliers. When one of them says its lines are already over capacity and new output only begins in the second half of 2028, it signals that this part of the supply chain could stay tight for the next two years and affect lead times and prices for networking equipment.
