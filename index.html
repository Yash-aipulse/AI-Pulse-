OpenAI is reportedly close to raising up to $30 billion at a $1.25 trillion pre-money valuation, ahead of a possible IPO

OpenAI is working on a new private funding round of up to $30 billion at a pre-money valuation of $1.25 trillion, according to a CNBC report cited by TechCrunch on 22 October. TechCrunch says the round is being led by Coatue and investors from the United Arab Emirates, and that CNBC reported the UAE money could be tied to an agreement giving OpenAI access to more computing capacity in the country. The terms are still under negotiation and could change. OpenAI declined to comment.

The reported valuation sits below the $1.4 trillion figure in a Bloomberg report about a month earlier, which also described a $30 billion raise. It is still well above OpenAI's March round, which valued the company at $852 billion after the new money. TechCrunch notes that a valuation above $1 trillion would make OpenAI the most valuable private company on record. OpenAI filed confidential IPO paperwork in June. Rival Anthropic, which raised $65 billion at a $965 billion valuation in May and also filed confidentially in June, is seeking a valuation of $1.5 trillion to $2 trillion for its listing, according to CNBC.

Why it mattersThe two leading US labs are now being priced as some of the largest companies in the world before either has listed, and both are tying fundraising to access to computing power. For enterprise buyers, that scale of capital points to continued heavy spending on models and infrastructure, but also to strong pressure on both companies to grow revenue, which will show up in pricing, bundling and multi-year contract terms. The gap between the reported $1.4 trillion and $1.25 trillion figures is a reminder that even these valuations are still being negotiated.

Venture Capital Policy & Regulation

DeepSeek has reportedly raised about $15 billion, two days after Washington ordered its AI out of federal civilian agencies

Chinese AI lab DeepSeek has raised about $15 billion, or roughly 100 billion yuan, according to a Bloomberg report cited by TechCrunch on 22 October. TechCrunch describes it as one of the largest funding rounds China has seen. Bloomberg's report lists battery maker CATL and Tencent among the potential investors, so the final investor list may not yet be settled. DeepSeek did not respond to TechCrunch's request for comment, and the company has not confirmed the figures.

The timing stands out. The reported raise comes two days after the US government ordered federal civilian agencies to remove DeepSeek's AI technology from their systems. The Hangzhou-based company is nonetheless increasing its spending on AI infrastructure and on open models aimed at both Chinese and overseas users. The round would follow a June raise of more than $7 billion and a July report that DeepSeek was seeking a further $1.5 billion at a valuation of around $71 billion. Founder Liang Wenfeng, who also co-founded the quantitative hedge fund High-Flyer, holds an estimated 81.6% of the Chinese operating entity, according to WireScreen data cited by TechCrunch.

Why it mattersUS restrictions are now limiting where DeepSeek's models can be used in government, but they have not slowed the money flowing into the company at home. For multinational buyers, this points to a lasting split: a well-funded Chinese open-model ecosystem that will keep improving, alongside growing legal and reputational limits on using it in US public-sector and regulated work. Vendor due diligence will increasingly need to ask which underlying models a product is built on, not just who sells it.

Pharma & Health Research & Science

Microsoft's MAI-DxO diagnosed 83% of complex simulated cases against 36% for generalist doctors, a study in Science reports

A study published in the journal Science on 22 October describes MAI-DxO, a Microsoft AI system that coordinates several AI agents to act like a panel of doctors: they question a simulated patient, order tests and debate a diagnosis. On complex cases drawn from the New England Journal of Medicine, it reached the correct diagnosis 83% of the time. Primary care doctors and general internists working alone with reference materials were correct 36% of the time. Specialists and physician teams, who would normally handle cases this hard, were not part of the comparison.

Microsoft tested the system with models from OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, Meta and DeepSeek. According to TechCrunch's reading of the paper, most of the improvement came from the underlying model, with the orchestration layer adding gains on top: with OpenAI's GPT-5.5, accuracy on complex cases rose from 72% to nearly 87%, while on emergency department cases both setups scored about 85%. Microsoft will next evaluate the system with Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, where consenting patients will use it alongside standard care, and is working with Mayo Clinic on a health care foundation model planned for release next year. No product launch date was given.

Why it mattersThe result adds peer-reviewed weight to the idea that AI can handle diagnostic reasoning on hard cases, and Microsoft is signaling that it wants to sell clinical decision support, not just dictation and imaging tools. The caveats are important for anyone making decisions on it: the test was a simulation, the comparison excluded specialists, and the gains were small on emergency cases. The real test is the hospital evaluation that comes next.

Operations & Enterprise

Uber extends OpenAI's ChatGPT Work to its roughly 30,000 employees after a 1,000-person pilot

Uber and OpenAI announced a wider partnership on 22 October, and Uber told TechCrunch that its roughly 30,000 employees worldwide will now have access to ChatGPT Work, OpenAI's enterprise agent product launched in July. The rollout follows a pilot earlier this year with 1,000 staff drawn from finance, operations, product and other teams. According to Uber's vice president of engineering, Sumanth Sukumar, about 80% of the early users were not engineers.

Uber has also tried a more hands-on approach. Earlier in 2026 it placed 30 AI engineers with teams across the company for two weeks to automate parts of their work, groups Sukumar calls "AI pods." Uber did not say what the rollout costs. It plans to monitor how employees use the tool and use those findings to guide its wider AI deployment, and says OpenAI worked closely with it on the integration.

Why it mattersA company-wide rollout at a large employer, after a pilot dominated by non-engineers, is a useful reference point for leaders deciding whether AI agents belong only with technical teams. It also shows the competition among AI labs for enterprise seats becoming more direct, which gives buyers room to negotiate. The missing cost figure is a reminder that the business case for such deployments is still mostly private.

Pharma & Health Operations & Enterprise

Boehringer Ingelheim and Microsoft will test AI "digital patient twins" for clinical trials, starting with lung health

German drugmaker Boehringer Ingelheim and Microsoft announced a partnership on 22 October to study whether AI can make clinical development faster and more patient-centered. The centerpiece is research into "digital patient twins," which the companies describe as computer models of individual patients built from clinical and other data to simulate how a disease progresses. The work covers disease-progression and outcome modeling and trial simulation, and will test whether synthetic or hybrid control arms built from existing data could reduce the number of patients who need to be enrolled in control groups.

The companies will assess feasibility through modeling, prototypes and evidence generation across several disease areas, beginning with lung health. No timetable or milestones were given. The partnership also extends AI use across Boehringer's workforce, building on what the companies call one of the largest Microsoft Copilot deployments in the pharmaceutical industry, and covers security, data center modernization and possible joint work in animal health. Boehringer's chief medical officer, Lykke Hinsch Gylvin, said scientific quality, patient safety and regulatory requirements "must always be upheld."

Why it mattersRecruiting patients, particularly for control groups, is one of the slowest and most expensive parts of drug development. If synthetic or hybrid control arms prove acceptable to regulators, trial timelines and costs could change considerably. The project is still at the feasibility stage, so the practical signal for now is that large drugmakers are moving AI from office productivity into the design of trials themselves.

Pharma & Health Venture Capital

Former OpenAI science lead Kevin Weil launches Warp Sciences with $215 million to automate drug-discovery labs with robots

Kevin Weil, who previously led science work at OpenAI, announced on 22 October that his new company, Warp Sciences, has raised $215 million at an $865 million valuation, according to TechCrunch. Lightspeed led the round, with IVP, Premji Invest and Lux Capital also taking part.

Warp Sciences plans to use robots to make and test the promising compounds that researchers identify, a labor-heavy step in drug discovery. Human chemists wear cameras and sensor gloves so the robots can learn lab tasks from them, and the goal is robotic labs that run around the clock. TechCrunch notes that biotech companies have long outsourced this kind of molecule synthesis to contractors, including China's WuXi AppTec, and Weil wants to bring the work back to the US and reduce the industry's exposure to geopolitical supply-chain risk.

Why it mattersAI has made it faster to propose drug candidates, which shifts the bottleneck to making and testing them in the lab. A well-funded US company aiming to automate that step, at a time of political pressure on Chinese contract research providers, gives drugmakers a possible alternative supplier. It is an early-stage company, so the practical question for buyers is how quickly it can deliver at scale.

Operations & Enterprise

Anthropic, OpenAI, Hugging Face and Alibaba hand four agent tools to the Agentic AI Foundation, which now hosts 10 open projects

The Agentic AI Foundation, a Linux Foundation body that oversees open standards for AI agents, announced four new projects on 22 October in San Jose, timed with its AGNTCon + MCPCon North America conference. Anthropic contributed Agent Skills, a format for packaging instructions, scripts and resources that agents load when needed. OpenAI contributed Open Responses, a specification based on its Responses API that lets developers write a request once and run it across different model providers. Hugging Face contributed OpenEnv, a framework for building reinforcement learning environments, and Alibaba contributed OpenSandbox, a platform for running AI agents and their code inside sandboxes.

With these additions the foundation hosts 10 projects, up from three when it launched in December 2025 with the Model Context Protocol (MCP), goose and AGENTS.md. The release did not give a membership count. The foundation describes its role as a neutral home for the standards and infrastructure that let agents work across platforms.

Why it mattersRival labs placing core agent tools under shared, neutral governance lowers the risk that companies get locked into one provider's way of building agents. An open interface for calling models from several providers, and a common format for agent skills, make it easier to switch vendors or run more than one. For enterprise architects, these are becoming the standards to build against.

Editor's note
Capital, clinical evidence and shared standards all moved in the same direction today: AI is being priced, tested and governed as core infrastructure.

OpenAI's reported round of up to $30 billion at a $1.25 trillion pre-money valuation, and DeepSeek's reported raise of about $15 billion despite US curbs, show investors still betting on the largest model builders on both sides of the US-China divide. Microsoft's Science study, in which its MAI-DxO system diagnosed 83% of complex simulated cases against 36% for generalist doctors, and Warp Sciences' $215 million plan to automate drug-discovery labs, show AI moving into the core work of medicine and drug development, alongside Boehringer Ingelheim's patient twin research. Inside companies, Uber's rollout to roughly 30,000 employees and the decision by rival labs to place agent tools under the neutral Agentic AI Foundation point to agents becoming standard workplace infrastructure. For executives, the thread is discipline: negotiate AI contracts with the providers' funding needs in mind, ask for real-world evidence before clinical or scientific use, and build on open standards so that no single vendor controls the stack.
