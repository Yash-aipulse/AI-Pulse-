Amazon agrees to acquire the technology behind AI startup Decart and hire its co-founders and engineers, in a deal Bloomberg sources put at $7.5 billion, PYMNTS reports

Two days after reports that talks were at an advanced stage, Amazon has confirmed the Decart deal. According to PYMNTS, Amazon said in a news release on Monday, 23 November, that it will acquire the technology behind the AI startup and bring its co-founders, Dean Leitersdorf, Moshe Shalev and Orian Leitersdorf, to Amazon along with Decart's researchers and engineers. Amazon added that it began working with Decart last year. In the release, Peter DeSantis, Amazon's senior vice president for foundational AI models, custom silicon and quantum computing, said "every leap forward in AI starts with getting more out of every chip."

Amazon did not disclose what it is paying. PYMNTS reports that Bloomberg News, citing people familiar with the matter, puts the price at $7.5 billion, the same figure the Financial Times gave in a weekend report that said the deal was ready to close. The Bloomberg report also says the tie-up helps Amazon Web Services compete more effectively with Nvidia.

Why it mattersThe deal shows Amazon is still willing to spend heavily to make its own chips more attractive. Software that gets more performance out of each chip is what decides whether customers can run AI work on Amazon's silicon instead of Nvidia's. For enterprise buyers, that means a more credible second option on price and supply over the next one to two years, but also a reason to check how portable their AI workloads really are.

Wall Street is on course to win back more than half of last week's AI-driven losses as Nvidia jumps 31.2% on Jensen Huang's return, AP reports at midday Monday

According to the Associated Press, US stocks rallied hard on Monday, 23 November, after Nvidia brought Jensen Huang back as chief executive. As of noon Eastern time, AP had the S&P 500 up 4.9%, the Nasdaq composite up 6.8% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average up 1,549 points, or 3.2%, enough to recover more than half of last week's slide. AP said the S&P 500 was on track for its best day since April 2025. Nvidia was up 31.2%, but AP noted the stock was still below where it stood before the controversy around Huang first broke, after a drop of nearly 23% last week.

The gains spread well beyond Nvidia. AP reported CoreWeave up 21%, Oracle up 12.5% after the company said it had all the funding it needs for its OpenAI deal, GE Vernova up 8.7% and Caterpillar up 7.3%. President Donald Trump told CNBC, "I believe my intervention was very important" in the board's decision, and said his administration had assured Nvidia there were no national security concerns, AP reports. AP adds that Nvidia's board is still looking into whether Huang was targeted by a foreign spy. All market figures here are midday readings, not closing levels.

Why it mattersOne strong session does not answer the questions that pushed US stocks into a correction, but it shows how much of the AI trade now hangs on confidence in a handful of companies and leaders. AP cites Barclays analysts who expect AI worries to make the build-out slower and more selective rather than stop it. That is a sensible planning assumption for finance teams and buyers of AI infrastructure: spending continues, but with more scrutiny of each project.

Seoul's Kospi climbs 5.84% and Tokyo's Nikkei 3.05% in early Tuesday trade as Asia picks up Wall Street's rally on Huang's return to Nvidia, AFP reports

Asia's chip-heavy markets took the baton from New York on Tuesday, 24 November. According to AFP, at around 0010 GMT South Korea's Kospi was 5.84% higher at 4983.65 points, after briefly gaining more than 6%, and Japan's Nikkei 225 was up 3.05% at 58,159.74 points as Tokyo reopened after a public holiday. AFP adds that futures for the tech-heavy South Korean and Taiwanese markets had pointed to gains of at least 7% at the open.

The move reverses a weak Monday in Seoul. AFP says the Kospi closed 3.3% lower on 23 November, held back by Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, before Nvidia ended its leadership crisis by bringing Jensen Huang back as chief executive and president. Nvidia's shares then rose 31.48% to $165.05 on Monday, and SK hynix's US-traded shares gained more than 20%, AFP reports. The agency cautions that volatility remains high after last week's rout. The Asian figures here are early-session readings, not closing levels.

Why it mattersKorean and Japanese memory and equipment makers sit underneath almost every AI server, so their share prices are a fast read on whether investors still believe AI spending will hold. A fall of 3.3% one day and a gain of almost 6% the next in Seoul shows how tightly Asian supply-chain valuations now move with news about one US company. Finance and sourcing teams should plan for that volatility to continue rather than read a trend into any single session.

Washington adds Megaspeed, the Singapore data center operator accused of moving Nvidia AI chips to China, to the Commerce Department's Entity List, CBS News reports

According to CBS News, the Trump administration on Monday, 23 November, restricted Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that runs data centers in Southeast Asia, from buying advanced semiconductors. The Commerce Department placed it on the Entity List, which means it now needs a license to buy sensitive US technologies, including advanced chips. Megaspeed has been accused of passing Nvidia AI chips on to China, an accusation the company denies. CBS says it has asked Megaspeed for comment.

The listing is closely tied to the turmoil at Nvidia. CBS reports that Jensen Huang admitted earlier this month to an affair with a former Megaspeed executive whom US intelligence agencies believe was a Chinese intelligence asset. Huang resigned, and Nvidia's board reinstated him early on Monday, less than two weeks later. A joint Justice and Commerce Department statement last week, cited by CBS, said Megaspeed had been on Nvidia's internal "no sell" list since June 2025. Trump told CNBC he spoke with the board personally and gave it assurances on national security, CBS adds.

Why it mattersExport controls on AI chips are now being enforced against the intermediaries in between, not only against buyers in China. A third-country data center operator can lose access to US chips overnight, and any company that rents compute from or sells hardware to such operators carries that risk too. Expect closer scrutiny of where advanced chips end up, and more questions from regulators about who your partners in Southeast Asia really serve.

Anthropic launches Claude Ship, AI agents that review and fix code changes and can approve routine ones under rules each team writes, TechCrunch reports

According to TechCrunch, Anthropic announced Claude Ship on Monday, 23 November, a set of AI agents meant to automate code reviews, fixes and the approval of routine code changes. It has three parts. Code Review is generally available. PR Steward, in beta, works on pull requests that are stuck on failing CI checks, merge conflicts or review feedback, and after every push it comments on what it changed and how to roll that back. Claude Approvals, also in beta, signs off changes against an approval policy that the team writes in natural language and keeps as a file in the repository; it can draw on Code Review findings or Anthropic's own classifiers.

The product is rolling out to Claude Team and Enterprise plans for repositories hosted on GitHub. Teams decide where it runs, choose between Quick, Standard and Deep review settings, and set which changes are escalated to a person. Anthropic suggests running approvals in observe-only mode first, and teams can begin with a single repository and set monthly spending limits. TechCrunch quotes Anthropic's blog as saying that on the Claude Code team, "Claude writes most of our code".

Why it mattersAI coding tools have so far produced code faster than people could review it. Claude Ship moves AI into the review and sign-off step itself, which is where engineering organizations keep their controls. That can speed up delivery, but it turns a written approval policy into a control that auditors, security teams and regulators will want to inspect, and it raises the question of who is accountable when an AI-approved change fails.

Oregon and Virginia sue SpaceX, accusing its Grok AI assistant of letting users create sexualized images of people without their consent, UPI reports

According to UPI, the attorneys general of Oregon and Virginia filed a lawsuit on Monday, 23 November, over Grok, the AI assistant built into the X platform. Both Grok and X are owned by SpaceX. The suit says Grok allowed users to create sexualized images of real people without their consent, and UPI reports that it includes thousands of such images that appear to show children. Oregon Attorney General Dan Rayfield accuses SpaceX of knowingly designing the assistant without safeguards against this kind of imagery, while Virginia Attorney General Jay Jones says the case seeks justice for Virginians whose data and images were not safe on X.

The case builds on research and events from earlier in the year. UPI cites an estimate by the Center for Countering Digital Harm that Grok created and posted about 3 million sexualized images between 29 December and 8 January, including about 23,000 that appeared to be of children. On 15 January, xAI, SpaceX's AI unit, said it would stop allowing the platform to digitally undress real people. The states now argue in court that the product was released without adequate protections in the first place.

Why it mattersThis is state law enforcement going after an AI product's design, not just individual users' misuse. If the claims succeed, companies that build or embed image-generation tools could be held responsible for the safeguards they did or did not ship. Any business deploying generative AI to customers or staff should expect regulators to ask what guardrails were in place on day one, and to treat later fixes as too late.

Robotics start-up Embo raises a $110 million seed round led by Andreessen Horowitz to build one AI model that can run humanoid robots

Embo announced on Monday, 23 November, that it has raised a $110 million seed round led by Andreessen Horowitz. Khosla Ventures, DST Global and NVentures, Nvidia's venture arm, also took part, along with other strategic investors the company did not name. Embo says it is building the brain for intelligent robots: a single model trained from scratch on internet-scale data, then refined on experience collected by its own fleet of humanoid robots, and finally improved through reinforcement learning.

The company did not disclose a valuation or a timeline for products or customers, saying only that it will share progress as it goes. Its announcement says team members created research systems including Dreamer, VideoGPT and Diffusion Forcing, have worked at Google DeepMind, Meta Superintelligence Labs, Nvidia Cosmos, UC Berkeley and MIT, and have previously built infrastructure to train multimodal models across 70,000 GPUs.

Why it mattersA seed round of this size, with no product yet announced, shows investors are now paying for proven research teams that promise general-purpose robot intelligence rather than robots programmed task by task. For manufacturers and logistics operators, it signals more capital flowing into robot software, while commercial deployment at scale remains unproven.
