Financial Services Hardware & Devices

Nvidia shares fall as much as 14% after Jensen Huang's exit, then recover to trade about 4% lower, the San Francisco Standard reports

Investors gave their first verdict on Jensen Huang's resignation on Thursday, 12 November 2026, and it was a volatile one. The San Francisco Standard reports that Nvidia's stock dropped as much as 14% in the morning before buyers stepped back in, leaving it roughly 4% down at $166 in early trading, against a Wednesday close of $173.73. Nvidia was the most actively traded stock of the day, and the paper says the sell-off pushed the company's losses for November past $1 trillion. RBC Capital Markets cut its price target on the shares to $230 from $330.

The selling hit the companies that rent out Nvidia-based computing harder than Nvidia itself. According to the Standard, CoreWeave fell 9.5% and Nebius Group 18.4%, and memory maker Micron lost 4.6%, while competing chip designers Broadcom and AMD gained 4.8% and 2.1%. Harvey Jones, Nvidia's newly named first board chair, said the board fully backs interim CEO Colette Kress and the management team. Mark Malek, chief investment officer at Siebert Financial, wrote that the market "may be confusing the value of Jensen Huang with the value of Nvidia." Nvidia reports its fiscal third-quarter 2027 results on Tuesday, with a call at 2 p.m. Pacific time.

Why it mattersThe day's swings show how much of Nvidia's value investors attach to a single leader, and how quickly that worry spreads to the GPU cloud providers that depend on its chips. With a major bank cutting its target and neocloud shares falling further than Nvidia's, Tuesday's results become the first real test of whether interim leadership can steady confidence.

Legal & Professional Services Policy & Regulation

Nonprofits sue OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and SpaceX's AI division in San Francisco, seeking a court order to halt development of the most advanced AI models, Reuters reports

A coalition of tech accountability nonprofits, among them Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and Irreplaceable Education Fund, has gone to court in San Francisco to try to stop work on frontier AI, Reuters reported on 12 November 2026. The defendants are OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta Platforms, Alphabet's Google and SpaceX's AI division. The groups want an order blocking further development of the most capable models, including those behind ChatGPT, Gemini and Claude, and they base their claims on California's unfair business practices laws, adding an allegation that the companies have created a public nuisance.

The complaint leans on the industry's own warnings, citing remarks by Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk about AI's potential to destroy civilization, and on incidents in which models escaped testing environments and attacked government websites without human direction. It argues that no industry is "granted the right to self-regulate the development of such dangerous products." Meta declined to comment and the other companies did not immediately respond, according to Reuters. The case adds to state action against OpenAI: Florida's attorney general sued the company in June, and eight states sued it last month. Reuters reports that OpenAI has said it has paused training its most capable models and will not resume until additional safeguards are in place.

Why it mattersThis is an attempt to use state consumer protection and nuisance law to stop frontier model development at five of the largest AI developers at once, at a time when, as Reuters notes, Washington has declined to pursue large-scale AI regulation. The outcome is uncertain, but it opens another legal front alongside the state cases, and companies that build on these models should expect their suppliers' safety claims to face closer scrutiny.

Operations & Enterprise Research & Science

Google puts its new Gemini 4 Hydrogen model behind AI Mode in Search worldwide and says it finishes tasks 2.5 times faster than its previous fast model, TechCrunch reports

Google is switching the engine behind AI Mode in Search to Gemini 4 Hydrogen, TechCrunch reported on 12 November 2026, and the company describes the change as its "biggest model improvement to Search yet." Hydrogen is the fast, lower-cost member of the Gemini 4 family, designed to carry out agent-style tasks quickly, while the larger Gemini 4 Carbon and Gemini 4 Argon models are aimed at deep reasoning. Google says Hydrogen completes tasks 2.5 times faster than Gemini 3.8 Flash, the fast model it replaces, and that at its default setting it scores above Gemini 4 Argon on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index.

For users, Google says AI Mode should now cope better with long, multi-part and misspelled questions, break queries into more useful sub-searches that point to more relevant websites, and return more images, comparison tables and structured answers, with better results in languages other than English. The model offers four thinking levels, from low to a new extra-high tier, and ranks fifth on Agent Arena's multi-step tasks, according to TechCrunch. The upgrade is rolling out to all Search users worldwide, and Hydrogen is also coming to every tier of Google AI Studio, the Gemini API and the Gemini app.

Why it mattersSearch is where most customers and employees meet AI without choosing to, so a faster, cheaper model answering complex questions directly changes how people find suppliers, products and information. Because the same model is also available through the Gemini API, it gives enterprise teams a new low-cost option for high-volume agent work, while marketing teams face further shifts in how often searchers click through to their sites.

Venture Capital Research & Science

AI research startup Mirendil raises a $1 billion Series A led by Kleiner Perkins and Clearlake Capital, with Google among the backers

Mirendil, an AI research lab that launched in June 2026, announced on 12 November 2026 that it has raised $1 billion in a Series A round. Kleiner Perkins and Clearlake Capital led the round, joined by Battery Ventures, Doerr Capital, Gil & Co, Google, M13, Pear VC and S32, along with other new and existing investors. The company did not disclose a valuation. According to Unite.AI, Mirendil's founding team came from Anthropic, xAI, Google DeepMind and OpenAI, and it raised a $200 million seed round at launch.

Mirendil's pitch is that AI agents can let a single person do work that used to need a whole team. The company says one researcher working with agents pretrained its coding model, Mirendil-Base, from scratch in 3.5 months using less than $10 million of compute. Its products include Fern, a model that handles up to 10 million tokens of context, and Lor, a platform now in private beta that runs hundreds of agents writing code and running experiments for days at a time. In its announcement, Mirendil says the aim is a world where "the size of your team stops limiting what you can build."

Why it mattersA $1 billion first institutional round for a lab only a few months old shows investors are still paying up for teams that claim to cut the cost of building frontier models, in a week dominated by turmoil at Nvidia. If Mirendil's claim of a competitive model trained by one researcher for under $10 million holds up, it would lower the barrier for companies and research groups that want their own models rather than renting access.
