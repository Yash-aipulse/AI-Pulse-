Asian governments respond to Jensen Huang's exit from Nvidia, with South Korea saying the company has reaffirmed plans to supply 260,000 advanced GPUs, TechCrunch reports

Governments across Asia have spent the past week working out what Jensen Huang's departure means for them, with some seeking reassurance on chip supply and others pointing to their own investigations, according to a TechCrunch roundup published on Saturday, 14 November 2026. South Korea's Science Ministry said Nvidia had reaffirmed its plan to supply 260,000 advanced GPUs to the country and to keep working on joint AI projects, and a local media report cited by TechCrunch says the government's AI strategy council held an emergency meeting on Friday to weigh the impact. In Taiwan, Economy Minister Kung Ming-shin said the change was unlikely to make a significant difference to the growth of the AI industry, according to local media, and Taipei's city government said Nvidia's planned overseas headquarters is still on track. Japan's Industry Ministry said it would keep a close watch on Nvidia, and Deputy Chief Cabinet Secretary Masanao Ozaki said it was too early to judge the effect on the global AI industry while stressing Japan's commitment to a stable chip supply.

Singapore and China focused on the allegations behind the episode. Nvidia's board opened an independent investigation on 8 November after reports that Huang had been targeted by a Chinese spy operation; Huang has admitted an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive of Singapore-based Megaspeed. Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs said that in February 2026 police found insufficient evidence of offenses in a probe into suspected false declarations about server purchases, but warned Alice Huang and barred her from re-entering the country; its investigation into Megaspeed and six other people is continuing, and it said it had received limited help from US authorities. China's foreign ministry rejected the spy allegations as groundless on 10 November and on Friday repeated its opposition to export controls that disrupt supply chains. TechCrunch also points to a Reuters review that found more than $3 billion in Chinese AI computing projects relying on Nvidia's restricted processors now face uncertainty.

Why it mattersSeveral countries have built national AI plans around Nvidia supply, so the first question after a leadership shock is whether those commitments hold. South Korea's public confirmation is an early sign that Nvidia is protecting its large government-backed deals, while the open investigation in Singapore and China's stance on export controls keep compliance risk high for anyone sourcing AI computing in Southeast Asia.

A petition urging Nvidia's board to reinstate Jensen Huang passes 115,000 signatures, with Elon Musk among its backers, TechCrunch reports

An online petition asking Nvidia's board to bring back Jensen Huang had gathered more than 115,000 signatures by Saturday afternoon, 14 November 2026, TechCrunch reports. Citing the petition's website, TechCrunch says the signers include 5,965 current Nvidia employees, 1,108 former employees and 63,063 shareholders. Its best-known supporters are Tesla and SpaceX CEO Elon Musk, Replit CEO Amjad Masad and Epic Games CEO Tim Sweeney. The petition argues that Huang was removed without "meaningful due process" and makes three demands of the board: reinstate him while an independent investigation establishes the facts, make sure that investigation is fair, and tell the public more.

Huang stepped down on Thursday, several days after admitting a relationship with a chip executive whom US intelligence has identified as a suspected Chinese spy, and CFO Colette Kress is serving as interim CEO while Nvidia searches for a permanent successor. TechCrunch notes that Nvidia's shares fell 2% on Friday. Its report carries no comment from Nvidia on the petition, so it is not yet clear how, or whether, the board will respond to the pressure from its own staff and investors.

Why it mattersThe petition turns Nvidia's leadership crisis into an open governance contest. The board has to run its investigation and CEO search while thousands of employees and tens of thousands of shareholders argue in public that the process was rushed. For the many companies that plan around Nvidia's products and partnerships, a long dispute over who leads the company adds uncertainty at a time when its interim leadership is still settling in.

Tesla opens Cybercab pre-orders for personal use at $29,990 plus a $299 monthly fee, with unsupervised driving limited to geofenced areas, TechCrunch reports

Tesla has begun taking pre-orders from select customers who want to own a Cybercab, its gold-colored autonomous vehicle, for personal use, TechCrunch reported on Saturday, 14 November 2026. The car costs $29,990, and owners must also pay $299 a month to operate it. According to a post by reviewer Marques Brownlee cited by TechCrunch, buyers have to take delivery in one of a handful of Texas cities, and the car will run only within certain geofenced areas. Tesla's estimated delivery date is December 2026.

Tesla's Ashok Elluswamy said owners' Cybercabs will drive unsupervised in the same locations as Tesla's robotaxi service, serving only their owners. Elon Musk said the car "will be available nationwide, as we receive regulatory approval," and argued that the monthly fee is set too high and should match the roughly $115 a month Tesla charges for Full Self-Driving and connectivity on its other models. TechCrunch adds that Tesla plans to stop making the Cybertruck next year, after sales fell well short of projections, and to use the freed factory capacity to build more Cybercabs.

Why it mattersSelling an unsupervised autonomous car to private owners, rather than only running it as a fleet service, moves driverless technology a step closer to everyday use. The Texas-only delivery and geofenced operation show that regulatory approval and mapped service areas, not the vehicle itself, now set the pace, and the monthly operating fee points to a subscription model for autonomy that changes how the cost of owning a vehicle is calculated.

The top scorer in South Korea's tax accountant exam admits using AI to cheat, as police investigate and the government moves to suspend his qualification, AFP reports

The candidate who came first in both rounds of South Korea's national tax accountant exam, held in April and July, has admitted that he cheated with the help of AI, AFP reported from Seoul on Sunday, 15 November 2026. He had scored 397.5 out of 400 in the first round, missing just one of 160 questions. In a written confession submitted through his lawyer, and later in a video, he said he photographed the questions with a miniature camera, sent them to AI and checked the answers on his smartphone. Suspicion had first focused on smart glasses, which he initially denied using.

The Seoul Metropolitan Police Agency said the case has been assigned to investigators, and the finance ministry said it plans to move quickly to suspend his qualification. The Human Resources Development Service of Korea, which runs the exam, said two supervisors were in the room while he sat the test. The agency also said its "AI inspector" caught a candidate wearing smart glasses at a gas engineer certification exam last month, and in October the government said it would pursue criminal complaints against students caught using such devices in the national university entrance exam, the Suneung, which is scheduled for 19 November.

Why it mattersProfessional licensing exams are the gate to regulated work such as tax, audit and law. A near-perfect score earned with a hidden camera and AI, under the eyes of two supervisors, shows that human proctoring alone may no longer be enough, which puts pressure on credentialing bodies and on employers who treat exam results as proof of competence.

The first US AI Music Awards honor songs made at least partly with AI, and AI-generated act Xania Monet wins artist, song and album of the year, Reuters reports

Music made with the help of artificial intelligence got its own awards night in Los Angeles on Friday, 13 November 2026, Reuters reported on Saturday. The first US ceremony of the AI Music Awards honored people who made songs at least partly with AI, and the top prizes for AI artist, song and album of the year all went to Xania Monet, an AI-generated avatar. According to Billboard, as cited by Reuters, Monet's "How Was I Supposed to Know?" was the first song by an AI-based act to earn a place on one of its radio charts. Her creator, Telisha "Nikki" Jones, said she writes all the lyrics and then sets them to music with AI's help. The winners were picked by AI artists who are members of the Sonic Intelligence Academy, a company that makes AI music charts and tools for spotting AI-made songs.

Presenters and winners argued that AI-assisted music deserves to be treated as a new genre that takes real skill and long hours of prompting. "In a world where everybody calls AI fake, the human behind it is real," said Drake Murphy as he presented the award for best AI hip-hop song. The event lands in a contested market: Reuters notes that record labels have sued AI companies, including Anthropic and OpenAI, over training on copyrighted works, that the Elvis Presley estate has also filed a lawsuit, and that the companies rely on the fair use doctrine in their defense. Musicians worry that AI songs will crowd out human artists and take work from producers, singers, engineers and songwriters.

Why it mattersAn awards circuit and a radio chart placement show AI-assisted music moving from novelty to commercial product while the core legal question, whether training on copyrighted works is allowed, is still being fought in court. For media, marketing and licensing businesses, the revenue opportunity and the copyright exposure are arriving at the same time.
