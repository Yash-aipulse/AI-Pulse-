Meta sets 2026 capital spending at $135–145 billion as third-quarter revenue rises 26% and headcount falls 15%

Meta reported third-quarter 2026 results on 21 October. Revenue for the three months to 30 September was $64,601 million, 26% higher than a year earlier, and capital expenditures, including principal payments on finance leases, came to $34.91 billion for the quarter. The company tightened its full-year 2026 capital spending range to $135–145 billion, from a previous $130–145 billion, and guided fourth-quarter revenue to $73–76 billion. Chief executive Mark Zuckerberg pointed to the launch of Muse and Muse Wonder, which Meta calls its first frontier model, as a milestone toward what he describes as personal superintelligence.

Costs rose much faster than sales. Total costs and expenses grew 68% to $51,613 million, partly because of a $9.89 billion charge tied to Meta's August settlement with 52 state attorneys general over teen mental health litigation, and operating margin fell to 20% from 40% a year earlier. Headcount stood at 66,775 at the end of September, down 15% year over year. Meta raised its full-year 2026 expense outlook to $175–179 billion to include the charge, and said it expects to keep investing aggressively in 2027. The board also approved a new non-voting Class C share, to be issued as a one-for-one stock dividend; Meta expects the Class A share price to reflect a two-for-one split once it is paid.

Why it mattersMeta's spending plan is one of the clearest signals of how much compute the largest AI builders intend to buy, and it now runs at well over $100 billion a year while the company's workforce shrinks. For suppliers of chips, power and data center capacity, a narrower but still very large range points to continued tight markets into 2027. For enterprise buyers, it is a reminder that frontier model providers are competing on scale of infrastructure, which shapes pricing and capacity across the market.

Treasury teams with large AI supplier contracts should note that the biggest buyers are still committing capital at this pace when negotiating multiyear terms.

Barclays will cut about 5,000 jobs this year as technology and AI savings feed a cost plan worth more than £2 billion

Barclays said on 22 October, alongside its third-quarter results, that it will reduce its global headcount by about 5,000 roles, which City AM puts at five per cent of the bank's workforce. Around 1,000 of those jobs went in the first half of the year, and the rest are due to go in the third and fourth quarters. The reductions fall mainly on support functions, developers among them. Chief executive C.S. "Venkat" Venkatakrishnan described the affected jobs as mostly operational and functional roles that serve the business, and framed the cuts as the payoff from several years of technology investment. On AI's share of that, he said it is "the icing on the cake, not the cake."

The job cuts sit inside a wider cost program. Barclays is targeting more than £2bn of cost efficiency savings over 2026 to 2028, booked £201m of what it calls structural cost actions in the quarter, citing technology-enabled reductions in full-time staff, and has set aside a further £300m of such actions for the fourth quarter. Pre-tax profit for the three months to September was £2.3bn, up 12 per cent from a year earlier but narrowly short of the £2.4bn internal consensus, as weaker fixed income trading offset a strong quarter in equities. The bank also launched a £500m share buyback.

Why it mattersA major European bank is now putting a headcount number on technology-driven efficiency and tying it to a multi-year savings target that investors will track quarter by quarter. The chief executive's framing is the useful nuance: most of the savings come from years of automation and process work, with AI adding to them rather than driving them alone. Peers, regulators and unions will read the 5,000 figure as a benchmark for what bank operations and technology teams may look like by the end of the decade.

Microsoft says a China-aligned group used an AI agent toolkit to hit more than 2,250 Microsoft 365 tenants through ConnectWise

Microsoft has attributed the recent supply-chain attack on ConnectWise's cloud backup service to Storm-0940, which it describes as a China-state aligned group active since 2021, CyberScoop reported on 21 October. According to Microsoft, the group used ARTEX, an open-source framework that runs autonomous penetration tests through AI agents and works with any AI model, starting as early as September 2026. A four-day password-spraying run against 327 Microsoft 365 and Exchange Online accounts at eight organizations ended on 14 October without success, after more than 151,000 failed logons. The next day the group turned the same infrastructure on the Microsoft 365 environments of ConnectWise Cloud Backup customers.

Microsoft says ARTEX helped the attackers reach more than 2,250 tenants at 2,900 sign-in attempts per minute. Once inside, the agents did more than log in: they ran reconnaissance through Microsoft Graph, changed application credentials to keep access, and altered identities, groups and privileged roles. Targets spanned hundreds of organizations in sectors including defense, energy, financial services, government, legal, semiconductors and think tanks. Microsoft did not say how many customers were actually compromised and noted that a successful sign-in does not necessarily mean data was taken. ConnectWise shut the affected services on Saturday, began a phased restoration on Wednesday and told customers to keep connections disabled while recovery runs for several days. Microsoft executive Igor Tsyganskiy described the incident as "one of many" in a wave of AI-driven attacks since early October.

Why it mattersThis is a named state-aligned group using freely available AI agents to work through thousands of cloud tenants at machine speed, and it came in through a trusted IT service provider rather than the victims' own front door. It shows attackers now operating at AI speed, and defenders need to match it. Any company whose backup, managed IT or remote-support vendor holds privileged access to Microsoft 365 carries the same exposure.

Tesla starts Optimus robot production in Fremont as AI and robotics spending cuts operating income 56%

Tesla reported third-quarter 2026 results after the US market closed on 21 October. According to Electrek, revenue was $28.05 billion, flat on a year earlier, while operating income fell 56% to $718 million, an operating margin of 2.6%, because spending on AI and the Optimus humanoid robot grew faster than gross profit. Research and development alone rose 69% to $2.76 billion. Capital expenditures reached $4.73 billion in the quarter against $2.25 billion a year earlier, and GAAP net income dropped 43% to $787 million. Adjusted earnings of $0.48 a share came in above the $0.45 consensus.

TechCrunch reported that Tesla has begun building Optimus at its Fremont, California factory, in space that used to produce cars. In its shareholder letter, Tesla calls these "the first production-quality bots that we have manufactured," but it has not given a production rate or a date for outside sales. Other AI-linked measures moved up: active Full Self-Driving subscriptions reached 1.74 million, 67% more than a year earlier (a count that, Electrek notes, includes upfront purchases), and its Robotaxi service reached nearly 2 million cumulative unsupervised miles by the end of the quarter, according to Electrek. Tesla ended September with $45.4 billion in cash and investments.

Why it mattersTesla is now spending like an AI and robotics company while its revenue still comes from cars, and this quarter shows what that does to margins. Starting Optimus production on a car line is the clearest sign yet that a large manufacturer intends to sell humanoid robots, but without volumes, prices or delivery dates, buyers should treat it as a pilot-stage product rather than a planning assumption.

Research lab Flapping Airplanes raises $1 billion at a $6 billion valuation to get more AI out of every chip

Flapping Airplanes, a San Francisco AI research lab founded in late 2025, has raised $1 billion at a $6 billion valuation, according to Bloomberg as reported by The Next Web on 21 October. Index Ventures and Lux Capital led the round, and The Next Web names Sequoia Capital, Alphabet's GV, Kleiner Perkins and Nvidia among its investors. The company raised a $180 million seed round in January, and its valuation has roughly quadrupled since then, according to the report. It does not yet sell a product.

The lab is working on ways to train AI models with less data and less computing power, including writing its own version of the software that runs Nvidia's graphics chips. Co-founder Asher Spector argues that "demand for AI is going to outpace chip and data supply," and says that even a 10% performance gain per chip would be very valuable economically. The company plans eventually to sell its technology to help other businesses run AI more cheaply.

Why it mattersInvestors are now backing efficiency research at a scale once reserved for frontier model labs. If methods like these work, the cost of training and running AI could fall without waiting for new chip generations, which would change the economics for companies that buy compute as well as for chip and cloud suppliers. The size of this round, for a company with no product, also shows how much capital is chasing the compute bottleneck.

Nokia's sales to AI and cloud customers rise 123% as third-quarter profit beats forecasts

Finnish network equipment maker Nokia published its third-quarter 2026 results on 22 October, and demand from AI and cloud companies stood out, according to Reuters. Group net sales to that customer group were 123% higher than a year earlier, and orders from the same customers climbed 118% to 1.5 billion euros. Comparable net sales for the whole company grew 10% to 5.15 billion euros, ahead of the 5.05 billion euros expected in a consensus compiled for the company.

Comparable operating profit reached 605 million euros, compared with 448 million euros in the same quarter last year and an average forecast of 453 million euros in a company-provided poll. Nokia kept its full-year outlook unchanged. In a statement, chief executive Justin Hotard said the company expects its combined Optical and IP Networks businesses to grow by 25% to 30% in 2027, adding that operating leverage is becoming more visible. Reuters noted that Nokia's shares have gained 48% so far this year.

Why it mattersAI data centers need more than chips: the optical links and routers that connect thousands of processors are becoming a spending category of their own. Nokia's results show AI budgets now flowing to networking suppliers at a pace far faster than the rest of their business. For buyers, that points to tighter supply and firmer pricing for optical and IP equipment; for investors, it widens the set of companies whose earnings depend on the AI build-out.

Saudi Arabia's Humain widens its Nvidia deal to up to 84 MW of AI computing, with most of the second phase earmarked for a US client

Humain, the AI company owned by Saudi Arabia's Public Investment Fund, has agreed with Nvidia to build up to 84 megawatts of AI computing capacity, roughly 27,000 GPUs, in two phases, The National reports. The expansion was announced on 22 October at Nvidia's GTC conference in Berlin. The first phase is 14 MW of Nvidia Blackwell HGX B300 systems, about 6,000 GPUs, with deployment starting in the fourth quarter of 2026 to serve customers of Humain Cloud, including AI companies, government bodies and businesses.

The second phase, planned from mid-2027, covers 70 MW of Nvidia's next-generation Vera Rubin systems, about 21,000 GPUs. Of that, 50 MW is set aside for a US client that has not been named, with deployment targeted for June, and the remaining 20 MW goes to Humain Cloud in July or August. The companies said the Vera Rubin projects still depend on final agreements and deployment plans. The deal builds on a May 2025 partnership that began with 18,000 Blackwell processors as part of a plan for up to 500 MW over five years, and Humain is aiming for about 3 gigawatts of data center capacity by 2030 and more than 6 GW by 2034.

Why it mattersGulf sovereign AI builders are no longer just buying chips for domestic use; Humain is reserving most of a future Vera Rubin phase for an American customer, which makes Saudi capacity part of the global market for AI compute. For buyers, that adds a new source of supply but also new questions about data location, export controls and power. The conditional wording on the second phase is a reminder that headline capacity is not the same as capacity delivered.

South Korea's competition regulator seeks comment until 11 November on draft guidelines against "AI washing" in advertising

South Korea's Fair Trade Commission, the country's antitrust watchdog, began taking public comments on 22 October on draft guidelines aimed at "AI washing" – marketing that overstates or invents the role artificial intelligence plays in a product. According to Yonhap, as published by The Korea Times, the draft sorts potential breaches of the Act on Fair Labeling and Advertising into four main categories and comes with a self-assessment checklist and a set of frequently asked questions for businesses. Comments are open through 11 November.

The regulator pointed to everyday goods sold under an AI label, including electric hot-water heating mats, cat litter boxes, pillows and weight-control shakes. It also flagged AI-generated virtual people and fabricated reviews used to sell food and pharmaceutical products to older consumers. The commission noted that companies use the AI label to signal "advanced technology, accuracy and objectivity." It says the guidelines are not legally binding; their stated purpose is to help companies stay within advertising law, help consumers choose rationally and support fair competition in AI-related markets.

Why it matters"Powered by AI" has become a sales claim in its own right, and regulators are starting to treat it like any other performance claim that has to be backed up. Even as non-binding guidance, the draft shows how Korea's advertising regulator is likely to read AI claims when it enforces the existing law. The explicit mention of AI-generated spokespeople and fake reviews aimed at older buyers of food and medicines puts health-related marketing at the front of the line.

Use the review to set one global standard for substantiating AI claims, since other advertising regulators may apply similar scrutiny to AI marketing.

Editor's note
The AI economy is now being built on two tracks at once: ever larger spending and ever closer oversight, while attackers move at machine speed.

Tonight's stories show both. Meta is holding its 2026 capital budget at $135–145 billion while running a smaller workforce, Tesla's AI and robotics spending cut its operating income by 56% as Optimus production began, and investors have put $1 billion into a young lab whose bet is that the same chips can do far more. Nokia's 123% jump in sales to AI and cloud customers shows that money now reaching the networking gear that links those chips. Microsoft's account of a China-aligned group using open-source AI agents to reach more than 2,250 Microsoft 365 tenants through a backup provider shows the other side of the same capability. The buildout is also going global, as Saudi Arabia's Humain plans up to 84 MW of Nvidia capacity and reserves most of a 2027 phase for a US client. Barclays put a number on the workforce effect, saying it will cut about 5,000 jobs this year as technology savings feed a cost plan worth more than £2bn, while its chief executive cast AI as an addition to years of automation rather than the main driver. Overnight, South Korea's competition regulator opened comment on draft guidelines against overstated AI claims in advertising, a reminder that the label itself is now under scrutiny. For executives, the practical lesson is to plan for abundant but contested compute, and to put governance, contract certainty on capacity, vendor oversight and evidence for every AI claim in place before agents, whether their own or an attacker's, get near money, customers or privileged accounts.
