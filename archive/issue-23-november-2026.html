Nvidia's board unanimously reinstates Jensen Huang as chief executive after input from senior US officials, and City AM reports the shares jumped 33% after the New York open

In a statement dated 23 November 2026, Nvidia said its board had voted unanimously to bring Jensen Huang back as president, chief executive and board director, effective immediately. The board says it reached the decision after a series of discussions and meetings, and that it is satisfied with his candor and ability to lead the company. It also says it took input from senior levels of the US government and, on that basis, is confident his return poses no national security risk. Chief financial officer Colette Kress, who had been interim CEO, keeps her CFO role. Huang said he takes on the job "with the greatest humility and renewed commitment."

The return does not close the underlying matter. Nvidia says a committee of independent directors, with its own outside counsel, will finish its investigation into reports that Huang was the target of a foreign intelligence effort, and that he supports it. The company has also set up a National Security and Export Control Committee to oversee trade compliance, is hiring a new chief compliance officer who will report to a board committee, and says it will keep cooperating with government inquiries. Markets reacted at once: City AM reports Nvidia shares rose 33% after trading opened in New York, reversing last week's 21% fall, while the Nasdaq 100 gained 7.1% and the S&P 500 4.8% in early trading. Investing.com reports that President Trump had urged the board on Sunday evening to reinstate Huang.

Why it mattersHuang's exit had become the single biggest driver of last week's AI sell-off, so his return removes the leadership vacuum at the company that supplies most of the world's AI computing hardware. It does not remove the compliance risk: the investigation continues, a new export control committee and compliance chief are being put in place, and Washington's questions about chips reaching China remain open. Buyers and investors should read Monday's rally as relief on leadership, not as a clean bill of health on export controls.

Taiwan, China and South Korea roll out market support over the weekend as Asia braces for the AI sell-off, while US stock futures open Monday with Nasdaq 100 futures up about 2%, Reuters reports

After a week in which the Nasdaq fell 10%, its steepest weekly drop since March 2020, governments in Asia spent the weekend trying to steady their markets before Monday trading. According to Reuters, Taiwan's financial regulator on Saturday ordered a further tightening of short-selling rules, Chinese stock exchanges announced support steps such as easier margin financing requirements, and South Korea's finance ministry said on Sunday it stands ready to deploy market stabilization funds immediately if prices keep falling. Taiwanese shares lost 17% last week, their largest weekly fall since 1990, and the Korean benchmark sank to 10-month lows.

When US stock futures opened for the new week, they recovered some ground: Reuters says Nasdaq 100 futures rose about 2% and S&P 500 futures around 1%. Asia was still expected to open weaker. Japanese markets were shut on Monday for a holiday, but futures pointed to selling, Hang Seng futures signaled a fall and Australian shares were 0.5% lower. Reuters links last week's slide to the exit of Nvidia chief executive Jensen Huang, among other factors; the Philadelphia semiconductor index dropped 10% on Friday alone.

Why it mattersWhen three of Asia's most chip-heavy markets change trading rules and line up rescue money over a single weekend, the AI sell-off has become a financial stability issue, not just a valuation debate. Short-selling curbs and looser margin rules can slow forced selling, but they also make price signals less reliable for a while. The bounce in US futures shows buyers are still there, yet it does not tell anyone where the bottom is for AI hardware and the suppliers that depend on it.

Policy & Regulation Hardware & Devices

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says Washington still has no answers from Nvidia on whether its AI chips reached China despite export curbs, and that the board is moving fast to find out, CBS News reports

Speaking on CBS News' Face the Nation on Sunday, 22 November, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the administration still needs Nvidia to explain whether it complied with US export restrictions on advanced AI chips. Last week he said on Fox News that he wanted those answers within 10 days; on Sunday he said they had not yet come in. He told CBS that officials have been talking to Nvidia's interim management, its lead directors and its lawyers. The US is investigating how China may have gained access to the company's most advanced AI chips, and Nvidia has been in crisis since chief executive Jensen Huang resigned after revelations about alleged affairs, including one with a woman CBS describes as a suspected Chinese spy. Chief financial officer Colette Kress has taken charge since his departure.

Bessent said a culture of noncompliance at Nvidia is only a possibility for now, and said the board is working quickly to establish whether one existed. He rejected the idea that the company's informal, handshake-style way of doing business would leave no record: "For deals that size, there's no such thing as an untraceable handshake deal." In his view, payments and paperwork can be used to rebuild what happened even without long formal contracts. He separated that compliance question from Huang's alleged relationship, which he played down. President Trump has called for Huang to return as chief executive; Bessent, who has urged Nvidia to bring in an outsider, said it is still unclear how far up the business knowledge of the chip flows went. CBS adds that Huang has agreed to testify before the Senate Banking Committee and will be interviewed by House panels in early December.

Why it mattersThe question the administration is pressing is not about one executive's private life but about whether a company at the center of the AI supply chain had the controls to stop restricted chips from reaching China. Bessent's point that large deals leave money trails signals that investigators will follow payments and intermediaries, not just contracts. Whatever the board finds is likely to shape how tightly Washington polices AI chip sales through distributors and cloud resellers, and that affects every buyer of high-end AI hardware.

Venture Capital Hardware & Devices

Six-month-old inference start-up Marlowe raises a $277 million seed round led by Accel, reported at a $1.15 billion valuation, to build AI data centers on Etched and AMD chips rather than Nvidia alone, The Next Web reports

On the same day public markets swung on Nvidia's leadership, a new company betting on a less Nvidia-dependent AI build-out raised one of the year's larger seed rounds. According to The Next Web, Marlowe announced on Monday that it has raised $277 million in seed funding. Accel led the round, which GeekWire reported values the company at $1.15 billion. Bessemer, Altimeter, Atreides and Eclipse also invested, along with individual backers including Box chief executive Aaron Levie and Uber chief executive Dara Khosrowshahi. The company was founded in May by Convoy co-founder Dan Lewis, now its chief executive, and Kutta Srinivasan, who previously ran AI infrastructure at Microsoft and is now chief technology officer.

Marlowe focuses on inference, the everyday work of running AI models for users, and plans to match different kinds of workloads, such as coding agents or voice assistants, to the chips and data center designs best suited to them. The Next Web says its first site, built around chips from Etched, goes live in December and will mainly serve an unnamed large AI coding company, and a second site using AMD's Helios racks is due in the second quarter of 2027. Marlowe claims that tuning every layer of its stack can give it a two- to three-fold advantage on performance and cost; that is the company's own claim and has not been independently tested.

Why it mattersInvestors are still writing large checks for AI infrastructure even as public AI stocks fall, but they are increasingly backing alternatives to a single-supplier model. For enterprises, inference is where most AI spending will recur, and a growing set of providers running non-Nvidia hardware could widen choice and pricing leverage over the next 12 to 18 months. The catch is that these are young operators with unproven sites, so capacity commitments need careful diligence.

Selling spreads across Asia on Monday, with Seoul's KOSPI down as much as 4.5% in morning trade and Hong Kong's Hang Seng off 1.8%, Al Jazeera reports

Friday's tech-led sell-off carried into Monday trading across much of Asia. According to Al Jazeera, the main indexes in South Korea, Taiwan, Hong Kong and mainland China all fell. The KOSPI slid as much as 4.5% during the morning, a deeper loss than at the open, while the Hang Seng Index dipped 1.8% and Shanghai's CSI 300 lost more than 1.3%. Taiwan's Taiex dropped more than 1% at the open and then moved back and forth between losses and gains. Japanese markets were closed for Labour Thanksgiving Day.

The slide follows a Friday on which, Al Jazeera notes, the S&P 500 closed 3.7% lower for its seventh loss in a row and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 5.3%, erasing everything it had gained in 2026. The KOSPI, Nikkei 225 and Taiex went into the weekend between 5.1% and 6.2% lower. US futures pointed to a rebound on Monday, with S&P 500 futures up 2% and Nasdaq futures up 2.4% at the time of the report. Al Jazeera also cites research firm Gartner's estimate that global AI-related investment, much of it financed with debt, will reach $2.7 trillion in 2026 and $3.6 trillion in 2027, and says the Democratic Party's midterm victory is expected to slow data center construction in US states.

Why it mattersThe selling is no longer limited to the chip-heavy markets of Seoul and Taipei: Hong Kong and mainland China are falling too, despite the support steps announced over the weekend. The Gartner figures show how much spending sits behind the AI trade. If a large share of that investment relies on borrowed money, a sustained fall in share prices can make funding dearer for data center builders and their suppliers, which eventually reaches the companies buying AI services.

Seoul trims its losses to about 3% by early afternoon as institutions buy, but IT services and construction stocks slump on data center regulation worries, with Samsung SDS down 13.89%, Seoul Economic Daily reports

By early Monday afternoon, South Korea's main index had recovered part of its morning slide. Seoul Economic Daily, citing Korea Exchange data, reports that the KOSPI stood at 4,711.71 at 1:44 p.m., down 158.25 points or 3.25%, after a sell-side sidecar was triggered soon after the open. Domestic institutions were the buyers, taking a net 270.9 billion won of shares on the main board, while individual investors sold a net 330.6 billion won and foreign investors 262.9 billion won. The KOSDAQ had earlier broken below 620, under its December 2024 low, before recovering to 640.51, down 0.80%.

The heaviest losses had moved away from the chipmakers. Samsung Electronics was down 3.19% at 177,250 won and SK hynix 3.59%, but IT services and engineering groups fell much further: Samsung SDS lost 13.89%, LG CNS 12.67%, Samsung E&A 10.03% and Hyundai E&C 9.35%, with internet platforms NAVER and Kakao down 7.92% and 7.10%. The paper links these drops to concern about data center regulation. It adds that Taiwan's Taiex was up around 2.6% and Nasdaq futures roughly 3% at the time, and that government work over the weekend to prepare a 10.76 trillion won market stabilization fund helped limit the fall.

Why it mattersThe sell-off is widening from the companies that make AI chips to the companies that build and run the data centers those chips go into. When system integrators and construction firms fall harder than Samsung Electronics or SK hynix, investors are questioning future data center order books, not just chip demand. For enterprises, that matters because these firms are often the contractors delivering AI infrastructure and cloud migration projects, and their funding costs and appetite for new commitments can shift quickly.

European shares edge up on Monday morning, with the STOXX 600 up 0.3% and chip equipment makers ASML, ASM International and BE Semiconductor gaining between 2.8% and 4%, after the region's steepest weekly fall since March 2025, Reuters reports

Europe opened the week on a calmer note than Seoul or Hong Kong. According to Reuters, the pan-European STOXX 600 was up 0.3% at 627.61 points by 0814 GMT on Monday, with Germany's DAX also up 0.3% and leading the major national indexes. The rise follows a drop of nearly 6% in the previous week, which Reuters describes as the index's sharpest weekly decline since late March 2025.

Technology shares did most of the work. Reuters reports the European technology index rose 1.3%, with semiconductor equipment suppliers ASML, ASM International and BE Semiconductor each up between 2.8% and 4%. The agency ties last week's slide to doubts about how quickly heavy AI spending will pay off, and notes that Anthropic lost momentum after its US stock market debut. Mining shares also gained 1% as gold climbed 3% from a 14-month low.

Why it mattersA modest bounce in European chip equipment makers is one of the first signs on Monday that investors are separating the companies that supply the tools for making AI chips from the broader AI trade. It does not reverse last week's losses, but it suggests the market is not yet pricing a deep cut in chip manufacturing investment. For buyers of AI hardware and the finance teams funding it, the key signal is that pressure is uneven across regions and parts of the supply chain, so price and allocation terms may move differently by supplier.

Defense & National Security Venture Capital

London defense AI startup Agon raises a $70 million Series A at a $450 million valuation, led by Northzone, with the UK Sovereign AI Fund and NSSIF joining as first-time investors, Resilience Media reports

While public AI stocks were under pressure, private money kept flowing into defense AI. According to Resilience Media, London-based Agon has raised $70 million in a Series A round that values the company at $450 million. Existing backer Northzone led the round, and two UK state-backed vehicles, the UK Sovereign AI Fund and the National Security Strategic Investment Fund (NSSIF), came in for the first time. Earlier backers Lakestar, XYZ, Lux Capital, Global Founders Capital and Speedinvest also took part, and chairman David Helgason, the founder and former CEO of Unity Technologies, is increasing his stake.

Agon came out of stealth only four months ago with $30 million. It is building what it calls a synthetic battle arena, powered by its Agon Engine, to train AI for military systems. Resilience Media notes the company has not yet launched a product publicly or named any customers; a spokesperson said the first products should arrive "by the end of the year." The new money is earmarked for platform development, hiring and business development. CEO and co-founder Tristam Constant previously led European defense at Applied Intuition, was senior director for Europe at Anduril and served 13 years in the British Army.

Why it mattersThe UK government is now putting its own investment funds behind domestic defense AI, at a valuation set before the company has a public product. For defense buyers and their suppliers, that signals where state support and procurement attention are heading: training and testing infrastructure for autonomous systems. For investors, it shows defense AI is still raising at rising valuations even as listed AI names sell off, which makes the gap between private and public pricing a risk to watch.

Editor's note
The AI sell-off has moved from trading desks to boardrooms and finance ministries: Nvidia's leadership answer came first, the compliance answer has not.

Over the weekend, Taiwan tightened short-selling rules, Chinese exchanges eased margin financing and South Korea said rescue funds are ready, all after a week in which the Nasdaq lost 10% and Taiwanese shares 17%.
