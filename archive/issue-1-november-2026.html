Quad foreign ministers pledge more than $85 million to widen access to trusted AI across the Indo-Pacific and plan to map chip and AI supply chain chokepoints

The foreign ministers of the US, India, Japan and Australia met in Atlanta on Saturday, alongside the G20 Foreign Ministers' Meeting, and set out a joint economic and security agenda with AI near the top. In a joint press release, the US State Department said the four countries intend to provide more than $85 million in technical and financial assistance to support access to trusted AI across the Indo-Pacific, including through a program it calls the Quad SI Accelerator. The release uses the term "Super Intelligence (SI)" throughout, with a footnote explaining that in Australia, India and Japan the term refers to artificial intelligence. It does not break down how much each country will contribute or what the money will fund.

The ministers also said they will start a new effort to spot semiconductor and AI supply chain chokepoints before they cause disruptions, stressed the need for secure supply of leading-edge chips and AI hardware, and agreed to pick priority countries for cooperation on 5G, 6G, Open RAN and AI-enabled radio networks. Alongside the AI measures, the US committed $10 million to facilities in India and ASEAN countries that recover critical minerals from waste, the group announced its first Quad Business Leaders' Forum, and its export credit agencies will meet in Tokyo on 18 November. According to the Press Trust of India, the meeting brought together Marco Rubio, S. Jaishankar, Toshimitsu Motegi and Penny Wong, was the group's third foreign ministers' meeting this year, and comes as a Quad leaders' summit is being planned for mid-November. Rubio said the four countries "are delivering results for our people."

Why it mattersThe four governments are treating AI access and the chips behind it as part of economic security, not just technology policy. A joint effort to find supply chain chokepoints signals that chip and AI hardware supply could become a coordinated policy issue across the region, while the assistance money and the accelerator program may open funded routes for companies building AI services in Indo-Pacific markets. The details, including who gets the money and how, have not yet been published, so the practical impact will depend on what follows at the leaders' summit.

South Korea's October exports reach a record $115.3 billion as chip shipments jump 259% to $56.4 billion on AI infrastructure spending

South Korea shipped a record $115.3 billion of goods abroad in October, up 93.7% from a year earlier, according to trade data reported by Yonhap via The Korea Times on Sunday morning. Semiconductors led the rise: chip exports climbed 259% to $56.4 billion, which works out to about 49% of all October exports (our calculation). The Ministry of Trade, Industry and Resources put the jump down to strong global chip demand and to "continued investments by major technology companies in artificial intelligence (AI) infrastructure."

Imports rose 36% to $72.78 billion, leaving a trade surplus of $42.5 billion for the month. By destination, exports to the US rose 185.8% to $24.9 billion, which the report linked to demand for chips, computers and electrical equipment used in AI infrastructure, while shipments to China rose 133.6% to $26.98 billion, above $20 billion for a fifth month in a row. Other products grew far more slowly, with car exports up 18.1% to $6.55 billion and petrochemicals up 27.5% to $4 billion.

Why it mattersKorea's trade data arrives at the very start of each month, which makes it one of the earliest hard readings on AI hardware demand. October's numbers show chip shipments still accelerating at the same time as investors are questioning how much the largest technology companies are spending on AI. For buyers, a surge of this size points to continued tight supply and firm pricing for memory and servers, while it also leaves Korea's economy more exposed to any slowdown in AI data center building.

Investors buy protection against an AI downturn, with record put-option trading in CoreWeave and default-insurance costs for five big tech companies at their highest since 2021, The Wall Street Journal reports

Investors and technology executives are paying for protection in case the AI investment boom turns, according to a Wall Street Journal report summarized by Investing.com. Trading in put options, which gain value when a share price falls, on AI infrastructure provider CoreWeave hit record levels in October, based on Cboe Global Markets data, and popular contracts would pay off if the stock fell to $35 from about $55. The report says the cost of credit-default swaps, which insure lenders against a company failing to repay, has reached its highest level since 2021 for Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle, with Oracle's spreads widening by more than one percentage point, according to bond data provider Solve.

New hedging tools are also forming around the chips themselves. According to the report, Jump Trading has set up a desk that trades computing power and sells derivatives that protect data center operators against falling chip rental prices, and Susquehanna International Group is hearing from data centers and private AI infrastructure firms that want protection against semiconductor-related risks. For now, rental rates for Nvidia's B300 chips are up about 56% this year to $7.56 per hour, according to Silicon Data. The hedging follows a 2.9% fall in the Nasdaq Composite on Friday, which the report says left the index with its worst month since March 2025, although demand for protection "remains relatively small" next to the scale of AI investment.

Why it mattersMarkets are starting to put a price on the risk that AI spending outruns its returns, rather than only debating it. Higher default-insurance costs for the biggest buyers of AI infrastructure can feed through to what they pay to borrow for data centers, and a market for hedging chip rental prices gives both operators and buyers a way to lock in revenue or costs. For finance teams, these prices are an early, public signal of how lenders and traders now view AI risk.

Aramco completes its purchase of a 49% stake in Saudi AI company Humain for about $2.6 billion, with the PIF keeping 51%

Saudi Aramco has closed its acquisition of a 49% stake in Humain, the AI company backed by the Public Investment Fund (PIF), Arab News reported on Sunday, citing a joint statement issued on 1 November. Aramco made a cash contribution of about SR9.9 billion ($2.6 billion) during 2026 and is also putting its own AI, computing and digital assets into the company. It holds the stake through a wholly owned subsidiary, while the PIF keeps 51% and majority control. The deal completes a plan first set out in a non-binding term sheet signed on 28 October 2025.

Humain, launched in May 2025, is building data centers, high-performance computing and cloud platforms, AI models including the Arabic-language ALLaM, and AI services for companies and government, and it is targeting 3 GW of AI data center capacity by 2030. According to the report, Aramco expects to use Humain's infrastructure to roll out AI across its industrial operations and to earn more from its existing digital assets. Aramco chief executive Amin Nasser said the partnership strengthens the company's position as "a global technology leader and AI powerhouse," and pointed to $11.3 billion in Technology Realized Value achieved since 2023. Separately, Humain and DataVolt announced a preliminary agreement on 21 October under which AI cloud company Nebius would take up to 50 MW at the Neom Oxagon campus.

Why it mattersOne of the world's largest energy companies now co-owns a national AI champion, tying Saudi oil wealth, power supply and industrial data directly to AI infrastructure. With the PIF and Aramco both behind it and a 3 GW target, Humain is positioned as a major regional supplier of AI computing capacity, which matters for any company weighing where to run AI workloads in the Middle East. The deal also shows energy producers moving from selling power to data centers toward owning the AI businesses themselves.

Residents near Adelaide rally against Quantum DC's proposed 1,200 MW AI data center, backed by three political parties, before any development application is filed

Locals in South Australia rallied at Mannum against a proposed AI data center at Tungkillo in the Adelaide Hills, ABC News reported. Developer Quantum DC wants to build a 1,200 MW AI data center with its own energy infrastructure on 120 hectares on Collins Road, about 41 km east of Adelaide's city center, with a first stage starting in 2028. The Liberal Party, the Greens and One Nation backed the residents' opposition. A local organizer, Magdalene Lippett, told the ABC that opponents "will fight it using every legal means possible."

The project has not yet entered the formal approval process. According to the ABC, Quantum DC has not lodged an application and says it will not do so until it holds community consultation, which has been moved to 20 and 21 November. The company's plan, as described in the report, would draw about 80% of its electricity through two on-site substations linked to existing ElectraNet lines and backed by solar and wind supply contracts, with an on-site gas-fired plant for firming. It says the site's water would come from captured and recirculated rainwater rather than the River Murray or existing groundwater.

Why it mattersOpposition to large AI data centers is no longer limited to the US, and it is now drawing support from political parties before a project has even been formally filed. For developers and the cloud and AI companies that rent their capacity, early community resistance can delay timelines, add conditions on power and water, and raise costs. It also shows that detailed power and water plans, here solar and wind contracts and recycled rainwater, are not by themselves enough to win local consent.

Samsung chairman Lee Jae-yong visits the company's Yokohama advanced packaging lab, a planned 350 billion won hub for boosting AI chip performance

Samsung Electronics Executive Chairman Lee Jae-yong visited the company's Advanced Package Lab (APL) in Yokohama, Japan, Samsung said on Sunday, according to a Yonhap report carried by Korea JoongAng Daily. Samsung describes the lab as a global research hub for technologies that improve AI chip performance. It develops and tests materials and production processes for advanced chip packaging, the step that connects and stacks chips, and is designed to draw on Japan's strength in materials, components and equipment.

During the visit, Lee met the lab's researchers to review progress on packaging materials and processes and discussed closer cooperation with Japanese suppliers, stressing that advanced packaging is essential to staying competitive in chips in the AI era. Samsung set up the lab in 2023, spent about two years preparing the facility and running early research, and held its official opening in September 2026. The company plans to invest 350 billion won ($262 million) in the facility over five years through 2028.

Why it mattersPackaging has become one of the main bottlenecks in AI chip supply, because high-performance AI processors depend on memory and logic chips being stacked and linked closely together. A visit by Samsung's top executive signals that the company sees packaging, and its Japanese supply base, as central to winning AI chip business. For buyers, progress here affects how quickly supply of AI memory and accelerators can grow.
