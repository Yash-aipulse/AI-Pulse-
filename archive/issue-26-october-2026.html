Anthropic files publicly for a Nasdaq IPO, showing $16.3 billion of revenue in the first half of 2026 and plans to spend upward of $450 billion on cloud computing

Anthropic said on 26 October that it has filed a Form S-1 registration statement with the US Securities and Exchange Commission for a proposed initial public offering of its Class A common stock. According to the company's announcement, it intends to list on the Nasdaq Global Select Market under the ticker "ANT," with Goldman Sachs and Morgan Stanley named for the preliminary prospectus. The number of shares and the price range have not yet been set, and no shares can be sold until the registration becomes effective. Anthropic is organized as a public benefit corporation.

The filing sets out the company's finances in detail. Yahoo Finance reports that Anthropic brought in $16.3 billion of revenue in the six months to June, against $4.6 billion for the whole of 2025, and that roughly one-fifth of its revenue came from two customers. It spent $9 billion on AI infrastructure in the first half of 2026, compared with $7.3 billion across 2025. The net loss through June was $80.7 billion, though Yahoo Finance says "the vast majority of that was the result of accounting charges"; the company lost $42 billion in 2025. Looking ahead, Anthropic expects to spend upward of $450 billion on cloud computing under agreements with providers including Amazon and Google.

Why it mattersThe filing opens the books of one of the leading frontier AI labs to public investors, and its numbers are likely to become a benchmark for how investors value AI model companies. By our calculation, revenue in the first half alone was more than three times the total for all of last year, but the spending commitments are far larger still, and a large share of sales comes from just two customers. For companies that rely on Claude, a listing brings regular disclosure on pricing power, capacity and customer concentration that was not available before, and also new pressure on the company to show a path to profit.

Meta plans to raise up to $95 billion in new equity for its AI build-out, starting with about $35 billion of stock offerings, and Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom signal interest in buying up to $5 billion

Meta Platforms on Monday launched public share offerings worth about $35 billion and said it plans a further at-the-market share sale program of up to $60 billion, Reuters reported on 26 October, bringing the potential total to $95 billion to pay for data centers, computing power and other AI infrastructure. In its announcement, Meta split the immediate raise into about $22.5 billion of Class A common stock, sold under forward sale agreements, and about $12.5 billion of depositary shares representing mandatory convertible preferred stock, with Morgan Stanley and Goldman Sachs leading the deals. The larger program is not expected to begin until the first quarter of 2027, after Meta's new non-voting Class C shares start trading, and Meta says its timing, size and terms have not been set. Nvidia, AMD and Broadcom have indicated interest in buying up to $5 billion of the common stock, although those indications are not binding.

The raise follows a sharp change in Meta's cash position. Reuters says the company burned $2.12 billion of cash in the third quarter, its first negative free cash flow in more than ten years, compared with positive free cash flow of $10.63 billion a year earlier. Meta has raised the bottom of its 2026 capital spending forecast to $135 billion to $145 billion, from $130 billion to $145 billion, and has about $825 billion of future spending commitments, according to Reuters. At 30 September it held $82.45 billion in cash, cash equivalents and marketable securities, only slightly more than its $80.92 billion of long-term debt. Meta's release says it owns data centers in 23 US states and is building four superclusters, each with more than one gigawatt of capacity.

Why it mattersUntil now the largest technology companies paid for AI mostly out of cash flow and, more recently, debt. Meta turning to new equity on this scale signals that even the most profitable platforms now need outside capital to keep up the pace of data center construction. Chip suppliers offering to buy Meta shares also shows how closely vendors and their biggest customers are now tied financially. For buyers of cloud and AI services, it is a reminder that their providers' spending plans rest increasingly on capital markets staying open.

Goldman Sachs lowers its 12-month Kospi target to 9,000 from 12,000 as concerns about AI infrastructure spending weigh on Korea's chip stocks

Goldman Sachs strategists led by Timothy Moe have cut their 12-month target for South Korea's Kospi index by 25%, from 12,000 to 9,000, The Edge Malaysia reported on 25 October. Goldman had raised the target several times earlier this year, most recently in June, and held it at 12,000 through the global AI selloff that started later that month. The bank says the cut reflects more conservative valuation assumptions after the index dropped more than 35% from its June peak. Because Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix dominate the index, the Kospi has become a bellwether for the AI trade, and investors have grown worried about whether heavy AI infrastructure spending can continue, about rising financing costs, and about the lack of clear returns.

Goldman still argues that Korean shares are too cheap. Even the lower target implies more than 60% upside from current levels, and the bank's note says the market "is pricing an overly negative fundamental outlook." By Goldman's estimate, current chip stock valuations assume the sector's 2027 earnings fall by 65%, which it considers far too pessimistic. The Edge reports that the Kospi now trades at about four times expected earnings for the next 12 months, a level last seen across 18 tracked markets in 2006, according to Bloomberg data, while non-memory stocks, roughly half the index, trade at 7.6 times. Goldman continues to favor power, industrial and corporate governance stocks, along with companies that benefit from domestic reflation and Korean popular culture.

Why it mattersKorea's memory makers supply a large share of the chips that AI data centers depend on, so the Kospi is one of the clearest market readings of confidence in AI infrastructure spending. A major bank cutting its target while still calling the market cheap shows how far sentiment has moved since June: investors are now pricing in a sharp fall in chip earnings, not just slower growth. For companies that buy memory or fund AI projects, the gap between that pessimism and Goldman's view is the key uncertainty for 2027 budgets and supplier risk.

Elon Musk says SpaceX has closed $40 billion in debt financing to buy GPUs, after its quarterly capital spending nearly tripled to $19.6 billion

SpaceX has completed a $40 billion debt raise to pay for graphics processing units, the chips that train and run AI models, Elon Musk said in a post on X, Reuters reported on 25 October. Musk described the money as "primarily callable debt for GPU purchases that will arrive in Q1 and Q2," meaning SpaceX can repay it early, and said it would not be a major factor in the current quarter. Earlier in October, Reuters had reported that the company was in talks with banks and asset managers to borrow $40 billion to buy Nvidia AI chips. According to Reuters, SpaceX's investment-grade standing and income from its Starlink satellite internet and launch businesses have helped it keep access to credit.

The borrowing comes as SpaceX's spending climbs quickly. Reuters says the company's revenue rose 161% from a year earlier to $14.58 billion in the three months to 30 September, while capital spending almost tripled to $19.6 billion. SpaceX is also moving toward making its own chips: Terafab, its chip manufacturing joint venture with Tesla in Austin, is expected to produce a first test chip around May or June of next year and aims for volume production by the third quarter of 2028. In its S-1 filing ahead of its IPO, SpaceX listed manufacturing its own GPUs among its substantial capital expenditures.

Why it mattersA single company raising $40 billion of debt for AI chips shows that the AI build-out is now being funded on a scale once reserved for governments and utilities, and increasingly with borrowed money. It also shows who can still borrow on good terms: a company with steady revenue outside AI data centers. For buyers of AI computing and the firms that lend to them, the gap between well-funded players and smaller providers is becoming a real factor in supplier risk and in who gets chips first.

Tata Communications plans to invest Rs 4,350 crore in a 10,000 km "AI Super Highway" of fiber linking India's data center hubs

Tata Communications said on 26 October that it will invest Rs 4,350 crore (USD 400–450 million) in about 10,000 km of AI-ready fiber across India, which it calls "India's AI Super Highway," news agency ANI reported. The network is meant to connect data centers directly to each other. The first phase covers the Mumbai–Pune–Hyderabad–Chennai corridor, and later phases will extend to Visakhapatnam, Bhubaneswar, Jamnagar and GIFT City. According to the company's regulatory filing, the investment should add about 6 Pbps of network capacity between FY2030 and FY2036. Its current network carries 1.25 Pbps and runs at roughly 70–80% utilization. The project will be funded from operating cash flows.

Managing director and chief executive Ganesh Lakshminarayanan said connectivity between data centers could become a market of more than USD 1 billion over the medium term. The plan comes as large technology companies commit heavily to Indian AI capacity: ANI notes that Microsoft has committed USD 17.5 billion to cloud and AI infrastructure, skilling and operations in India, Google USD 15 billion to an AI hub in Visakhapatnam, and Amazon an additional USD 13 billion. Citing the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology, ANI says India's installed data center capacity grew from 375 MW in 2020 to about 1,575 MW in 2026.

Why it mattersAI workloads increasingly run across several data centers at once, so the links between sites matter as much as the sites themselves. With its current network already running at 70–80% of capacity, Tata is betting that India's data center boom will need much more long-distance bandwidth. For banks, cloud users and global capability centers in India, faster and more resilient links between Mumbai, Hyderabad and Chennai should widen options for spreading AI workloads and backups across cities, though most of the new capacity is not due until FY2030 to FY2036.

SWI Compute launches with about $1 billion of Nvidia systems on order, aiming to turn its parent's 3.6 GW of secured power into AI factories, starting in the UK

SWI Compute, a unit of Euronext Amsterdam-listed SWI Group, came out of stealth on 26 October to build and run Nvidia-based AI data centers, which it calls AI factories, beginning in Europe. In a press release, the company says it has ordered approximately $1 billion of Nvidia systems, became a Preferred Nvidia Cloud Partner in August, and has signed contracts with its first customers. The first of these, which it describes only as a US-based AI inference platform, has contracted capacity. Its initial deployment runs Nvidia Blackwell Ultra systems, with partners led by systems integrator Delta Computers set to deliver more than 8,000 GPUs.

The company's pitch rests on power. SWI Group says it holds 3.6 GW of power capacity across Europe and the US, spread over 24 sites and secured over six years, after it began investing in land, power and building shells in 2020. Its five European campuses are in Ireland, the UK, Denmark, Spain and Italy, and one is already leased to a major hyperscaler. The first large AI factory, in the UK, is planned at 30 MW with about 7,000 next-generation Rubin GPUs and is expected online by the end of 2027. That campus is planned to grow to 530 MW, as a first step toward 2.3 GW of Nvidia AI factory capacity by 2029. SWI Compute chief executive Halil Mirakhmedov says "energy availability represents the primary bottleneck" for European AI infrastructure.

Why it mattersIn Europe, the hard part of building AI capacity is no longer buying chips but getting grid connections, and those take years. SWI's model is to start from a bank of power and sites assembled before the AI boom and then add Nvidia hardware on top. If it works, it adds a new, non-US-hyperscaler source of large-scale computing in Europe, which matters to companies that want AI capacity located in the region. The timelines are long, though: the flagship UK site is not due online until the end of 2027.

Bedrock Robotics raises $600 million at a $4.1 billion valuation after its self-driving excavators start doing paid work on US job sites

Bedrock Robotics, a San Francisco company that builds autonomous systems for construction equipment, announced on 26 October a $600 million Series C round at a post-money valuation of $4.1 billion. CapitalG and Emergence Capital co-led the round, with the US Innovative Technology Fund, Bessemer Venture Partners and Temasek joining as new investors alongside existing backers including 8VC, Eclipse and NVIDIA. The company says it has now raised nearly $1 billion in total and will use the money to expand deployments, build up its field operations and work with more partners.

Bedrock says fully autonomous excavators running its technology began paid work on live commercial job sites in mid-August 2026, and that its customer volume has more than doubled since 2025. Haydon, an employee-owned contractor based in Tempe, Arizona, is using the system for fully autonomous earthwork on a remote site in Texas and plans to test trench work and larger machines. The company also named new customers in Minnesota, Florida, Virginia, Colorado, Missouri, Georgia and Arkansas. Chief executive Boris Sofman said the company has shown that "fully autonomous excavators can perform real work of value."

Why it mattersMost AI investment news is about chips, data centers and software. This round is a large bet on AI doing physical work in an industry that struggles to find skilled equipment operators. The key detail for buyers is that the machines are now doing paid work on commercial sites rather than only running pilots, which turns autonomous earthmoving from a demonstration into something contractors, infrastructure owners and their insurers will need to price into bids and schedules.

Samsung says it will use AI to cut the time it takes to develop new chip materials from five years to two

Samsung Electronics plans to use AI to shorten the development of new semiconductor materials by three years, from five years to two, Yonhap reported via The Korea Times on 26 October. Choi Young-sang of the Samsung Advanced Institute of Technology, the company's research center, presented the plan at a Korea Industry Association Forum event on the use of AI in industrial research and development. Samsung also aims to find more than five times as many promising material candidates and to cut the number of experiments needed to find the best conditions by more than 80%.

The problem Samsung is trying to solve is scale. According to the report, a semiconductor structure with seven layers can produce more than 100 million possible combinations of materials, while researchers can carry out only about 50 experiments a month. Choi said the traditional approach has depended on researchers' experience and intuition, and that results from failed experiments have not been fully used. Samsung expects the AI-driven approach could lower development costs and could later be applied to battery materials as well.

Why it mattersNew materials are one of the main ways chipmakers keep improving memory and logic chips, including those used in AI data centers, and they are slow and costly to discover. If Samsung can turn a five-year cycle into a two-year one, it could speed up its product roadmap against rivals. It is also a clear example of a pattern other industries can copy: using AI to decide which experiments to run, and learning from failed tests rather than discarding them.
