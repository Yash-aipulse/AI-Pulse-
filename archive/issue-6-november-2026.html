Seoul's Kospi falls 8.5%, triggering a 20-minute trading halt, and Tokyo's Nikkei drops 5.1% as the AI selloff spreads from Wall Street to Asia, AFP reports

In early trading on Friday, 6 November 2026, the AI-led share selloff that hit US markets on Thursday spread to Asia's two biggest chipmaking markets, according to AFP. Seoul's Kospi index dropped 8.5% to 4,923, a fall steep enough to set off a circuit breaker that stopped trading for 20 minutes. Tokyo's Nikkei 225 lost 5.1% to 56,930. Memory and chipmaking-equipment companies led the declines: AFP reports that Samsung Electronics fell nearly 10%, SK hynix more than 11%, Tokyo Electron around 9% and Kioxia 13%.

The slide followed a Wall Street session in which the tech-heavy Nasdaq closed 5.5% lower, its worst percentage loss since April 2025, with Nvidia and other chip stocks among the hardest hit. AFP ties the rout to Tuesday's US midterm elections: investors at first welcomed the Democratic gains, then grew worried that the results could slow or derail the wave of spending on AI data centers. Japanese online broker Monex pointed to "concerns about stricter regulations on AI-related technologies," along with fears that tighter rules on building data centers could hold up projects.

Why it mattersSouth Korea and Japan are home to many of the memory chip and chipmaking-tool suppliers that AI data centers depend on, so their share prices are a quick read on how investors see future AI spending. A drop of this size does not cancel any orders, but it makes capital more expensive for chipmakers and data center developers and can make their boards more cautious about committing to new capacity. For buyers, the lesson is that the AI supply chain now reacts to US political signals within hours, and that the risk of tighter rules on data center construction has become a concern for global markets, not just local planning boards.

Potential investors doubt Anthropic can start its IPO roadshow next week and list in November, with some treating a $1.5 trillion valuation as the ceiling, Reuters reports

People weighing an investment in Anthropic's planned stock market listing are questioning whether the Claude developer can begin marketing its shares next week and still list in November, Reuters reported late on Thursday, 5 November 2026, citing four people familiar with investor discussions. The roadshow had been expected to start in the week of 9 November, with pricing the week after. According to these people, investors want a more attractive valuation, and some regard the recently discussed $1.5 trillion as the top of their range. Earlier this year, investors had expected an October listing at a valuation of as much as $2 trillion, and some expect the offering to reach $100 billion, the people said.

Investors are also waiting for Anthropic's October revenue, which was left blank in its updated registration filing; two of the people said that figure will show whether growth has recovered from a recent slowdown. The people said Anthropic may need to offer a bigger discount because of its record of quarterly losses and its large commitments to partners building AI infrastructure, and that it could hold off if AI-related assets stay under pressure. The doubts follow a two-day AI selloff after Tuesday's US midterm elections, in which more than 50 technology stocks in the S&P 1500 fell 10% or more. Anthropic declined to comment. Keith Lerner of Truist Advisory Services described a "buyers' strike in tech."

Why it mattersAnthropic's listing is the clearest test yet of whether public investors will pay private-market prices for a frontier AI lab. If the company has to accept a lower valuation or wait, it resets price expectations for other AI companies raising money and for the chip, cloud and data center suppliers whose growth depends on what the labs can afford to spend. For enterprise buyers, it is a reminder that the financial health of an AI model provider is now a real vendor-risk question, not just a matter for investors.

Defense & National Security Venture Capital

Defense technology company Shield agrees to buy radar maker Echodyne in a deal valued at $5.3 billion and raises $2.5 billion at a $28.8 billion valuation

Shield, the defense technology company behind the Hivemind autonomy software and the V-BAT and X-BAT autonomous systems, announced on 5 November 2026 that it has agreed to acquire radar company Echodyne in a transaction valued at $5.3 billion. According to Shield, $4.8 billion will be paid and issued when the deal closes, and up to $500 million more depends on Echodyne hitting agreed performance targets. The companies expect completion in early 2027, subject to regulatory approvals and other usual conditions. Echodyne's team stays in place, its chief executive will report to Shield's CEO, Gary Steele, and its radars will still be sold as standalone products.

To fund the purchase, Shield is raising $2.5 billion in three parts: $1 billion of Series H equity at a $28.8 billion post-money valuation, co-led by Disruptive and Innovation X Capital; a $1 billion structured equity investment from funds managed by Blackstone; and $500 million of debt arranged by JPMorgan Chase Bank. Echodyne makes compact, solid-state radars based on its MESA technology that are built into hundreds of counter-drone and security systems, with customers in more than 40 countries. In September it won a counter-drone radar contract with a $250 million ceiling from Joint Interagency Task Force 401, and its new Kirkland, Washington, facility will be able to make about 30,000 radars a year. Echodyne CEO Eben Frankenberg framed the logic simply: "autonomous systems can only act on what they can sense." Both companies say they expect triple-digit revenue growth this fiscal year.

Why it mattersAI autonomy software is only as useful as the data its sensors feed it. By buying a radar supplier, an AI-led defense company is moving to control both the sensing and the decision-making layers of drones and counter-drone systems, the way large platform vendors bundle hardware and software elsewhere. The financing matters too: a mix of new equity, structured equity from Blackstone and bank debt shows that large investors are still willing to fund defense AI at scale, on the same day that US AI chip stocks fell sharply. For buyers and integrators, a radar many of them rely on will soon be owned by a company that also sells competing autonomous systems.

Cybersecurity & Infrastructure

OpenAI releases GPT-6 Cyber to approved security customers and says researchers used it to build working exploits for 948 Linux kernel flaws, Fortune reports

OpenAI has released GPT-6 Cyber, a model built for security professionals, Fortune reported on 5 November 2026. According to Fortune, the model beats OpenAI's general-purpose GPT-6 Astra on most cyber tasks, and because it could help attackers if misused, OpenAI is keeping it behind vetted access. It goes first to approved customers in Daybreak Red, OpenAI's tier for offensive work such as attack simulation and red-teaming, with access widening to all Red customers "over the coming weeks." OpenAI is also letting more organizations and individual researchers test GPT-6 Astra for defensive work through Daybreak Blue, which covers tasks such as code review, malware analysis and incident investigation.

OpenAI says researchers used GPT-6 Cyber to uncover 81 previously unknown, high-severity vulnerabilities in Firefox and to produce working exploits for 948 Linux kernel vulnerabilities, 588 of which had not been known before. Fortune notes that these Linux flaws could let someone with ordinary local access take full root control of a machine, and that the total is more than five times the number exploited in a July study that used GPT-5.6 Sol. The company says it trains its Cyber models to sharpen specialist security skills and to refuse fewer requests that can serve both attackers and defenders. The release lands in a crowded market: Fortune cites a Gartner forecast that worldwide spending on AI cybersecurity will reach $51.3 billion this year, up from $25.9 billion last year, and notes that Google and Anthropic are also competing for security customers.

Why it mattersThe time between a flaw being found and a working exploit existing is shrinking fast. If one model can turn hundreds of kernel bugs into usable attacks, the same capability will eventually reach less careful hands, and patch cycles measured in weeks or months will not hold. Access controls like Daybreak Red slow that spread but do not stop it. For security leaders, the practical shift is to assume that AI-assisted attackers can find and weaponize known weaknesses faster, and to bring the same tools into their own defensive testing before adversaries do.

Hardware & Devices Financial Services

TSMC weighs a move into advanced memory chips, including 3D DRAM requested by Nvidia, and its US-listed shares jump 7.7% in early trade, Investing.com reports

TSMC is studying whether to begin producing advanced memory chips, according to a report by The Information that Investing.com covered on Friday, 6 November 2026. Citing two people with direct knowledge, The Information says the chipmaker recently started drawing up plans to make 3D DRAM, a type of memory in which cells are stacked vertically, after Nvidia, its main customer, asked it to. The options under review are building dedicated plants or buying an existing site and refitting it, the route Micron took in March when it agreed to pay $1.8 billion for a Taiwan facility from Powerchip Semiconductor Manufacturing Corp. TSMC's US-listed shares rose 7.7% in early trading on Friday.

The effort is led by Hsu Kuo-chin, a TSMC vice president in charge of advanced packaging who previously ran Micron's regional operations. Investing.com points out that memory is a volatile business dominated by Samsung, SK hynix and Micron, and that while TSMC holds patents on vertical DRAM, making it in volume without defects is hard. An entry would also complicate an existing tie-up: in 2024, TSMC agreed to make base logic dies for SK hynix's next-generation high-bandwidth memory. Memory shortages have already pushed up hardware costs, and Apple raised device prices earlier this year after memory became more expensive.

Why it mattersMemory has become one of the tightest limits on AI hardware supply, and the market is dominated by three companies. A fourth supplier backed by Nvidia's demand could ease prices and shortages over time, but the report gives no production date, and making 3D DRAM in volume is technically difficult. For now this is a planning signal, not new supply: buyers of AI servers should expect memory costs and allocation to stay tight in the near term, while watching whether TSMC commits to a plant.

IREN's quarterly AI cloud revenue nearly triples to $200 million, but the company pushes its target of more than $4 billion in annualized revenue back to January 2027

IREN, which rents out AI computing capacity from its own data centers, reported results on 5 November 2026 for the three months ended 30 September 2026, the first quarter of its 2027 fiscal year. The company says AI cloud revenue rose 182% from the previous quarter to $200 million, up from $71 million, while total revenue rose 66% to $228 million. Its older bitcoin mining business shrank to $28 million from $67 million. IREN reported a net loss of $263 million, narrower than the $684 million loss of the prior quarter, and adjusted EBITDA of $66 million, a 29% margin, compared with $19 million.

The more cautious part of the release concerns timing. IREN says its target of more than $4 billion in annualized recurring revenue is "fully contracted and fully funded," but it now expects that capacity to be running in January 2027 rather than by the end of 2026, and says it could slip to the end of February. The company attributes the change to a design change requested by a customer, which it does not name, and to supplier timing. It says its Horizon 1 facility went into service for Microsoft during the quarter, and that Horizons 2 to 4 are in late-stage construction. IREN lists more than 5 gigawatts of secured power and $9 billion of cash, committed GPU financing and prepayments, against $15 billion of remaining planned capital spending for the fiscal year; it aims to cover more of the gap with a further $6 billion of financing that depends on market conditions.

Why it mattersSpecialist AI cloud providers are now a real source of computing capacity for large technology buyers, and IREN's numbers show how quickly that revenue can scale once facilities are switched on. The same release also shows the weak points: delivery dates depend on customer design choices and supplier schedules, and the build-out relies on raising billions more in financing. For companies buying AI capacity from these providers, revenue growth matters less than whether the promised capacity arrives on time and whether the provider can keep funding construction.

Indian IT services stocks rise as much as 8% in early trade while the AI selloff hits Asia, with the Nifty IT index up more than 2.35%, Business Today reports

While chip and AI stocks fell across Asia on Friday, 6 November 2026, India's IT services companies moved the other way. Business Today reports that the Nifty IT index gained more than 2.35% in early trade. Mphasis led the group, rising as much as 8% to Rs 2,712, HCL Technologies added more than 2.5% to Rs 1,468, and Infosys and TCS each rose by up to 3%. Persistent Systems gained over 2%, and Coforge and Wipro each rose more than 1%. Oracle Financial Services Software was the one decliner the report names, down nearly 2%.

The report links the move to investors asking whether money pulled out of AI-linked stocks could shift toward other markets, India among them. It notes that Wall Street's selloff wiped out more than $2 trillion in market value and that provisional data showed foreign portfolio investors bought Rs 13,801.83 crore of Indian shares on Thursday. VK Vijayakumar, chief investment strategist at Geojit Investments, told the publication the slowdown in the AI trade "will turn out to be beneficial for India." Business Today also cites Nomura as seeing a federal US moratorium on AI data centers as unlikely, though a temporary pause at state level is possible. The report cautions that the gains depend on whether global money keeps moving toward emerging markets and whether worries about AI spending ease.

Why it mattersIndian IT services firms face the risk that AI tools automate part of the work they bill for, yet Friday's early trading suggests some investors see them as a place to shelter when AI hardware stocks fall. A single morning's move does not change any company's outlook, but it shows that worries about AI spending can reprice Indian IT suppliers quickly in either direction. Executives who buy services from these firms, or who hold their shares, should watch whether this holds once the selloff settles.

Equinix opens SG6, its sixth Singapore data center, with an initial investment of about $290 million and 8 MW of first-phase capacity aimed at AI and GPU workloads

Data center operator Equinix announced on 6 November 2026 that SG6, its sixth IBX data center in Singapore, is now open, with AI, high-performance computing and other data-heavy workloads as its target market. Equinix puts the initial investment at approximately $290 million. The first phase brings 8 MW of capacity and roughly 1,550 cabinets, and the company says the site will offer up to 20 MW of IT capacity when it is fully built out.

According to Equinix, SG6 combines its own proprietary cooling systems with liquid cooling for power-hungry GPU equipment, is designed to run at a power usage effectiveness (PUE) of 1.20 and is aiming for Green Mark Platinum certification. The company says its Singapore sites have had 100% renewable energy coverage since 2020, and that it plans to open a co-innovation facility at SG6 with the National University of Singapore in 2027, focused on liquid cooling, digital twins and battery storage. Chief executive Adaire Fox-Martin described Singapore as "one of our most important markets globally".

Why it mattersIn a week when investors are questioning the pace of AI data center building in the US, Equinix is still adding AI-ready capacity in Asia, but in a measured way: 8 MW to start, far smaller than the gigawatt-scale campuses that dominate AI headlines, with efficiency and liquid cooling built in from day one. For companies running AI in Asia-Pacific, this is the kind of capacity they will actually rent: shared, well connected and close to customers, rather than dedicated training campuses.
