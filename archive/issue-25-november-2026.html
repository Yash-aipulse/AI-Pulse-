Wall Street opens sharply lower as the AI chip sell-off extends: Nvidia drops 8% at the open and the Nasdaq falls 3.2%, Yahoo Finance reports

The retreat in AI shares deepened when US markets opened on Wednesday. Yahoo Finance reports that chipmakers led the decline, with Nvidia down 8% at the opening bell, adding to its losses from Tuesday. At the open the Nasdaq Composite was down 3.2%, the S&P 500 2.1% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average about 700 points. Yahoo's quote data a few minutes into the session (9:35 AM Eastern, about 8:05 PM IST) showed Nvidia at $141.90, down 8.68%.

Two pressures from outside the AI sector added to the selling. The Bureau of Economic Analysis reported that the PCE price index was 3.8% higher in October than a year earlier, which Yahoo calls the highest reading since 2023, and that core PCE rose 3.3% against the 3.2% economists expected; citing CME Group, Yahoo says traders now see a roughly 98% chance that the Federal Reserve raises rates by 25 basis points at its meeting next month. The Trump administration also proposed tariffs of up to 20% on China, the European Union and 12 other trading partners, which a US official said would probably not take effect before January. Yahoo's Jared Blikre points to a split inside tech: chip stocks fell about 8% on Tuesday while software held steady, yet the iShares semiconductor ETF (SOXX) is still up about 28% this year and the software ETF (IGV) is down about 10%. Apollo's Torsten Sløk says software faces "a double whammy of AI disruption" and higher-for-longer rates. All market figures here are from the first minutes of trading and will change by the close.

Why it mattersThe AI trade is now being hit by the cost of money, not only by doubts about returns. If the Fed raises rates, financing data centers and GPU fleets becomes more expensive and investors place a lower value on profits that are years away, which weighs hardest on richly priced chip and AI infrastructure companies. Executives planning 2027 AI spending should expect tougher financing terms as well as tougher questions.

AI stocks give back ground a day after Jensen Huang's return: the Nasdaq closes down 1.5% and Anthropic ends back below its $83 IPO price, AP reports

Monday's relief rally did not last. By the close on Tuesday, the Associated Press reports, the S&P 500 had fallen 0.5% for its eighth loss in nine sessions and the tech-heavy Nasdaq composite had dropped 1.5%, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average gained more than 400 points as most stocks outside technology rose. In AP's report with an hour of trading left, Nvidia was down 7.6% after jumping 31.5% on Monday, which AP calls its largest one-day gain in more than two decades. Other AI names fell by similar amounts in that late-session snapshot: CoreWeave was down 12.7% and GE Vernova 5.5%, even though 2 out of every 3 stocks in the S&P 500 were higher.

Anthropic, which went public last week, finished the day back below the $83 price at which it sold shares in its IPO, according to AP; it was down 9.3% in the late-session report and had briefly reached $115 on its first day of trading. The agency lists several reasons for the renewed selling: worry that AI share prices rose too far and may need to deflate, doubts that AI will earn enough to justify data center spending, high bond yields that AP links partly to the war with Iran, and concern that Democratic midterm gains could bring tighter AI rules, possibly including pauses on data center construction. AP notes Huang's return does not resolve those questions. Index moves above are closing figures; the individual stock moves are from AP's report about an hour before the close.

Why it mattersA one-day bounce on a leadership change has not repaired confidence in AI valuations. With the newest listed AI developer already below its offer price within a week, boards and finance teams should assume that capital for AI projects will be priced more carefully, and that investors will keep asking for proof of returns on data center spending rather than promises.

Dell raises its full-year revenue forecast to $202 billion as its AI server backlog reaches a record $132.3 billion, Reuters reports

Dell lifted its annual revenue and profit outlook on Tuesday, the third increase this year, according to Reuters. The company now expects $202 billion in revenue for the fiscal year, up from its earlier target of $192 billion and ahead of the $194.48 billion analysts expected, based on LSEG data. Its forecast for revenue from AI-optimized servers rose to $78 billion from $74 billion, and its adjusted earnings per share outlook moved to $29.68 from $25.50. Dell also announced an additional $25 billion share buyback, and Reuters says the stock was up about 5% in extended trading.

For the third quarter, which ended on 30 October, Dell reported record revenue of $54.94 billion against an average analyst estimate of $49.32 billion, and adjusted earnings per share of $9.45 against estimates of $6.59. Revenue at its Infrastructure Solutions Group, which includes servers, nearly tripled to $41.97 billion, while PC revenue grew 3% to $12.89 billion. Dell booked $60.2 billion of new AI server orders in the period, taking its AI server backlog to a record $132.3 billion, and said more than 7,500 customers are deploying its AI Factories. Chief operating officer Jeff Clarke said enterprises are "buying more compute to accommodate growing workloads," and Dell expects the memory chip shortage to last into 2027, Reuters reports.

Why it mattersOn the same day investors sold AI shares, one of the largest suppliers of AI servers reported that customer orders are still growing quickly. A backlog of this size, alongside a memory shortage Dell expects to run into 2027, means companies planning their own AI infrastructure should expect long lead times and firm pricing for servers and components through next year.

Seoul's KOSPI falls 4.90% in the first 15 minutes of Wednesday trading, setting off a sell-side sidecar as the US chip rout reaches Korea, MoneyToday reports

South Korea's main index gave back much of Tuesday's rally as soon as trading opened on Wednesday. According to MoneyToday, the KOSPI opened 3.94% lower at 4,809.00 and by 9:15 a.m. was down 245.47 points, or 4.90%, falling back below the 5,000 mark. A sidecar, a brief automatic pause in program trading, was triggered early in the session. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix were both down in the 6% range at the time of the report, and Samsung Electro-Mechanics in the 8% range.

Foreign investors were net sellers of 338.5 billion won of KOSPI shares in the early trade and institutions of 65.5 billion won, while individual investors were net buyers of 388.7 billion won, MoneyToday reports. The outlet links the drop to renewed worry about returns on AI investment, reports that the US could impose additional tariffs on 16 countries including South Korea, and a BNP Paribas cut to its price target for Micron on the risk of excess memory supply. A researcher at iM Securities pointed to the 7.88% plunge in the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index overnight and a 4.56% fall in KOSPI 200 night futures. Figures are from early trading and will change by the close.

Why it mattersKorea's market is heavily weighted toward memory chipmakers that supply AI data centers, so it shows quickly how global investors feel about AI spending. A swing from a sharp rally one day to a drop of nearly 5% at the next open suggests the AI trade remains highly sensitive to each new headline, which affects currency, funding and supplier risk for companies exposed to the Korean chip sector.

OpenAI says its GPT-6 models designed proteins using only reasoning and code, with 35 of 322 lab-tested binders attaching to their intended target

OpenAI published research on 24 November saying its GPT-6 family of models can design protein shapes and amino acid sequences without calling the specialized biology models the field normally relies on for that step, working instead through reasoning and code it writes itself. The designs were made computationally by OpenAI, mostly with GPT-6.2, and then built and tested in the laboratory by Onava, which OpenAI describes as its research partner. In the headline experiment, 322 small proteins called minibinders were tested against 5 target proteins and 35 of them, or 10.9%, tested positive for binding the target they were designed for, with hits spread across all five targets.

A second set of experiments asked the model to rewrite the amino acid sequences of binders and antibodies that already existed. There the success rates were much higher: 75 of 88 designs bound for the MDM2 target, or 85%, against 26 of 42 for VEGF-A and 8 of 59 for PD-1. Redesigned antibody binding loops performed similarly, with 128 of 182 tested designs showing signs of binding across six single-domain antibodies aimed at IL-4Rα, and 179 of 272 fragments binding for a set of five therapeutic antibodies. OpenAI is explicit about the limits: the campaign still used specialized structure-prediction models to filter which candidates went to the bench, so the work does not show the models generating new binders end to end on their own, and predicting a complete protein structure from sequence remains far behind purpose-built tools, at an average TM-score of 0.38 for its best internal model against 0.93 for ESMFold2. OpenAI calls full structure prediction "a clear weakness" and says it is weighing biosecurity risks under its Preparedness Framework. The models used here are internal or pre-release, and the company announced no availability.

Why it mattersProtein design has been the domain of specialized models built for the task. If a general-purpose reasoning model can get useful hit rates without them, the barrier to entry for computational biology work drops towards access to a frontier model and a wet lab, which changes build-versus-buy thinking for discovery teams and widens the pool of groups capable of this work – a point the biosecurity reviewers will also take. The honest reading of these numbers, though, is early capability rather than a replacement: the lab hit rates come with heavy filtering by other tools, and binding is only the first of several hurdles before a molecule does anything useful in a patient.

Companies linked to Alice Huang pledged up to 764 AI servers and modules valued at $270 million to secure financing in China, Reuters reports

Reuters says it reviewed dozens of filings in a Chinese central-bank credit registry, which records equipment used to back loans and other financing, and found that companies tied to Alice Huang raised money against computing hardware for large AI data centers in China. The filings cover up to 764 AI servers and modules valued at $270 million, plus $31 million of networking gear. Reuters describes Huang as the businesswoman whose affair with Jensen Huang led to his brief exit as Nvidia's chief executive, and says she formerly controlled Megaspeed, the Singapore company that Washington put on its trade blacklist on 23 November. Corporate records link the Chinese companies to her sister, Huang Yu, and to her husband, Kang Wei.

One of the companies, Century Litong, which Reuters calls an Nvidia preferred cloud partner, signed five leasing arrangements between May and December 2024 covering up to 512 servers with a declared value of 1.42 billion yuan; its equipment sits at a large AI computing complex in Shanghai. Another affiliate pledged 252 AI computing modules to Ping An Bank as collateral for a $70 million loan in April 2026. Reuters stresses that the records do not show the servers contained chips barred from export to China, or how the equipment entered the country, and that the dollar values reflect financed assets rather than necessarily new purchases. Megaspeed has denied wrongdoing and says Alice Huang left in August 2025; Nvidia and the Chinese companies did not respond to Reuters.

Why it mattersThis is the first documentary trail showing how much Nvidia-class hardware sits with a network tied to the person at the center of the Huang affair, and it surfaces a week before Huang is due before Congress. It also shows Chinese banks and lessors treating AI servers as financeable collateral, which makes the location and ownership of GPUs a question for lenders and compliance teams, not only for chip sellers.

Two dozen House Democrats challenge the FAA's nationwide rollout of an AI air traffic tool built under an $875 million contract, Reuters reports

A group of about two dozen House Democrats, led by Representative Rick Larsen, the top Democrat on the House Transportation Committee, has raised concerns about the Federal Aviation Administration's decision to deploy a new AI tool for air traffic controllers across the country, Reuters reports. The lawmakers described the move as the "sudden expansion of a largely untested system" and asked the FAA to answer a set of questions by 30 November.

According to Reuters, the FAA started using the software in the Washington area in September, where it applies predictive analytics to how flights are managed, before deciding to deploy it nationwide on 10 November. The work sits under an $875 million, 12-year contract awarded to Air Space Intelligence. The Reuters report does not include a response from the FAA.

Why it mattersAir traffic control is one of the highest-stakes places where government has put AI into daily operations. Congressional pushback here sets expectations for how much testing evidence agencies, and their contractors, must show before scaling AI in safety-critical systems, a standard that is likely to spread to other infrastructure such as power grids, rail and hospitals.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul signs a five-year ban on toys with built-in AI chatbots, with fines of up to $15,000 a day for each violation

Governor Kathy Hochul signed legislation on Tuesday, 24 November, that pauses for five years the manufacture, distribution and sale in New York of toys that have AI chatbots embedded or built in, the governor's office said. Examples in the announcement include talking stuffed animals, dolls and robots that go online to hold conversations with children. The bill, S.9408A/A.11144B, was sponsored by State Senator Andrew Gounardes and Assemblymember Rebecca Kassay.

The law takes effect 90 days after signing, so it will not apply during this holiday season, according to the release. Attorney General Letitia James will enforce it, with civil penalties of up to $15,000 per day for each violation. The governor's office points to testing by an unnamed national consumer watchdog of chatbot toys marketed to children aged 3 to 12, which found some toys discussing sexually explicit topics, offering unsafe guidance and showing weaker safety limits as conversations ran longer, with few or no parental controls. The measure follows an October New York law that bars certain unsafe chatbot features for minors.

Why it mattersThis is an outright product pause rather than a disclosure rule, and it targets a fast-growing consumer category. Toy makers, AI model providers that license chatbots into devices, and retailers selling into New York now face a hard deadline, and other states watching child safety and AI may copy the approach.

Editor's note
Eight stories, one thread: AI is now being judged by the people who answer to the public.

Investors went first, twice. A day after cheering Jensen Huang's return, AP reported the Nasdaq down 1.5% at Tuesday's close and Anthropic back below its $83 offer price. On Wednesday the selling spread: MoneyToday reported Seoul's KOSPI down 4.90% within 15 minutes of the open, and Yahoo Finance reported Nvidia down 8% as US trading began, with October PCE inflation at 3.8% and traders pricing a roughly 98% chance of a Fed rate increase next month, so the cost of capital has joined doubts about returns as a weight on AI. The demand side told a different story: Dell, according to Reuters, raised its full-year revenue forecast to $202 billion on a record $132.3 billion AI server backlog. Capability news ran alongside the market noise: OpenAI said its GPT-6 models designed proteins through reasoning and code alone, and that 35 of 322 lab-tested binders attached to their intended target, while conceding that full structure prediction is still a weakness. Officials followed: two dozen House Democrats asked the FAA to justify putting an AI air traffic tool, built under an $875 million contract, into nationwide use, and New York went furthest, with Governor Kathy Hochul signing a five-year ban on toys with built-in AI chatbots, backed by fines of up to $15,000 a day. Reuters added a paper trail to the questions around Nvidia: central-bank filings show companies linked to Alice Huang, the former controller of the newly blacklisted Megaspeed, pledged up to 764 AI servers and modules valued at $270 million to raise financing in China, though the records do not show whether any restricted chips were involved. For executives, the common lesson is that buyers are still spending while markets, regulators and legislators now expect evidence – of returns, of compliance and of safety testing – before they extend trust, and that the money to fund AI plans may soon cost more.
