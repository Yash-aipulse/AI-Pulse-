Democrats plan a wave of AI and data center bills once they take control of Congress in January, covering power costs, model testing and liability, Reuters reports

Democrats intend to bring a large package of legislation on AI systems and data center growth when they take over Congress in January, according to lawmakers, congressional aides and advocates who spoke to Reuters for a report published on Saturday, 7 November 2026. The planned bills fall into three areas: protecting households from higher electricity costs linked to data centers, requiring independent testing of advanced AI systems, and setting out when AI companies can be held liable for harm caused by their products. Reuters contrasts this with the current Republican-led Congress, which mostly encouraged AI development and passed no restrictions.

Senator Chuck Schumer, expected to become majority leader, said on Thursday that shielding consumers from energy costs tied to data center expansion will be a priority next year. Representative Greg Casar, who chairs the Congressional Progressive Caucus, wants AI executives to testify under oath, and Representative Lori Trahan plans to push for independent experts to assess leading AI models against safety standards, according to her deputy chief of staff. Reuters notes that any bill faces steep odds while President Donald Trump, who has repeatedly rejected mandatory AI rules, is in office; overriding a veto takes two-thirds of both chambers. Ed Mills, policy research manager at Raymond James, said Democrats could attach AI rules to must-pass annual bills. "The probability of legislation is much higher than people anticipate," he said.

Why it mattersEven if few of these bills become law while President Trump is in office, committee control gives Democrats hearings, document requests and public pressure, and the three themes – who pays for data center power, who tests frontier models and who is liable when AI causes harm – will shape contracts and disclosures well before any statute passes. Companies building or buying large amounts of AI capacity in the US should assume more scrutiny of grid costs, model safety claims and product liability from early 2027.

The US approved Chinese firms with ties to blacklisted entities to buy or handle Nvidia's H200 AI chips, a New York Times investigation reports

The Trump administration cleared Chinese companies linked to US-blacklisted entities and military contractors to buy or handle Nvidia's advanced H200 AI chips, according to a New York Times investigation published on Saturday, 7 November 2026, as reported by The Jerusalem Post. One of them, New Matrix, is about 17 months old and has a registered office outside Beijing that looked empty when Times reporters visited; its owners have links to Wise Road, a private equity fund the Commerce Department put on its trade blacklist in 2024. The office of another licensee, Maginfra, also appeared deserted, and analysts linked that company to Inspur, which is on the US entity list. The Times did not establish that any chips were diverted to the Chinese military.

The Commerce Department allowed Nvidia to sell H200 processors to China in December 2025, with Taiwan-made chips routed through the US, where a 25% tariff applies. About two dozen Chinese companies reportedly received approval, including Baidu, BYD and Tesla Shanghai, and each could buy tens of thousands of chips but had not reached those limits, according to people familiar with the approvals. The Bureau of Industry and Security said it carefully reviews applications for security risks, and Nvidia spokesman John Rizzo said "it makes no sense for the Chinese military to depend on American technology." Beijing has restricted H200 imports, and shipments to China made up under 1% of Nvidia's data center revenue in the quarter ending July.

Why it mattersUS chip export policy toward China has swung between restriction and licensed sales, and reporting that questions how licensees were vetted makes another tightening more likely, especially with Democrats about to control Congress. The direct revenue at stake for Nvidia is small, but the episode adds compliance risk for anyone in the AI hardware supply chain who sells, resells or services accelerators that could end up with Chinese buyers.

Anthropic agreed to pay $3.4 billion for 1.7 gigawatts of gas-fired generators to power AI data centers, the Financial Times reports

Anthropic has committed $3.4 billion to buy large gas-fired generators with 1.7 gigawatts of capacity, according to a Financial Times report published on Sunday, 8 November 2026, as summarized by The Next Web. The FT says the deal was signed in late September with OnePWR Solutions, a power supplier based in San Antonio, Texas, which had ordered the machines from Caterpillar about two years earlier but needed funding to complete the purchase. Anthropic took over those orders along with the installation work rather than acquiring OnePWR, and no OnePWR staff moved across. The FT describes it as the largest known single purchase of power equipment by an AI company.

The 1.7 gigawatt figure comes from one person familiar with the deal, according to the FT, and where the generators will be installed is not known. Both companies declined to comment to the FT, and The Next Web says it has not independently confirmed the deal. The report follows Anthropic's amended IPO filing last week, which The Next Web says showed $895 billion in commitments, nearly all for computing power and infrastructure. Buying generation equipment directly suggests AI developers are no longer willing to wait for utilities and grid connections to supply the electricity their data centers need.

Why it mattersAn AI lab taking over gas turbine orders shows that power, not just chips, now limits how fast frontier AI capacity can be built, and that the largest buyers will pay to jump the queue for scarce generation equipment. For utilities, equipment makers and anyone planning their own data center or industrial load, this means longer lead times and more competition for generators, turbines and grid slots, plus closer scrutiny of on-site gas power as lawmakers focus on data center energy costs.

SoftBank and ABB weigh carving out ABB's China robotics business to rescue their $5.4 billion deal after Beijing blocked it, Bloomberg News reports

SoftBank Group and ABB are looking at separating the Chinese part of their $5.4 billion robotics deal, which Beijing blocked earlier in the week, according to a Bloomberg News report citing people familiar with the matter that Reuters carried on Saturday, 7 November 2026. Under a carve-out, the China business would become a separate entity with different owners. Bloomberg reports that ABB is also considering other routes, including finding another buyer, and that the China business could be worth $2 billion to $3 billion.

China is ABB's biggest robotics market, making up about 30% of its robotics business, with about 30 local companies and roughly 15,000 employees in nearly 130 cities, Reuters says. This is the second deal China has blocked this year: in April, Meta was ordered to unwind its purchase of AI startup Manus, valued at more than $2 billion. ABB declined to comment, and SoftBank did not respond outside business hours.

Why it mattersIndustrial robotics is one of the main ways AI moves onto factory floors, and Beijing has now shown twice this year that it will block foreign deals that touch Chinese AI and automation assets. Splitting businesses along national lines may become the standard way to get such deals done, which affects how buyers value targets and how customers in China are served after a sale.

Eminem's publisher Eight Mile Style sues AI music platform Suno over 269 songs, seeking damages that could exceed $40 million, KPIX reports

Eight Mile Style, the Michigan-based company that owns or controls Eminem's song catalog, has sued AI music generator Suno in the US District Court for the Northern District of California, according to a KPIX (CBS San Francisco) report published on 7 November 2026. The complaint, filed on Friday, says Suno copied 269 Eminem compositions without permission and accuses it of direct copyright infringement, getting around technical protection measures, and removing or altering copyright management information. The publisher wants a finding of willful infringement, the impoundment or destruction of unauthorized copies, and damages that KPIX says could exceed $40 million.

The suit alleges that Suno copied millions of protected works to train the model behind its service and, citing reporting by 404 Media, that it used a service to scrape songs and lyrics from YouTube Music, Deezer and Genius. As one example, the complaint says Suno generated a track called "Lose Yourself by Eminem" for a user that repeated the song's lyrics word for word. In a statement to CBS News, Suno said it prohibits unauthorized use of artists' work, blocks prompts that name specific artists and songs, uses outside providers to screen uploaded audio and lyrics, and will keep adding safeguards. The claims are allegations and have not been tested in court.

Why it mattersThe case adds a major rights holder to the legal fight over how generative AI models are trained, and it targets not only training data but also outputs that allegedly reproduce lyrics verbatim and the stripping of copyright information. Companies that use AI-generated music, audio or text in marketing and products carry some of this risk too, because an output that copies protected material can expose the user, not only the vendor.

Investors shift money from Asia's AI chip giants into Indian and Chinese stocks after the US midterms, as the Nifty 50 posts an 8.6% weekly gain, Bloomberg reports

Fund managers are moving money in Asia away from the AI trade and toward Indian and Chinese markets that have been out of favor, according to a Bloomberg report published by Business Standard on 8 November 2026. Bloomberg ties the shift to the unwinding of AI stocks after Democrats swept Congress in the US midterm elections. Asia has been hit especially hard because several of its largest listed companies, including Samsung Electronics, SK Hynix and TSMC, supply the global AI build-out, leaving those markets exposed as investors question whether heavy spending on computing infrastructure will pay off. India's Nifty 50 rose 8.6% last week, its best week since 2021, and foreign funds put a net $1.9 billion into Indian shares over the week through Wednesday. A Hong Kong index of mainland Chinese companies gained 5.2%, its strongest week since March 2025.

Several managers described where they are putting money. Aberdeen Investments favors India and Vietnam and sees upside in Chinese technology stocks, while LOGOS Asset Management's Charlie Hong said he sold SK Hynix, Samsung, Intel and Lumentum last month and added Indian holdings. Allianz Global Investors said healthcare and consumer staples could gain while defense and financials may lag, and Fidelity's Dale Nicholls sees early signs of a rotation toward consumer stocks in Korea. Emmer Capital Partners' Manishi Raychaudhuri described India and China as offering "a considerable array of non-AI exposures." Not everyone is abandoning AI: Bloomberg reports that some investors see the selloff as a chance to buy, given the sector's longer-term growth prospects.

Why it mattersThe AI selloff is now changing how global investors allocate money across Asia, not just how US tech shares trade. That matters for the cost of capital at the memory and chip makers that supply AI data centers, and for Indian and Chinese companies with little AI exposure, which may find foreign investors more willing to fund them. Whether the rotation lasts will depend on how far US scrutiny of AI spending goes once the new Congress sits.

Only 23% of companies surveyed by EY India get high value from AI in customer service, though those leaders report KPI gains of 50% or more, ANI reports

A new EY India report on AI in customer service finds a wide gap between the companies that are getting results and those that are not, according to an ANI report published on 8 November 2026. EY sorts respondents into three groups: "Achievers," 23% of respondents, who report high or very high value; "Challengers," 33%, with moderate returns; and "Aspirants," 44%, with low or very low returns. Achievers regularly reported improvements of 25% to 50% or more, and above 50% on measures such as customer experience, revenue uplift, average handle time and agent productivity. Challengers reported gains of 10% to 25%, while Aspirants saw less than 10% or did not track metrics at all.

The report links the difference to how far companies have gone beyond pilots. EY says 91% of Achievers had scaled voice AI deployments, and that they had extended AI across roughly 60% of their core technology areas, using conversational copilots and knowledge bases that update themselves. More than 80% of Challengers and Aspirants still relied on human agents or basic menu-driven phone systems. Customer experience, rather than revenue or cost cutting, was the main goal for 45% of Achievers. The obstacles differ by group: 38% of Achievers named data quality, while more than 40% of Challengers and Aspirants pointed to governance, compliance and change management. "While AI adoption in customer service has become widespread, value realization remains uneven," said Preeti Anand, a partner in business consulting at EY India.

Why it mattersCustomer service is one of the most common places companies deploy AI, yet by EY's count fewer than a quarter of the firms it surveyed report high value from it. The findings suggest that results depend less on buying the technology and more on scaling it, setting clear goals and fixing data and governance – useful evidence for leaders deciding whether to expand or rethink their current pilots.

AI should lift long-term demand for industrial robots, but robot makers may gain less than their customers, S&P Global says, as installations hit a record 603,000 units in 2025

S&P Global expects AI to improve the long-term growth outlook for industrial robotics, but warns that the companies that build robots may benefit less than the manufacturers that use them, according to an ANI report on the S&P Global analysis published on 8 November 2026. The report argues that AI could make automation affordable for work that involves frequent production changes or a lot of programming, which could widen the market for robots. Citing International Federation of Robotics data, it says global industrial robot installations rose 11% to a record 603,000 units in 2025, and that the number of robots in operation rose 9% to about 5.1 million. Labor shortages, the push for higher productivity and manufacturing investment are supporting demand, though growth varies by region and industry.

The catch, in S&P Global's view, is that smarter robots may reduce the need for new ones. If AI makes existing equipment more flexible and keeps it busier, manufacturers could automate more tasks with the machines they already have, which could partly offset demand for additional units. S&P Global expects AI gains to show up first in robot programming, simulation and application engineering, before fully autonomous robots become commercially viable at scale. It says the overall effect on equipment sales and robot makers' revenues remains uncertain, and that it does not assume wider AI adoption will automatically raise profitability in the industry.

Why it mattersFor manufacturers, the analysis points to value from AI coming as much from getting more out of installed robots as from buying new ones. For investors and suppliers in the robotics chain, it is a reminder that a technology shift that helps customers does not always translate into higher sales or margins for equipment makers.

Editor's note
AI is becoming a political question as much as a technical one, on both sides of the Pacific.

Today's eight verified stories show governments and civil society moving to set the terms for AI. Reuters reports that Democrats plan bills on data center power costs, independent model testing and AI liability once they take control of Congress in January, while a New York Times investigation, as reported by The Jerusalem Post, finds that some Chinese firms cleared to buy or handle Nvidia's H200 chips have ties to blacklisted entities. And in the courts, Eminem's publisher Eight Mile Style has sued AI music platform Suno over 269 songs, KPIX reports, adding pressure on how models are trained and what they output. Markets are reacting as well: Bloomberg reports that investors are moving money from Asia's AI chip suppliers into Indian and Chinese stocks, with India's Nifty 50 up 8.6% last week. The common thread is that the next phase of AI adoption will be shaped by who pays for power, who is allowed to buy chips and assets, who must disclose impacts, and who is paid for the content models learn from. For executives, the practical steps are to prepare for congressional hearings on testing and liability, tighten export screening of chip customers, plan deal structures that can survive a China veto, secure generators and grid capacity early as AI developers buy up equipment, check that AI vendor contracts cover copyright claims over outputs, review how concentrated their portfolios and supply chains are in AI hardware names, and measure whether their own AI deployments are delivering value before scaling them.
