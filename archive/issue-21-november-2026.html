Trump says US tech companies that build AI data centers abroad will face a heavy tax, without giving details or telling his own staff first, The National News Desk reports

Speaking at a White House press briefing on Friday, 20 November, President Donald Trump said American technology companies that put AI data centers in other countries will face "a tremendous tax," according to The National News Desk. He said the US would not let its companies build data centers outside the country, and argued that letting chipmaking and other industries move offshore had hurt America. He did not explain how the tax would work and said he had not yet told his staff about the idea. The comments came as he discussed more than $17 billion in deals announced during his trip to Asia.

The remarks land as data centers face resistance at home. The outlet reports that voters turned down data center proposals in 40 of 41 local ballot measures earlier this month, and that some governors have promised to pause new projects because of worries about electricity bills. On Thursday the Senate passed a bipartisan bill to speed up permits for power plants, transmission lines and other energy projects; it would also make data centers pay for the energy they use and for transmission upgrades, and it now moves to the House. On Monday the administration issued rules restricting remote access to advanced US chips and servers, aimed at limiting China's AI training.

Why it mattersFor now this is a statement, not a policy: there is no rate, no draft and no timeline. But it signals that where AI capacity is built may become a tax and trade question for US companies, at the same time as local opposition and new energy-cost rules make building at home harder. Companies with overseas data center plans, and the countries hosting them, face a new layer of uncertainty that could affect site choices, contracts and pricing for cloud and AI services.

The SEC's investigation of Nvidia is looking at its accounting practices, Reuters reports, and the shares fall about 13% on Friday as the company's market value drops by roughly $491 billion in one session

US Securities and Exchange Commission staff are examining Nvidia's accounting practices, according to an exclusive Reuters report on Friday, 20 November, that cites two people briefed on the matter. Reuters says it could not establish which accounting issues the regulator is looking at or when the inquiry began. The SEC declined to comment and Nvidia did not immediately respond. The report builds on The Wall Street Journal's news on Tuesday that the SEC had opened an investigation into the chipmaker, which is already facing pressure over the scandal that led to Jensen Huang's exit as chief executive.

Reuters notes that Nvidia changed how it reports revenue by region, using customers' headquarters instead of billing addresses, and that the change took Singapore out of its geographic breakdown; the Financial Times had reported that Singapore sales rose to almost half of global revenue. Nvidia shares were down about 13% on Friday, according to LSEG data cited by Reuters, after being down more than 6% before the report came out. The Motley Fool says the slide erased roughly $491 billion in market value in a single session, taking Nvidia from about $3.52 trillion to about $3.03 trillion, and left the stock about 48% below its 6 October record close of $239.24.

Why it mattersA probe that reaches a company's accounting is more serious than one about the tone of its disclosures, because it can lead to restated figures. Nvidia's reported revenue is a reference point for much of the AI buildout, from chip supply contracts to data center financing and the valuations of AI companies, so any doubt over how it books or locates sales spreads well beyond one stock. Customers and lenders will want to know whether the regional revenue data they have relied on still holds.

The AI sell-off deepens on Wall Street on Friday afternoon, with the chip index down 8.3% and Anthropic down 10.8% at $86.72, as the S&P 500 heads for a seventh straight loss, AP reports

US stocks fell sharply on Friday afternoon as investors kept selling AI-linked shares, according to the Associated Press. In its afternoon update, AP reported the S&P 500 down 2.2% and the Nasdaq composite down 3.6%, with the Dow 329 points, or 0.7%, lower as of 1:44 p.m. Eastern time. The S&P 500 was on course for a seventh straight daily loss, its longest such run since the start of the Covid pandemic in 2020, and AP notes it would officially be in a correction if it closed more than 10% below its 6 October high. These are intraday figures; final closing numbers were not yet available.

Chipmakers led the slide. AP says the PHLX Semiconductor Index sank 8.3%, Broadcom fell 11.5% and AMD dropped 10.9%. Nvidia was down 7%, falling on nine of the last ten days and down more than 25% since the scandal around Jensen Huang broke earlier this month. Anthropic fell 10.8% to $86.72, still above its $83 IPO price but about 26% below the $115.10 peak it reached the day before. Michael Antonelli of Baird told AP that the idea AI can keep spending without limit "is ending as we speak," though he does not think the bull market that began in October 2022 is over.

Why it mattersThe sell-off has moved from a reaction to one company's results or one IPO into a broad repricing of AI hardware and infrastructure. Falling chip and AI valuations change the cost of capital for the whole buildout, from data center developers to the startups that depend on fresh funding, and they can quickly affect pension, treasury and client portfolios heavy in technology. Whether the S&P 500 closes in correction territory will shape sentiment into next week.

Databricks is in early talks to raise several billion dollars at a valuation of about $225 billion, up from $190 billion in August, The Wall Street Journal reports, according to Reuters

Data and AI software company Databricks is in early discussions to raise several billion dollars in a new funding round that would value it at about $225 billion, according to a Reuters report published on the evening of Friday, 20 November, which cites The Wall Street Journal and people familiar with the matter. The talks are still going on and the terms could change. Databricks did not immediately reply to Reuters' request for comment.

Reuters notes that Databricks said in August it had raised $5 billion at a $190 billion valuation. Founded in 2013, the company sells software that businesses use to store, manage and analyze data and to build AI applications, competes with Snowflake, and is widely seen as a candidate for a future stock market listing. Databricks has previously said its Lakebase product has passed a $100 million annual revenue run-rate and its data warehousing business has passed $1.5 billion. The report landed at the end of a week in which AI and chip stocks fell sharply, suggesting private investors are still willing to pay up for AI platforms that sit close to enterprise data.

Why it mattersDatabricks is the layer where many large companies keep the data their AI projects depend on, so its funding and valuation are a gauge of how investors see enterprise AI spending, separate from chipmakers. A higher private valuation in a week of public-market losses shows the gap between private and listed AI prices, and it gives Databricks more money to bundle AI features into contracts that many enterprises are already locked into. Buyers should expect pricing and packaging to follow that ambition.

Cursor, the AI coding company now owned by SpaceX, tells two House committees it wants to explore phasing out Chinese open-weight AI models, as lawmakers call for a US open-weight initiative, Techzine reports

A report by the House Committee on Homeland Security and the House Select Committee on China, which was shared with CNBC ahead of release, says Cursor told the committees it wants to look at whether it can stop using AI models developed in China, according to Techzine. The issue is concrete for Cursor: Techzine says its Composer 2 coding model is largely based on Kimi K2.5 from China's Moonshot AI, and Cursor says Composer 2 scored 61.3 on its CursorBench test, compared with 44.2 for the earlier Composer 1.5. SpaceX announced the purchase of Cursor in June at a reported valuation of $60 billion and completed it in August, and Cursor now helps train SpaceXAI's Grok models.

Airbnb and DoorDash also took part in the months-long investigation. Techzine says Airbnb runs a customer service bot on Alibaba's Qwen and DoorDash uses Kimi for internal tasks. The report finds that companies pick Chinese models for their low cost, their adaptability and performance close to the leading systems, and it says the US has no open-weight alternative that meets the market on price and performance. The committees propose a national US open-weight initiative, building on the ban on DeepSeek in federal civilian systems announced in October. Cursor's position, as described, is an option it wants to study rather than a final decision.

Why it mattersChinese open-weight models already sit inside widely used US software, often without buyers knowing. Congressional pressure on a high-profile vendor like Cursor signals that the base model under an AI product is becoming a national security and compliance question, not just a technical choice. If vendors swap to US models, customers could see changes in cost, performance and contract terms, and companies that serve government clients are likely to face the sharpest questions first.

Thinking Machines Lab releases Inkling 2 Mini, an open-weights model of 26.4 billion parameters that it says runs on a laptop and leads open models under 100 billion parameters on two benchmarks

Thinking Machines Lab on Friday, 20 November, released Inkling 2 Mini with open weights, and made it available on its Tinker platform for inference and customization. The company says the model is small enough to run on a laptop: it has 26.4 billion parameters in total, of which 3.4 billion are active for each token, and a memory footprint of about 20GB. It uses a "looped" design that runs its middle layers three times for every token. Its researchers say they ran it offline on a MacBook Pro during a flight and used it to build a playable flight simulator in a few minutes.

Thinking Machines says Inkling 2 Mini "leads every open-weights model with less than 100B total parameters" on DeepSWE v1.1, at 48.2%, and on Vals Index v2.2, at 46.5%. It also cites early testing by researchers at Stanford, in which the model scored 0.703 on an NVIDIA DGX Spark desktop AI computer against 0.430 for Qwen3.6-35B-A3B. The company is open about limits: it scores 50.0% on MultiChallenge and 33.4% on ComplexConstraints v1.1, behind Alibaba's Qwen3.8-27B. All of these results are published by the company itself.

Why it mattersA capable US-built open-weights model that fits on a laptop changes the options for companies that cannot send data to a cloud model, from regulated banks and drugmakers to field teams with no connection. It also lands on the same day a House report said Cursor wants to explore moving away from Chinese open models, such as the Qwen family that Inkling 2 Mini is measured against. If its claims hold up, it gives buyers a domestic alternative for on-device and on-premise AI.

Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge claims a proof of the Riemann hypothesis backed by 17 million lines of Lean code, which experts are now racing to check, Quanta Magazine reports

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician who works at Anthropic, posted a four-page paper on his personal website on the morning of Friday, 20 November, claiming a proof of the Riemann hypothesis, one of the most famous open problems in mathematics, according to Quanta Magazine. He says the work actually proves a stronger statement, the generalized Riemann hypothesis. The evidence he offers is not a conventional paper but about 17 million lines of code in Lean, a language in which a computer checks each step of a proof. Quanta reports that his efforts to have Claude turn the result into a version people can read ran to more than 250,000 pages, so he has not released one.

Alpöge says he reached the result on Monday, 16 November, and waited several days before sharing it; on the delay, he says, "The precise timing is horrible." Specialists in Lean are now working to confirm independently that the code proves what he says it does, and Quanta reports they expect an answer within hours. When Quanta published, Anthropic had made no official announcement. Quanta also notes that Alpöge claims, with Tristan Buckmaster of New York University, to have resolved a version of the Navier-Stokes problem. Neither result had been independently verified at the time of writing.

Why it mattersThe claim is unverified, and that caveat comes first. But the way it is being tested matters on its own: instead of years of peer review, the question is whether software accepts a machine-checkable proof. If it holds, it would show that AI systems can help produce frontier research whose correctness is confirmed by a computer rather than by trust in the model, which is exactly the assurance R&D-heavy and regulated industries want before relying on AI-generated work.

South Korea's President Lee tours the SK Group and AWS data center being built in Ulsan and tells the country that speed matters in the AI race, Reuters reports

On Saturday, 21 November, South Korean President Lee Jae Myung visited a data center under construction in the southeastern city of Ulsan, which SK Group, the country's second-largest conglomerate, is developing with Amazon Web Services, according to Reuters. In a public video of the tour, Lee said, "The whole world is competing right now, and speed is vital." He said he hoped the project would attract more investment from global AI companies, and noted that AI investment has so far clustered around Seoul, so the Ulsan site shows growth outside the capital is possible.

Reuters reports that South Korea's science ministry put the project's estimated cost at 7 trillion won ($5.2 billion) until 2029, a figure it gave last year, and that operations are due to start in the second half of 2027. SK Group Chairman Chey Tae-won said in September that the capacity being built is an initial 103 megawatts by 2029, with plans to add nearly 900 megawatts later. After hearing that about 900 people work on the site, Lee said advanced facilities are short of construction workers and that ministries should look at how to train people to build data centers and AI infrastructure.

Why it mattersGovernments are now treating data center construction as a national race, and the bottleneck Lee pointed to is not only chips or power but skilled construction labor. For companies planning AI capacity in Asia, the Ulsan project is a sign that South Korea wants to compete as a host market outside its capital, and that the timeline for new capacity depends as much on workforce and permitting as on money.
