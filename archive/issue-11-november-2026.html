Anthropic adds Claude Sheets, Claude Meetings, Claude Whiteboards and Claude Data, and lets partner apps run inside Claude, Engadget reports

Anthropic used an event in San Francisco on 10 November 2026 to introduce a set of workplace tools built into Claude, according to Engadget. Claude Sheets lets Claude build and edit spreadsheets with several tabs, formulas and formatting. Coworkers with edit rights can work on the same file, Claude's changes are flagged so others can follow them, and files can be exported to Excel. Claude Meetings is an agent that joins video calls as a visible participant, takes notes, and lets people ask Claude about plans and action items once the call is over. Anthropic says the agent "won't join a call with outside guests unless an admin allows it."

Claude Whiteboards sorts a team's ideas into themes and drafts action items for each person, and Claude Data, the new name for Claude Dashboards, links to a company's CRM so Claude can answer questions about that data. Anthropic is also letting users open full partner applications inside Claude, where Claude can act on what is shown in the window, starting with a small group of partners: Adobe, Atlassian and HubSpot. Availability varies by tool. Claude Sheets and Claude Whiteboards are in beta, Claude Data is rolling out to all paid users, and Claude Meetings starts in Google Meet for Team and Enterprise customers only.

Why it mattersAnthropic is moving beyond supplying a model that sits inside other companies' office software and is now offering its own spreadsheet, meeting and whiteboard tools, which puts it in closer competition with the established productivity suites. For enterprises, a meeting agent raises questions about consent, recording and how long notes are kept, while AI edits to shared spreadsheets need a clear record of who changed what. Flagging Claude's edits and keeping the agent out of calls with outside guests by default address part of that, but not all of it.

AI training data firm Mercor raises $750 million at a $20 billion valuation, double its October 2025 valuation, The Next Web reports

Mercor, a San Francisco startup that pays experts to train and test AI models, has raised a $750 million Series D round at a $20 billion valuation, The Next Web reported on 10 November 2026, citing the company's statement. The valuation has doubled from $10 billion, set when Mercor raised $350 million in October 2025. Named investors include Benchmark, Felicis, Fundomo, General Catalyst, Nvidia, Salesforce, Sapphire Ventures and Zoom, together with funds managed by affiliates of Apollo, G Squared, Glade Brook Capital, StepStone Group and XTX Ventures. The round included an employee tender offer, but Mercor did not say how much of the $750 million went to it.

Mercor runs a network of more than five million experts, and its main customers so far have been frontier AI labs; the new money is meant to widen sales to other businesses. In a blog post, co-founder Brendan Foody wrote that Mercor spends three times as much on inference as on employee salaries, and argued that if the same ratio held across the economy it would point to near double-digit GDP growth over the next five years. That is his own projection. The Next Web notes that neither the funding statement nor Foody's post mentions an April breach at Mercor that led Meta to freeze its AI data work with the company.

Why it mattersExpert-made training and evaluation data is still a scarce input for AI models, and backers such as Nvidia, Salesforce and Zoom are paying a higher price for it than a year ago. Mercor's push beyond the AI labs means more enterprises will be offered expert-graded evaluation services for their own agents and models. The breach that cost Mercor its Meta work is a reminder that these suppliers handle sensitive customer data, so security checks matter as much as price.

Chip stocks have lost about $5 trillion in market value since 6 October as Nvidia hits its most oversold level since 2019, Yahoo Finance reports

A hotter-than-expected October consumer price index hit US stocks on Tuesday, and AI chipmakers took the brunt of it, Yahoo Finance reported at midday on 10 November 2026. By then the S&P 500 was down nearly 1%, which Yahoo Finance calculates as about $500 billion in lost market value, and the PHLX Semiconductor Index was off more than 1%. Nvidia was down about 6% and on course for its lowest close since 30 March, and its relative strength index had dropped to 23 out of 100, the most oversold reading since June 2019. Micron, SanDisk and Arm were each down roughly 3% to 5%, while AMD and Broadcom held up.

Yahoo Finance puts the total loss for chip stocks since 6 October at about $5 trillion, roughly a quarter of their value, against about $3 trillion for the whole S&P 500 over the same span. The equal-weighted S&P 500, by contrast, is up about 4% since 6 October, a sign that the selling is concentrated in AI names. The 10-year Treasury yield moved back above 5%. Analysts quoted were split: Bernstein's Stacy Rasgon said the Nvidia drop could be a strong buying opportunity if CEO Jensen Huang stays, while veteran trader Michael Martin said: "I don't think these are buying opportunities." Nvidia reports quarterly results after the bell on 17 November, and Anthropic's IPO could follow as soon as 19 November.

Why it mattersA quarter of the chip sector's value has disappeared in about five weeks, while the average stock has gained, so this is a repricing of AI hardware rather than a broad market crash. Higher bond yields make it more expensive to fund the debt-heavy data center build-out, and the next two weeks bring two tests of AI demand in Nvidia's results and Anthropic's listing. Companies that rely on chip suppliers' cash flows, or that plan to raise money on the back of AI, should expect tougher questions from investors and lenders.

CoreWeave reports $3,661 million in third-quarter revenue and a backlog of about $132.5 billion, and raises its 2026 revenue outlook to $13.0 billion to $13.3 billion

AI cloud provider CoreWeave published its results for the quarter to 30 September 2026 on 10 November. Revenue reached $3,661 million, compared with $1,365 million a year earlier, an increase of about 168% (our calculation). Operating income was $207 million and adjusted EBITDA was $2,256 million, a 62% margin. The company still lost money: its net loss widened to $709 million from $110 million, as net interest expense rose to $846 million from $310 million. CEO Michael Intrator called it "a breakout quarter for CoreWeave."

CoreWeave says its revenue backlog stood at approximately $132.5 billion at the end of September. It raised its full-year 2026 revenue guidance to $13.0 billion to $13.3 billion and now expects an annualized revenue run-rate of $20 billion to $21 billion as it exits 2026. Spending stays heavy: it bought $9,441 million of property and equipment in the quarter and guides to $37 billion to $39 billion of capital spending for the year, and it expects fourth-quarter interest expense of $1.05 billion to $1.15 billion. It reports over 1.7 GW of active power and over 4.6 GW under contract. New customers named include Canva, Substack and Wayve, and it expanded work with OpenAI, Jane Street, Cohere and Perplexity.

Why it mattersCoreWeave rents out AI computing capacity to AI labs and enterprises, so its numbers are one of the clearest public reads on whether AI demand is holding up while chip stocks fall. A raised outlook and a backlog above $130 billion point to continued demand, but the widening loss and rising interest bill show how much of the build-out is funded with debt. For buyers of AI capacity, that combination means plenty of supply on offer from providers whose finances are worth watching closely.

US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announces a US-China dialogue on superintelligence and a direct channel for alerting each other to serious AI incidents, Reuters reports

Speaking in New York on 10 November 2026 to the National Committee on US-China Relations, US Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent announced a new two-way dialogue between Washington and Beijing on what the Trump administration calls superintelligence, Reuters reported. In his prepared remarks, Bessent said the talks are meant to guard against risks the two countries share and to "prevent a misunderstanding between our two systems." According to Reuters, the two sides expect to swap best practices on evaluating and testing advanced AI models before they are released, and on keeping human judgment in charge of the technology.

The two governments have also agreed to set up a direct channel to warn each other about serious incidents, building on an AI alert system that Presidents Trump and Xi Jinping agreed at their White House meeting in September, Reuters reports. Bessent was clear that cooperation has limits: he told the audience the two countries should have no illusions about their differences on AI and that their interests will not always line up. He travels to China next week for Trump's third meeting with Xi this year, to be held in Shenzhen alongside the APEC leaders' summit on 18 and 19 November. Bessent also described a planned "Board of Trade" to set mutual tariff cuts on $30 billion of non-sensitive goods in each direction. Reuters noted that his remarks did not refer to the board investigation into Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang.

Why it mattersA standing US-China channel on frontier AI risk would give the two largest AI powers a regular forum to compare how models are tested before release, and any shared practices could shape what regulators and customers expect from model providers. Bessent's caveats show this sits alongside, not in place of, competition over chips and models, so companies should not assume export controls or tech restrictions will ease. The Shenzhen meeting on 18 and 19 November is the next point at which details may emerge.

A military-linked Chinese university on the US Entity List bought two Nvidia H200 chips through a local reseller in October, a Reuters review of procurement records finds

Northwestern Polytechnical University in Xi'an, one of the "Seven Sons of National Defense" institutions that support China's armed forces, paid 874,700 yuan (about US$123,000, according to Reuters) for two Nvidia H200 chips last month, Reuters reported in an exclusive on 11 November 2026. A procurement record dated 21 October shows the university's management school bought them from a reseller, Shaanxi Bosuan Network Technology, rather than from Nvidia. The university has been on the US Commerce Department's Entity List since 2001, and restrictions tightened in 2019 mean licenses for controlled US technology are denied in most cases. Reuters notes that the Trump administration's decision in January to allow some H200 sales to China case by case does not lift Entity List restrictions.

The records also show the university's cybersecurity school signed two seven-month contracts in January, worth 441,700 yuan and 441,000 yuan, for servers with eight H200 chips each, to fine-tune AI models and run reinforcement-learning training. It also rented servers with A100 and A800 chips, which cannot be exported to any Chinese entity. Reuters says it reviewed 26 Chinese procurement and corporate records from this year showing universities and research bodies buying restricted Nvidia chips or renting Nvidia-powered computing through domestic suppliers. The university, Nvidia and the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security did not respond to Reuters, which could not reach the reseller. The report lands as three Republican committee chairmen have requested a briefing with Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang on 18 November.

Why it mattersThe records suggest that advanced US AI chips can still reach blacklisted buyers through resellers and rented computing, which weakens the case that case-by-case licensing keeps the most capable hardware away from military-linked users. With Nvidia already under scrutiny in Washington, findings like these raise the chance of tighter rules on resale, cloud rental and end-use checks, which would affect chipmakers, server vendors, distributors and cloud providers selling into Asia.

Oura agrees to buy AI-native primary care startup Counsel Health for $625 million and makes its CEO chief clinical AI officer, TechCrunch reports

Smart ring maker Oura said on 10 November 2026 that it is acquiring Counsel Health, an AI-native primary care company, for $625 million, TechCrunch reported. Counsel combines a chat-based medical AI that gives users personalized advice with access to licensed physicians who step in when needed, and its service has been built into the Oura app since June. The two companies plan to extend their partnership into preventive, acute, specialty and chronic care. Counsel CEO Muthu Alagappan will become Oura's chief clinical AI officer, and Counsel's 70 employees will also move to Oura, according to Bloomberg as cited by TechCrunch. Counsel will keep serving its existing and new enterprise partners.

Counsel raised a $25 million Series A led by Andreessen Horowitz and GV about a year ago, and PitchBook estimates its total funding at $36 million. Alagappan said the company keeps "physicians in the loop for clinical decisions." TechCrunch says the deal is expected to close before the end of 2027 and did not report whether it is paid in cash or stock. It comes a few weeks after Oura indefinitely postponed an IPO of up to $2.2 billion. Oura's revenue grew 153% year over year to $533 million in the quarter ended 30 September, and its fiscal 2026 revenue was about $1.75 billion, up 92%, according to the report.

Why it mattersA consumer device company is paying a large price for an AI care service, which shows that combining wearable data with AI triage and human doctors is becoming a business in its own right. For health systems and insurers, it means a new kind of competitor for routine primary care that sits on a customer's finger and phone. The deal also shows that AI health startups with a physician-in-the-loop model can command values far above the capital they raised.

New York opens registration under its RAISE Act, giving frontier AI developers with over $500 million in revenue until 1 January 2027 to sign up, TechCrunch reports

New York opened a registration portal on 10 November 2026 for the largest frontier AI developers covered by its RAISE Act, TechCrunch reported. The law applies to companies with more than $500 million in annual revenue, and TechCrunch names OpenAI and Anthropic among them. These developers must register by 1 January 2027, when the law takes effect. Once covered, they have to post their safety frameworks online, issue public transparency reports on their models, and tell the state about critical safety incidents within 72 hours.

Oversight sits with a new state office called the Office of Digital Innovation, Governance, Integrity, and Trust (DIGIT). Fines can reach $1 million for a violation and $3 million for later violations, and the state can take enforcement action against companies that do not register, although a spokesperson would not give details. New York also launched the Center for AI Responsibility and Research at SUNY Binghamton, backed by $55 million in donations and capital, which will test advanced AI systems independently and help DIGIT review safety frameworks. Governor Kathy Hochul called the approach "a national model for smart, sensible AI regulation." TechCrunch notes that California's SB 53 already requires incident reports within 15 days and that Illinois's SB 315 starts in 2027 with independent compliance audits.

Why it mattersWith California in force and New York and Illinois starting in 2027, the largest model providers now face a patchwork of state safety rules with different deadlines – New York's 72-hour incident window is far shorter than California's 15 days. Companies that buy frontier models will gain new public documents to inform vendor due diligence, and should expect contract terms and incident notices from suppliers to change as these rules bite.
